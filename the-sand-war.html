The Sand War

He sat on a stack of cut timber at the edge of the yard because his leg had had enough, and watched them get ready to die.

Some of them probably believed him. The woman at the fire had given the chart four full seconds before she went back to bundling reeds. A coast that might exist somewhere west couldn’t compete with three hundred soldiers walking down the north road.

Here, the next hour ate everything. Water mattered. Another foot of wall mattered. Whether the boy on the east corner had ever held a spear mattered. Dren had lived the same way nine years ago, worrying about the next allotment and whether Sotha Bal would eat through winter. If an old man had come talking about an ocean, Dren would’ve nodded and gone back to work.

Knowing that didn’t make him less angry. It only spoiled the pleasure of it.

They had a plan for dying here. Every man on the wall could say where he’d stand and who’d be on his left. Nobody had a plan for leaving. Leaving was the mad idea.

He went to Sheo one more time.

He waited for a gap. He didn’t mention the coast or touch the strap on his bag.

“One thing,” he said. “Then I’m out of your way.”

Sheo didn’t have time to say no.

“Your children. Trovgar.”

Everything in Sheo’s face moved about an inch.

“I’ll go and get them.”

“You’ll what?”

“I can get a horse. Trovgar’s about ninety kilometers. I’ll swing wide of the road, come up on the market from the south, find them, and bring them back.”

Sheo looked at him for a moment. “I sent a rider four days ago.”

“And?”

“Nothing. Four days.” He rubbed his face hard with the flat of his hand. “Riders get held up. It’s ninety kilometers and there are men on the roads.”

“Then let me go.”

“Go. Nobody’s stopping you.”

“When I bring them back, you give me an hour.”

Sheo went still.

“One hour, sitting down, with the chart open. I bring your children home and you listen to the way out. If you don’t like it, I’ll get on that wall and never say another word.”

He’d promised himself he wouldn’t bargain like this. He’d done it anyway.

Sheo’s voice came out low and flat and worse than shouting.

“You’re bargaining with me. Over my children.”

“That’s not what I’m doing.”

“Stop.” Sheo put both hands up. “Dren. We’re being invaded. There are three hundred men on the north road. My wall isn’t finished. My children are ninety kilometers away, and I can’t go because this town falls tonight if I do. Every hour you spend talking to me about paradise is an hour I’m not doing the one thing I can do.”

He was shaking a little. Not with rage.

“Enough. Please. Enough nonsense.”

He went back to the wall. It was there and it needed him.

Dren stood in the yard for a while after that.

He thought about getting up on that wall.

He worked it honestly, the way he’d learned to work things out on the beach instead of jumping at them. Nobody here had seen a crossbow like his. He had forty bolts and could put down some men from the east corner.

Twelve, if it went well. Twenty if it went perfectly and nobody got a ladder up near him.

Three hundred.

Enough people would die here that one fifty-something man with a bad leg changed nothing except where his body ended up.

If he was going to spend himself, he could spend himself on the two who weren’t here.

If he couldn’t evacuate Sotha Bal, he’d try Trovgar. Traders moved for a living. They understood roads, cargo, and a better price somewhere else. They might hear him.

It was thin, but it was more than he’d had an hour ago.

He tried three more people on his way through town because he couldn’t help himself.

A man carrying staves heard nine words and kept walking. One of two women filling sandbags kindly told him to get inside before dark. A man his own age heard about the coast, the water, and the road around the green, nodded through all of it, then asked if Dren had anything to drink.

That was the closest he got.

The stalls were down at the south end, past the well.

Halfway there he caught himself looking for Betty. She’d been old when he left. Nine years settled the question without his help.

There were four horses in the pens. Three were young and barely broken. The fourth was a dark bay gelding with an old saddle rub, sound legs, and the dull patience of an animal trained for relay work. He watched the wall but didn’t fight the rail. Somebody had watered him. Nobody had brushed him in days.

There was nobody at the stalls. The stableman was on the wall with a basket of dirt like everybody else.

Dren waited. He wanted to put the sale to the man and pay in front of witnesses, but nobody was coming.

He checked the bay’s teeth and hooves, walked him around the pen, then backed and turned him with the reins. The gelding knew distance work. Dren saddled him slowly, his stick against the post, talking to him the way he’d talked to an empty cabin for eight years.

Then he opened the pouch.

He’d brought twenty-four gold coins and a separate pouch of silver from the ship. He counted four gold into his palm, each stamped with the face of a king nobody on this continent had heard of. He’d carried them for a little over a month across dead country for no reason he could’ve defended.

He set them in a row on the top rail where a man would find them.

They were worth more than the horse, the stalls, and probably the whole south end of town. In the wrecked cabin he’d let a fistful run through his fingers and thought it was the most money he’d ever seen and worth exactly nothing.

It was the first time any of it had ever bought anything.

He rode out through the south gate. The guards didn’t ask where he was going. A man riding into nothing wasn’t their problem today.

He went east first.

He went wide, well off the road, into the flat where the ground turned pale and hard and the grass gave up. Three hundred men were somewhere north of him. He wanted plenty of empty between them.

It was the country he had been born into and he had forgotten how much of it there was.

Nine years on a coast where food grew by itself had taken the wastes out of him. The air pulled water off his lips. The horizon shook, and the sun came off the pale ground and got under his brim from below.

It was worse than he remembered. Scrub had once held the dust along this stretch. A herders’ track had once cut toward a pond. Both were gone.

The demon had kept working the whole time he was away.

He rested the horse every hour, walked him until his breathing settled, and offered what water he could. There was one horse and no spare. Dren’s leg enforced the same arrangement on him.

He swung north in the late afternoon, picked up the old east line, and rode into the dark. There was a moon and flat ground, and he couldn’t make himself stop.

He gave it up somewhere in the middle of the night. Hobbled the horse, lay down in his coat on ground that was still giving back the day’s heat, and slept about three hours with the crossbow next to his hand.

He was moving again before it was properly light.

He saw the smoke about an hour after that.

It stood ahead and to his left, straight in the still morning before it flattened and drifted, higher than any smoke he’d seen.

He stopped the horse.

He knew cookfires, kilns, burnt farms, and the black weight of a granary going up. This wasn’t any of them. There was too much under it besides wood.

He got the glass out with his hands not quite steady and put it on the smoke and followed it down.

Twenty kilometers, near enough. Heat was already lifting off the flat, so the image crawled and went soft at the edges. It was enough.

The town lay under it, a long dark smear with almost none of the square edges a town should have. Whole runs of it were low to the ground.

Orange in three or four places, still working, still eating something.

He held the glass as long as he could, put it away, and asked the rested gelding for a canter.

He came up on it from the south through the middle of the morning.

The smell reached him a kilometer out. He’d learned it from his own leg in the swamp and again in the ship’s hold. Here it lay over the whole town with wood smoke through it.

He tied a strip of cloth over his mouth and it did nothing.

They’d built a wall recently: packed earth between timbers, a ditch, a gate frame with a tower. It wasn’t much, but somebody had put months into it.

A thirty-foot stretch of wall had come inward, not fallen. Timbers snapped and pointed back, dirt spread behind them in a fan, everything from the tower down in the ditch. Whatever broke through hadn’t stopped.

Birds worked the ditch. They rose when he came near and settled again behind him.

He got down off the horse.

Past the wall were streets, or had been. Burned frames stood empty. Roofs lay in the rooms below. Mud-brick walls a foot high still marked houses, rooms, and hearths. Everything else was black stubs.

The market was ash to the ankles for a hundred paces.

People were everywhere.

On the ride in he’d reminded himself that he had seen dead people. He’d buried eight strangers with his own hands and thought about them for years. This was something else.

Some were burned small and dark, no longer shaped like people. Those were almost easier. The rest lay where they’d been caught and had been worked on afterward. Arms gone. A man in a doorway with the front of his head driven away. Two at the mouth of a lane, chopped long after either could’ve moved. Pieces in the street that had come off somebody.

A battle was two lines until one ran. This had continued afterward, at leisure.

Forty paces in, Dren dropped to one knee in the ash and brought up everything he’d eaten. He stayed over it with his eyes streaming while the birds carried on behind him.

This was days old. Sheo’s rider had left four days ago, already too late. The town had fallen before Dren entered Sotha Bal, perhaps before he came down off the mountains.

Two names got him back on his feet. Caius. Fimmion.

He didn’t know their faces now. Fimmion had been about sixteen when he left, old enough to laugh at him on purpose, and Caius had been growing into himself. Nine years could remake both of them. He was looking for Sheo’s grown children in a town full of somebody’s children.

He started looking anyway.

He started along the market row, where Sheo said they’d been defending a stall. He looked at every body, one at a time, crouching when he had to and turning nothing that didn’t need turning. Before he moved on, he said something under his breath.

The young ones were worst. There were many.

He worked the row, the lane behind it, then the ground in front of the breach where most of them lay. The sun climbed above the smoke and began cooking the town. Dren kept searching for two faces he hadn’t seen in nine years.

Somewhere in the middle he started counting without meaning to. Eight years alone had made counting a habit: fish in a net, marks on a doorframe, men in a column.

He came out the far end of the market row with a number that couldn’t be right.

He counted the lanes, back yards, ground inside the wall, and ditch outside. It took most of the day and was the worst work he’d ever done.

Trovgar had held about two thousand people, more on a trading day. Dren found fewer than five hundred bodies. More than fifteen hundred lives remained to account for.

The ground carried part of the answer. Thin trails left the east and south sides from before the siege ring closed: families, field hands, traders with time to run. At the breach, three broad columns split through the wreckage. One went west between horse tracks. Another bent north. A third joined the army road, where boot marks swallowed it.

The westward track was forty feet wide, packed flat by feet going out and horse patrols on both sides. Prisoners.

Dren stood in the breach with one hand on a broken timber. Grief had somewhere to go now.

Vey Trading Camp was an hour north and he smelled it before he saw it, cook smoke and latrines and horses, all of it clean and ordinary and appalling.

It was a proper camp: ditched palisade, watched gate, tents in rows, a forge, and horses in better condition than anything at Sotha Bal. Men played a game with stones in the dirt.

Two men met him well outside with spears low while a third held back with a bow. These weren’t farm boys.

“Off the horse.”

He dismounted slowly and let the stick make it look worse. The spears lowered with him.

“Which side.”

“Drenovar,” Dren said.

The spears rose a foot. Drenovar fed Veyloth, so a Drenovar man stood on the correct side of a line Dren hadn’t known existed until this week.

“Long way from home.”

“Buying,” Dren said.

They walked him in.

He hadn’t expected them to be pleasant. They’d been out here for months, and a stranger was better company than stones in the dirt. Within ten minutes Dren sat in the shade against a wagon wheel, his horse watered and fed without asking, while a man of about thirty with a burn up one arm told him how it had gone.

They were proud of it.

The wall had held eleven days. They respected it for that. Then they’d brought up a ram under a roof of green hides and gone through the weak seam before dawn. The burned man drew it in the dust: the shallow ditch, the archers, the gate tower coming down with people on it.

He was a good storyteller. He kept checking that Dren was following.

“And after,” Dren said.

“After.” The man shrugged. “After is after.”

Somebody laughed. Dren joined in, easily and at the right volume, and hated how little effort it took.

They talked about another army column moving south toward Sotha Bal. Dren let it pass over his face, then asked whether the roads were safe for a man carrying money. A buyer would ask that.

Then he put it in.

“I heard you took a lot of prisoners.”

“A lot.”

“Selling any of them?”

The men by the wagon broke up. It was the funniest thing they’d heard all week. The burned man slapped his knee, called to the next fire, and made Dren drink from a skin before he answered.

It was whiskey, or their idea of it: thin, hot, and tasting of whatever had been in the barrel first. After eight years of a dead lord’s spirit, Dren drank it and said it was good. They liked him for that.

“Not us. We don’t sell. The market-quarter lot went west four days ago. Two hundred, under escort.”

“Only two hundred?”

The man shrugged. “Plenty ran before we shut the roads. Other prisoners went north to the field camps. They pressed the sound men into levy companies and put others on baggage trains. Veyloth got the two hundred we tagged for sale.”

“West where.”

“Veyloth. Where else? There’ll be an auction out of that big hall up the hill. Census and Excise. You want the counter inside; they book you in, and you can’t just walk up. Go soon.”

“Why soon.”

“Two hundred head at once will put the price on the floor.” He was delighted to give good advice. “Nobody’s got barns for that many. There’ll be deals.”

Dren thanked him and took another pull when the skin came around.

He stayed another quarter hour so his interest wouldn’t look too sharp. The men who had done that to Trovgar talked around him about the price of grain.

Then he got on his horse and went west.

Veyloth was about eighty kilometers west. Dren took the rest of that day and most of the next, walking the gelding as often as he rode. Twenty gold coins and the separate silver pouch knocked against him, a fortune no roadside seller could change for a bag of grain.

Veyloth came up out of the flat the way it always had, the wall first, then the smell.

He entered through the south slums because that was the way he knew.

They were empty.

He followed the lane he’d ridden Betty down nine years ago. Back then, the crowd stopped her at a walk. Hands reached for Dren’s legs, a barefoot man shouted about a wheel, and washing hung low enough to slap him. He remembered holding his breath.

There was nobody in it.

The garbage remained in gray drifts against the walls, light now and crusted by a few rains. Doors stood open. A stool waited in one of them. Cloth hung above the alley in strips bleached of color.

The smell of the slums remained in the stone, faint but recognizable: ten thousand people who weren’t there.

He rode slowly. Hoofbeats came back off the walls, the only sound in the quarter.

He worked out most of it as he went. The uprising had marched from these lanes to Drenovar and killed a market full of people, including a young man at a farm stall. Veyloth had put it down. Afterward, conscription fed the survivors into the war until the quarter emptied.

The lanes and turns hadn’t changed. Knowing the way felt like knowing the way around a body.

The city filled again farther in, and everybody was doing war. Carts carried spear staves. Men drilled in a yard. A woman nailed up a notice while a queue formed behind her. The city had no attention to spare for one old man on a horse.

In the official district, the streets opened and the stone got cleaner.

The fountain was still dry. Sand lay smooth up one side of the bowl. At least the old yellow scum was gone.

Dren paid a stableboy from the silver pouch to cool the gelding, rub him down, feed him barley, and leave the saddle off until morning. Then he crossed the square toward the hall with its columns and high doors.

The ceiling was the same. The brown tide mark had grown and acquired a second stain. Light came through the badly fitted shutters in dusty stripes. Dren stood in the doorway with one hand on the frame.

A guard put a board in front of him.

“Name and source.”

“Drenovar Kvatch. Drenovar.”

The guard wrote it down without looking up and waved him at the queue.

The queue took an hour. Dren didn’t mind. Four counters moved requisitions, headcounts, tariffs, and the conscription quotas posted by district.

Then the queue moved and he came up to a counter and looked at the man behind it and stopped.

He saw the colors before the face.

Turquoise. A heavy robe dyed through, the color of the bay when morning sun came over the trees. A worked-gold belt lay over it. On the hand moving papers, a cut green stone threw the color of shallow jungle water at the emerald hour.

This man was wearing the two colors Dren had once named aloud with nobody there to hear.

He had gone up in the world. Nine years ago it had been a purple coat.

“Business.”

“The lot coming in from Trovgar,” Dren said.

The clerk’s pen stopped. He looked up and sat back.

“I know you.”

Dren said nothing.

“I do. I know you.” His smile was real and delighted. He called down to the woman at the next counter. “This is the jungle man. Nine years ago, stood right there and wanted to fetch supplies out of the trees.” He turned back, warm as anything. “I’ve told that story a hundred times. You’re a story in this building.”

“Is that right.”

“Everyone said you’d die.” He was still smiling. “You didn’t.”

“No.”

“Well.” The clerk inspected the coat, boots, beard, and stick. Dren watched the arithmetic begin. “You’ve done all right for yourself, and you’ve come back to a different city.”

The smile came off like a hand wiping a table.

“You came in on Sotha Bal’s business.”

“That was nine years ago.”

“GUARDS.” He was on his feet, voice in the stained ceiling. Every counter stopped. “Guards. This man is from Sotha Bal.”

Two came fast, one from each end. They had his arms before he finished turning, and his stick cracked against the stone.

Dren didn’t struggle. A swamp had taught him what panic did for you.

“Ahem.” He spoke flatly, at a volume for the room. “I’m from Drenovar.”

“He’s lying. He’s a Sotha Bal man. I took his declaration myself.”

“You took it before there was a war or sides in one. I gave you the town I was buying for. Check your book at the door. An hour ago I gave my name and source, same as now.”

“Anybody can say a word at a door.”

“Then check the record.”

The guards stopped moving him. He wasn’t fighting, and he spoke like a man who expected an answer. In that building, nothing carried like a records citation.

“My name is Drenovar Kvatch. I’m the father and legal heir of James Kvatch of Drenovar. His property passed into my name when his death was entered. Seventeen Hlograth Avenue, outer-district property roll. Go look, then decide what I am.”

The hall went silent.

The clerk hated him for four seconds, then went to the shelves. Forty people had heard a file cited in a records office. He had to fetch it.

It took long enough for Dren to count the stripes of light through the shutters.

Sheo never filed it.

James’s shack had passed automatically to Dren as the recorded heir. In that front room, Dren had taken a borrowed pen and scribbled the sale to Sheo. He couldn’t read the words on the page; Sheo helped him copy the names, and Dren finished with the ownership mark he used whenever paper required one. Sheo folded the transfer, carried it forty kilometers home, and never spent two days reaching Veyloth to file it and pay a fee on an empty shack.

Between the two men, the shack was Sheo’s from that day. For nine years the transfer sat in a drawer while Veyloth’s property roll kept Dren’s name.

The clerk came back with a folder.

He didn’t apologize. He set the folder square on the counter and said, “Release him,” without looking up. The hands left Dren’s arms.

A guard picked up his stick and gave it back to him. Dren thanked him.

“You are,” the clerk said, in a voice full of paper, “a registered citizen of Drenovar and the recorded owner of property there.”

“That’s what I said.”

“Of a property at seventeen Hlograth Avenue.”

“Yes.”

“On which,” the clerk said, almost pleased now, “there is an outstanding balance.”

He turned the folder so Dren could see the column, a pointless courtesy. Dren couldn’t read it.

“Twenty-seven thousand, five hundred and forty-two.”

“For a shack.”

“For a shack, over nine years, unoccupied and undeclared.” He ran the pen down the page. “Assessment. Late penalty. Compounding. Annual absentee-property fee. Annual unoccupied-property fee. Wartime levy, backdated.” He looked up. “How would you like to settle it?”

Dren had once stood at this counter without enough money to warrant another minute of the man’s day.

He put his hand in the pouch and set three coins down on the wood.

They made a particular sound. Everyone at that counter heard it.

The clerk stared before picking one up. His thumb crossed the stamped face, and something happened to his mouth.

“What is this.”

“Gold.”

“I can see it’s gold.” He held it close. “Whose head is this?”

“I’ve no idea.” The truth came easily.

They sent for a proper scale. A second man weighed all three coins, then weighed them against each other while he and the clerk talked too low for Dren to join.

The coins matched exactly.

“These are struck,” the second man told the clerk. “Look at the edge and the ground behind the head. No hammer and punch did that. A machine did.”

“Value them.”

The valuation took more weighing and low talk. At last the clerk wrote a figure, turned it around, then had to read it aloud.

A little over ten thousand each.

Dren did the arithmetic in his head. Three coins would clear the debt.

They took all three gold coins. Arrears, fees, assay, and one final filing charge consumed the value neatly. The separate silver pouch from the ship stayed at Dren’s belt.

He put the receipt inside his coat.

“Now,” he said. “The Trovgar lot.”

The clerk had been about to call the next man. He put his hand down.

“The Trovgar intake is being processed.”

“I want to buy.”

“Then you will need an appointment.”

“Fine.”

“Viewings are by appointment, in rotation.” He opened a ribboned ledger and ran one finger down it with care. “Nothing this week or next. The fourth of the following month, afternoon. By then the sound ones will be gone.”

“That’s six weeks.”

“That’s the list.”

“Is there any way to move up the list?”

The clerk looked at him.

Dren put one of his seventeen remaining gold coins on the counter.

The ledger closed at once. Nobody lowered a voice or glanced aside. As far as that hall was concerned, this was what the system was for.

“There’s a cancellation this afternoon.”

“How fortunate.”

“Two hours. Take this to the north yard gate and give it to the sergeant.” He wrote, blotted, stamped, folded, and held out the paper without releasing it.

“You want to know the funny part,” he said.

“Go on.”

“Nine years ago you asked me for supplies for a thousand people and didn’t have the price of a cart.” He released the paper. “Now look.”

“Now look,” Dren agreed. He put it in his coat and walked past the queue into the light.

He stood by the dry fountain a while. The gelding remained at the stable, eating and resting.

Nine years ago he’d told that man the truth about ground and water worth finding and been laughed out of the building. Today he’d put metal on the counter and been given the afternoon.

The yard was on the north side, outside the district wall, and he heard it before he got to it.

It was a long open lot with timber-and-iron pens on both sides. Patches of old sailcloth gave a little shade. A trough stood at each end, flies hazed the air, and the place smelled of people who’d walked eighty kilometers under guard and been packed into the yard the previous evening.

Two hundred, near enough, crowded twenty and thirty to a pen. There wasn’t room for everyone to stand.

They were all young. He understood why and stopped a moment. Trovgar had old people, but flight, the other columns, and the army’s levies had sorted them elsewhere.

The sergeant read his paper and showed him down the middle like a yard of horses. Two soldiers followed because that was procedure.

“What are you after?”

“Just looking.”

“Look at that one.” The sergeant knocked the rail. A man inside flinched without rising. “Look at the back on him. Put him on a plow and he’ll pay for himself in a season.”

Dren said something noncommittal.

“Or that.” One soldier grinned toward a woman against the far rail, arms around her knees. “That one’s wasted on a farm. You wouldn’t want her on a farm.”

The other one laughed.

Dren looked at him a little too long, then faced forward and kept walking. Forty years of temper hadn’t disappeared because a swamp taught him patience. He’d have to watch himself.

He went along the pens.

He looked at every face. Some looked back. A few came to the rails and called to him, because a buyer was a way out of a pen. He kept walking.

He got to the end of the yard and had nothing.

He went back slower and still found nothing. At the end he stood with one hand over his mouth.

He knew Sheo’s children as they’d looked nine years ago, not as adults starved, bruised, and burned by the road. Fimmion had been sixteen then. Here, two hundred people wore the same dirt and the same shut expression.

“Do they have names?” he asked the sergeant.

“They have numbers.”

“Their names.”

“I’ve got a lot number and a headcount.” The sergeant wasn’t being cruel. He didn’t understand the question. “What do you want names for?”

Dren returned to the middle, took a breath, and used the voice he’d once thrown across markets.

“IS THERE ANYBODY HERE,” he called, “WHO IS THE CHILD OF SHEO. OF SOTHA BAL.”

His call ran down the yard and came back off the end wall.

Everything stopped. Two hundred heads came round.

“SHEO OF SOTHA BAL,” he called again. “SPEAK UP.”

“Here.”

It came from his left, a man up at the rails with his arm through. “Here. That’s me. Sheo. That’s my father.”

“Me.” Another one, three pens down, already up on his feet. “Sir. Over here, sir, that’s me.”

A third shouted farther off. Then a fourth, two more, and within seconds eight or nine people had hands through the rails, claiming to be Sheo’s son or daughter. A name was the only thing they had left to sell, and a buyer looking for one family was the best chance that yard had offered since dawn.

The sergeant started laughing.

Dren let it run. He’d have done the same. In a hall in this city, he already had.

When it quieted he said, “Then tell me my name.”

The yard went quiet in a different way.

“Any of you. Sheo’s children know who I am. Tell me my name and I’ll take you out today.”

Nobody answered. One tried and stopped at the first sound.

Hands left the rails a few at a time. People sat again. Dren had made two hundred people watch a door open and shut.

A voice from the back of a pen on the right said:

“You’re dead.”

Dren turned around.

“You’re dead,” the voice said again. “My father put a board up for you. It’s still there.”

She rose and came through the others to the rail.

“You’re Dren.”

He’d been searching for Sheo’s grown children among the dead and then the living. He’d passed this pen three times without seeing the sixteen-year-old he remembered in the woman before him.

She was about twenty-five. Dirt covered her, her split lip had closed badly, and the rag around her forearm needed changing. She had Sheo in the shape of her eyes and nowhere else Dren could find.

“Fimmion,” he said.

Something went out of her legs and she held onto the rail.

“Your father sent me,” he said, near enough. “I came from Sotha Bal.”

“Is he alive.”

“He was when I left. Standing in his yard, shouting about a wall.”

She made a noise and put her forehead against the timber.

He gave her a moment. He gave himself one too.

Then he said, “Where is your brother.”

She didn’t lift her head.

“Fimmion. Where is Caius.”

“On the wall,” she said, into the wood.

Dren stood there.

“He was on the wall when it came in. Gate end.” Her voice was flat, one Dren recognized from his own mouth in a Drenovar shack. “I didn’t see it. I asked four people. They all said the same, so I think it’s true.”

“Fimmion.”

“I haven’t said it out loud yet,” she said. “That’s the first time I’ve said it out loud.”

She cried without any change in her face.

Dren put his hand through the rail. She held it. The two soldiers let them, and even the sergeant went quiet.

He’d come ninety kilometers, then eighty more, to bring Sheo’s two children home. He would arrive with one.

After a while he let go and faced the yard.

Two hundred people from a town that no longer existed, with nothing behind them and an auction ahead. None had much reason to refuse somewhere else.

He’d spent the last week begging people who still had a wall to stand on.

“Sergeant,” he said. “Who do I talk to about price.”

“Depends what you want.”

“The lot.”

The sergeant looked at him.

“All of it,” Dren said.

They set a trestle table under the sales awning. The clerk hurried down with his ledger. A buyer taking an entire intake in one transaction was new enough to bring him in person.

Word spread. By the time the scale was ready, the awning held two merchants after the same lot, a pair of officers, a man in a good coat who never gave his office, and soldiers gathering along the rail.

The clerk priced the lot aloud in front of everyone, for his own protection.

Eight hundred a head for the sound ones. Four for the injured. Men discussed a dozen who wouldn’t be sound again, and the clerk entered those at two. Dren kept both hands flat while they priced bodies.

It came out at a hundred and fifty-one thousand, and change.

“Fifteen coins,” Dren said, “and the rest out of the silver.”

“Wait.”

It was one of the merchants. He had been getting redder for some minutes.

“He can’t take the lot. I’ve been on that list five weeks. I have carts outside for forty head, and he can’t clear an intake because he happens to be carrying.”

“He has an appointment,” the clerk said serenely.

“So do I. Mine is Thursday.”

“Then this is not your appointment.”

“There has to be an allowance,” an officer said. “People are waiting on this intake. You can’t let one man clear a yard.”

It got loud. The clerk held his pen up and let it. Dren stayed still with fifteen coins under his hand.

A voice from the back said, “No need.”

Everybody stopped.

Dren hadn’t picked him out because he wasn’t dressed for it. Gray, sixty-odd, plain coat, working hands. He looked like somebody’s uncle, and every officer under the awning had gone straight-backed.

He came forward and looked into the yard for a moment and then at the ledger.

“Let him have Trovgar. Sotha Bal has fallen. I’ve had no report, but I sent enough men to be sure. You’ll have more intake inside the month, and better stock, because they’ve been eating.” He nodded to the merchant. “Bring your carts in three weeks and take your pick.”

The merchant called that generous. The officers called it sensible.

Dren sat at the table with his hands flat on the wood.

He didn’t move his face. He knew the distance to his sword, felt fifteen coins under his palm, and saw Fimmion at a rail forty feet away. Two hundred lives depended on him staying dull. He stayed dull.

He had held a leg in salt water and not screamed the third time. He could do this.

“Now,” said the man in the plain coat. “What was the name? Dren, was it?”

“Drenovar Kvatch.”

“Kvatch. Drenovar.” He pulled over a stool and leaned his forearms on his knees. His interest was genuine and worse than anger. “Help me with this. Nobody buys two hundred head. What are they for?”

“Labor.”

“Labor for what?”

“I’m opening a manufactory.”

“A manufactory.” The man smiled a little. “Of what?”

He reached into the bag and took out the hourglass and set it on the table.

It was a good one: dark turned wood, two glass bulbs that fit between both hands, pale gold sand in the lower bulb. It came from a dead lord’s cabin and was the one object on that ship Dren understood at sight.

“This,” he said.

The whole awning leaned in.

“I invented it.” Dren turned it over. Sand began to fall. “You set it going. It runs out in the same time, every time.”

The man in the plain coat picked it up and turned it in his hands.

“And what does it do?”

“It runs out.”

“Yes. And?”

“You know how long a thing took.” Dren made his shrug small, pleased, and stupid. “It’s for households, mostly. Cooking. My wife never got a pot off the fire at the right moment, and I got tired of hearing about it.” He put a little laugh in. “Boiling, steeping. Traders use them so a customer can’t say he waited longer than he did.”

There was a pause under the awning.

“Cooking,” said the man in the plain coat.

“There’s been more interest than I expected,” Dren continued warmly. Now was the time to be tedious. “It seems small. But every household boils something, and once one house has one, the rest of the street wants one. I can’t fill the orders by hand. That’s why I’m in this yard buying two hundred people instead of sitting at home like a sensible man.”

He was aware of the merchant with the carts losing interest first. Then one of the officers looked away down the yard.

“Keep that one. You’d do me a service. I need men to use it hard and tell me where it fails. The glass is always the trouble. Send me word in six months about the seal and I’ll count it a favor.”

The man in the plain coat looked at the hourglass in his hands. The sand was about a third down.

“How will this serve me?”

“Honestly? Not much. It’s for kitchens.”

Attention left the man’s face. He’d approached because a stranger buying two hundred people was the most interesting thing in the province. He’d received an egg timer and a small businessman worried about glass seals.

He set it down on the table, still running.

“The coins,” said the clerk.

The fifteen coins lay on a tray while the back-room man checked them. Both merchants drifted closer to the money.

“These aren’t from anywhere I know,” one merchant said.

“Whose head is that?”

“Look at the edge on it.”

“They’re all the same,” the back-room man told the table. “Not similar. Same weight, width, depth of strike, all fifteen. I’ve never handled coin like it. Nobody in this city has.”

They all looked at Dren.

This question could kill him. He’d prepared for it on the ride.

“Eastern trade,” he said.

“East.”

“Past Trovgar.” Dren turned one hand over. “I’ve got three buyers farther east than the market runs, and they pay in that. I asked your question and got talk about a mint I didn’t follow. A man paying gold can keep his secrets.” He looked around the table. “They’ve ordered more timers than I can make. That’s the other reason I need the labor.”

The awning relaxed.

A notice had hung in the hall for a decade: Trovgar, The Far East Market. Neighboring trade. Everyone under the awning knew goods came from unnamed people farther east over bad routes. Dren had handed them their own map.

Not one of them asked another question about it.

Nine years ago he’d told the truth in that hall and been laughed out. Today he said his gold came from the east and he made kitchen equipment, and they sold him two hundred people.

The clerk wrote it all out. It took an hour and a half.

The clerk itemized a bill of sale and charged four hundred silver for a transit warrant. The silver came from Dren’s separate pouch. For the movement declaration, Dren registered a Drenovar-owned timer works at Dry Fork, with the column authorized through the west gate and down the southern bypass because the eastern military roads were closed.

“Dry Fork,” the clerk said. “There’s nothing there.”

“There’s sand,” Dren said. “The timers need filling.”

The clerk wrote that down. It sounded like planning.

He sealed and stamped the papers, then pushed them across the trestle.

The man in the plain coat had stood up to go. He picked the hourglass up off the table on his way past, and looked at it once more, and put it in his coat pocket.

“Kvatch. When your manufactory opens, send word. I’ll want to know how it goes.”

“I will.”

The man left with two hundred people watching. Dren sat before the paper.

He had one gold coin left, about half the silver he’d brought separately, a horse, and two hundred human beings listed by condition in his name under a seal.

They chained the two hundred Trovgar captives that evening.

Dren hadn’t thought past the sale. He’d spent an hour being clever under an awning without asking how two hundred people left a Veyloth yard. The answer was a smith, four soldiers, and a cart of iron working by lamplight.

Wrist to wrist on a running chain, twenty to a line. Ten lines, a ring and lock at each end.

It took most of the night. He made himself watch all of it.

The sergeant gave him ten keys on a wire loop. In Dren’s hand they weighed about as much as a good knife.

“Don’t lose those,” the sergeant said. “Smith’s not coming out to the wastes for you.”

The quartermaster sold him the rest next morning and did him no favors.

The four-wheeled wagon was sound except for a broken brake Dren argued down. He bought thirteen full-ration days of coarse eating grain and a cask of salt. Three bags of seed grain cost more than the food; he had their mouths sewn, waxed against damp, and stowed high under canvas. Seed wasn’t supper.

He added four mattocks, two axes, a maul, rope, an iron pot, canvas, and one waterskin for every traveler. Three large barrels rode as reserve, their loose hoops soaked tight while Dren watched. Barley and pressed hay went aboard for the gelding, enough to reach Dry Fork with something left.

It cost his final gold coin and most of the separate silver.

“No draft animals?”

“No. I’ve got two hundred.”

The quartermaster thought that was very funny.

He spent a second night in Veyloth while the gelding ate, drank, and rested without a saddle. The room above the square had the softest bed he’d seen since the ship, but Dren sat on the dry fountain in the dark and turned the keys in his hands.

In the morning they brought them out of the pens and put them in the road.

Two hundred people stood in ten chained lines in the gray light outside the north yard because he’d bought them.

The clerk handed two folded counterparts of the warrant up to him, businesslike now that the money was counted.

“Keep one with the column and one on you. If ours stop either of you, show it and say nothing else. Two hundred head, in transit by the west gate and southern bypass, sealed and paid. Nobody argues with a seal.”

“And if they want to see the seal twice?”

“Then you show it twice.” The clerk stepped back. “Good fortune with your manufactory, Kvatch.”

They went out through the west gate.

The west-gate guard counted them, touching every fourth shoulder and calling the number. At two hundred he entered it in a book.

Dren left one sealed counterpart in Fimmion’s hand and put the other inside his coat.

A kilometer out, Dren turned the rested gelding and rode back to the wagon to check his arithmetic.

There were thirteen full-ration days of eating grain. The sealed seed bags didn’t enter that count. Every person carried a filled skin, and the three barrels were reserve, not the whole supply. Even so, Dren wasn’t crossing open country in one pull. His return chart marked an old toll cistern, two herders’ soaks, the Dry Fork channel, and the rock cisterns farther west. He’d move from water to water and dig where the marks had gone dry.

Dry Fork was the first real bet.

Dry Fork had no well. It had a dead river.

The river had left its channel and an old stone bridge across the empty middle. That crossing was the only reason a checkpoint had ever stood there.

Water lay under the sand, not much and not close. The patrol had kept a stone-lined soak open under the second arch for as long as Dren could remember. He’d watered animals there for twenty years, and it had never failed him.

Nobody had tended it in years. Dren had seen the abandoned tents himself days ago.

The soak had sat under a desert bridge for years. It would be buried.

He had four mattocks, two axes, a maul, and two hundred people. They’d find the water or learn exactly how bad his plan was.

He got up on the wagon board where they could see him and told them.

He told them about the western coast, its river, fish, and food growing wild. Nobody owned it. The road was long, and he wouldn’t lie about that. At the first safe fresh water on the coast, he promised to take the chains off. They wouldn’t remain anybody’s property, whatever the paper in his coat said.

He’d rehearsed it hundreds of times on a beach. Out loud, it came badly.

Nobody argued.

He waited for the polite face, the return to work, the man asking which way west and losing interest. Two hundred people only waited to see if he’d finished.

He hadn’t said it right. They had nothing behind them, and the alternative that morning had been another buyer’s cart. A person in chains didn’t get an opinion about the road.

He climbed down and felt good about nothing.

They followed the authorized western route most of that day, refilled at the old toll cistern, then bent south around the ridge. The direct road led to Sotha Bal, where an army was waiting.

That night he sat down by the wagon and asked the question.

“Who knows Dry Fork?”

The question traveled down the lines and returned with almost nothing. They were Trovgar people. Dry Fork was a bridge and three tents on a road to a place that had died before most of them were born.

Two men had heard of it. Neither could have found it.

“I can find it.”

Fimmion, from the third line, with her wrist up because the chain came with it.

“My father took me when I was nine or ten, years before you left. He had to sign something, and my mother made him bring me because I wouldn’t stop asking.” She thought. “There’s a stone bridge with no river, three tents, and a dead fig tree.”

“Still dead.”

“Then I can find it.”

He looked at her for a while.

“I’ve got to go to Sotha Bal.”

“I know you do.”

“I need to know about your father and mother. I can’t take two hundred people in there. An army is sitting on the town, and I won’t march you through it to find out what’s left.”

“Then go.”

“Three days. Refill tomorrow at the limestone soak, then at the patrol cistern. Stay west of the ridge with the high ground on your left. Don’t go near the main road. At the bridge there’s a soak under the second arch. It’ll be full of sand. Start with the mattocks as soon as you arrive. Don’t wait for me.”

“All right.”

He repeated the route line by line so Fimmion didn’t carry it alone. Follow her. Keep together. Stay off the main road. Refill every skin at both marked sources. At the end, dig for water under the bridge.

Two hundred people stood on ten running chains, twenty to a line. The lines went where the wagon went, and the wagon moved only when they pulled. They couldn’t scatter or even lie down without agreement.

Dren kept one foot in the stirrup too long. A man on the fourth line asked the question waiting for him.

“What happens if you don’t come back?”

The whole camp heard it. Fimmion turned her head.

Dren looked at two hundred people chained in a waste, each with a skin, reserve barrels in the wagon, and only his marks between one water source and the next. Thirteen days of eating grain had already begun to shrink. The seed grain remained sealed.

“Then you want to be at that bridge, and you want to be digging,” he said.

It was honest and not much. He didn’t dress it up.

He got up on the horse and rode away east into the dark.

He smelled Sotha Bal before he saw it. By now, that was familiar.

He came from the northwest in midmorning, stopped on the rise a kilometer out, and raised the glass.

It was nearly over. Three or four men still fought in each of two lanes, small and ugly, after there was nothing left to hold.

The wall was gone in three runs, taken apart. Two timber machines stood on the flat with counterweights hanging from them. Beside them lay round stones the size of a man’s chest, and short shots pocked the ground to the wall.

Sotha Bal had raised its wall by carrying dirt in baskets. Veyloth had brought machines.

Through the glass, the difference was obscene. Veyloth men came in mail, helmets, shields, and ranks with sergeants behind. Sotha Bal wore whatever its people owned. A man charged a shield line with an eight-foot pole. Three soldiers put him down in less than two seconds.

One side had been eating.

Sotha Bal had once held about a thousand. Refugees from Ghor Vhuul, West Outpost, and the failing farms had swollen it toward two thousand. The eastern shanties now stood half empty, bedding and pots abandoned beside paths trampled south before the siege closed. Some people had run in time. Many hadn’t.

He put the glass away and rode down.

Two soldiers rushed him from behind a burned cart three hundred yards out. They shouted, and Dren had the crossbow out of its saddle sleeve before he decided to move.

The stock found his shoulder. The first soldier filled the sight picture. Eight years in a hide by the tree line made the choice for him.

At forty feet Dren dropped the point from the man’s chest to the outside of his thigh and pulled.

The bolt struck above the knee and spun him down. He hit hard, rolled onto his side, and began shouting through his teeth.

Dren was already cranking. The mechanism didn’t care what had happened; it took twenty seconds every time. The second soldier kept coming while the horse turned under him. At half wind, Dren used his voice.

“I’M FROM DRENOVAR.”

The runner didn’t stop. Somebody else stopped him.

“HOLD.”

An older voice called from the cart. The runner cut sideways and stopped with his spear up, chest heaving, eyes moving between Dren and the wounded man.

Dren finished winding, laid in another bolt, and kept the bow low.

Five or six surrounded him. One cursed Dren. The older soldier told him to shut his mouth and knelt beside the wounded man.

“You shot Kess.”

“He was running at me.”

“He was doing his job.”

“So was I, and mine involves not being run at.”

The older man cut Kess’s trouser leg, checked both sides of the wound, and bound around the bolt without pulling it free.

“Missed the big vessel,” he said. “He’ll keep the leg if nobody gets clever.”

Kess told him what Dren could do with the leg.

The older soldier held out his hand. “Give me that.”

Dren gave him the crossbow. The man turned it over. “What is it?”

“A bow.”

“That’s not a bow.”

“A bow with a crank. Takes twenty seconds to wind. He would’ve had me.”

That settled them a little. They understood a weapon once they knew its limit.

“Papers,” the older man said.

He read the warrant while two soldiers lifted Kess onto a door. His finger paused at the number, then at the Dry Fork route.

“You’ll answer for Kess to the officer inside. You ride between us, and this stays with me.” He tucked the crossbow under one arm. “Try anything and twenty seconds won’t help.”

“Before we go, what’s your business here?”

“I’ve got a licensed labor column on the Dry Fork route. What intake came out of Sotha Bal?”

The group went quiet.

“No sale count here,” the older soldier said.

“There’s always a count.”

“Not one count.” He glanced at the town, smoke lifting from nine places. “Hundreds ran before we closed the south. Patrols caught two groups and sent them north under separate orders. Able ones went to baggage and levy companies. Some slipped through. The people left on these streets wouldn’t stop fighting, so there won’t be a yard lot from this assault.”

“Why not.”

“Because they wouldn’t stop.”

He kept talking without being asked.

“We called terms twice the first day and again this morning. Put it down and live. My officer went up under a cloth. They shot at the cloth.” He wiped his mouth with his wrist. “Old men came out this morning. One fellow charged our line with a hoe. You can’t take a man alive when he won’t have it. You let him kill you, or you do the other thing. We did the other thing all day.”

A younger soldier said, “They knew. From the second day, they knew it was finished.”

“Then why?”

He was about nineteen and wanted an answer. Nobody had one.

Dren knew why. He’d watched them build the wall days ago. They had positions for dying and no plan for leaving.

He’d offered one. They told him to get out of the sun. For half a second, something warm and disgusting rose in him: I was right. He put it down.

“Nobody at all,” he said.

“Well.” The older one shrugged. “There’s the tavern.”

“What tavern.”

“South side. Mud and sand, hardly any timber, so it won’t take a light. Somebody barricaded it the first night. There are people inside. We could burn off the roof, but we’ve got better work. They’ll come out when the water’s gone.”

The older soldier escorted Dren through the army under guard, crossbow tucked under his arm. Another walked at the horse’s right stirrup with a spear. Kess went the other way on a door, still conscious and swearing.

Men sat in rows eating. A farrier worked beside the road. Two lads carried another wounded man on a door and argued about their grip. The ruined town had become a workplace at the end of a shift.

At the broken wall, an officer came out and raised a hand.

He was about thirty, clean, in a good coat with a sword that hadn’t left its scabbard.

“Report.”

“He shot Kess in the thigh,” the older soldier said. “Kess charged before challenging him. The bolt missed the vessel. Stranger surrendered the bow and has sealed transit papers.”

The officer looked at Dren. “You’re having a busy morning.”

“So is Kess.”

Dren handed down the warrant.

The officer read until he reached the number.

“This is a transit warrant.”

“It is.”

“For two hundred head.”

“Sealed and paid. Census and Excise, Veyloth. Look at the stamp and route.”

“I can see both. Trovgar intake, all two hundred, to a Drenovar-owned works at Dry Fork by the western and southern bypass.”

“That’s right.”

“Where’s the column?”

“On the authorized route. I received every person I paid for. I’m not here to add Sotha Bal people to that bill.”

Dren dismounted.

He did it slowly with the bad leg, making the officer wait. On the ground, Dren stepped close and took back the warrant.

“Do you know what I’ve got out in the wastes? Two hundred Trovgar laborers, a wagon, and a sealed schedule your own office approved. I paid a hundred and fifty-one thousand at that counter. Now your army has shut the roads, scattered every settlement, and left free civilians barricaded in a tavern inside your lines.”

“Your warrant doesn’t cover Sotha Bal.”

“I KNOW.” His shout crossed the broken wall and turned heads fifty yards away. “I’m not asking you to sell me anybody. I’m asking for a release and escort certificate. Free citizens, named and counted, placed under my protection as far as Dry Fork. Something every patrol can read before deciding they’re deserters, strays, or unbooked prisoners.” He tapped the seal. “Your office sent my works down this road. I need the road to produce living people occasionally.”

“Sir.”

“Don’t sir me. Get your general. I sat with him under an awning and put a piece of my own manufacture in his hand. I’ll ask whether he wants twenty-odd civilians using his soldiers and water, or one certificate clearing them off his ground.”

The older soldier looked off to the side with an expression of pure enjoyment.

Forty men heard an angry rich stranger in a foreign coat shout over sealed paper and money. None thought it was his job to interfere.

“There’s the tavern,” the officer said.

Dren stopped.

“Twenty or so on the south side. We haven’t gone in. Get them out unarmed, on their feet, and I’ll certify them as free civilians released from the military perimeter into your escort. They don’t go on your sale warrant, and you don’t own them.”

“Good.”

The officer took a breath. “Until I sign, they stay inside our line. If they run, my men will treat them as fugitives. Get them to walk.”

“They won’t run.”

The older soldier returned Dren’s unloaded crossbow. Two guards stayed with him all the way to the tavern. Kess had bought him that much attention.

He walked the horse down through what was left of the town to get there.

Days ago, forty people had been building a wall in this street.

He kept his eyes on the road ahead and let the rest sit at the edges, where it was still too much.

The tavern was a long, low mud-and-sand building, one story, with small high windows. Timber, stones, and most of a cart packed the doorway. Sacking and furniture blocked the windows. Somebody had done the job properly.

There were four Veyloth men sitting in the shade across from it who had clearly been sitting there for two days.

Dren went to the barricade and laid his palm against it.

“SHEO. ARE YOU IN THERE? IT’S DREN.”

Nothing.

“SHEO. IT’S DREN.”

Movement sounded through the timber, many people trying not to make any.

A voice came through the door. It wasn’t Sheo.

“...Dren?”

A woman stood close to the wood, speaking more steadily than she had any right to.

“Is that really you, Dren?”

He put his forehead against the timber.

“Arvyna.”

“Oh,” she said. “Oh, it is.”

“It’s me.”

“They said you were dead. Sheo made a board.” Her voice broke on the last word and returned. “He wouldn’t let anybody take it down.”

“I know. Arvyna, listen to me.”

“Are they still out there?”

“They are, across the road. Listen. Open this and come out together, slowly. Nobody will own you or put you in chains. The officer is signing a military release that names you as free people under my protection to Dry Fork.”

A long pause.

“I don’t understand.”

“I know.”

“Dren, what have you done?”

“I’ll explain every bit. I promise. But come now, on your feet and slow. Nobody runs before the paper is stamped. If anyone runs, the soldiers will go after them and I can’t stop it. Do you understand? Nobody runs.”

She turned from the door and spoke low and fast. Somebody argued. Arvyna stopped it.

They unbuilt the barricade one piece at a time. The four soldiers across the road stood and watched with empty hands.

The door came open into the dark.

They came into the light in a line, blinking, filthy, holding one another.

Seven old people. Two had to be carried, including a woman who seemed to weigh nothing.

Eleven children. The oldest about twelve and the youngest being carried.

Two young women. One held a butchering knife and didn’t want to release it. Dren said, “Please,” and waited until she put it on the step.

Arvyna came last.

She’d gone gray. She was too thin, her hands cut from the barricade, and two sleepless days showed in her face.

Twenty-one people.

Dren stood there and waited.

He watched the doorway through the last person, then longer than he had reason to.

He leaned in.

“Where’s Sheo?” he asked quietly.

Arvyna looked at him.

Her face held no answer. She’d spent two days in a room with eleven children, seven old people, and no windows while the town ended in noise. Nobody had told her. Nobody could.

She shook her head. Once. Small.

Dren nodded.

He turned to the officer, who waited down the road with his hands behind his back.

“Twenty-one,” Dren called.

“I’ll certify twenty-one.”

“Separate from the two hundred Trovgar people already on my warrant,” Dren said. “These twenty-one are free citizens in my escort. Put that plainly.”

The officer looked uncomfortable with the word free, as if it complicated the form.

“I can’t issue them against your warrant.”

“I don’t want you to. What can you issue?”

“A military release and escort certificate.” He had thought about it on the walk down. “Twenty-one civilians, no prisoner status, permitted through our lines under your protection to Dry Fork. My name and seal. Patrols will honor it.”

Dren looked at him for a moment.

“Write exactly that and I’ll put my trust in you.”

“You have it.”

Pleased to have found the correct paper, the officer called for a clerk and writing board. His soldiers spread into the lanes again.

Dren turned back to Arvyna.

“I found Fimmion.”

Her hand came up over her mouth.

“She’s alive. She’s west of here with two hundred people, leading them to Dry Fork. I spoke with her before I rode out.” He couldn’t stop smiling. “She’s filthy and thin, but food and rest will mend most of it. She’ll reach the bridge before us.”

Arvyna made a sound and folded forward and he caught her arm and held her up, and one of the young women came and took her other side, and she got a hand onto Dren’s shoulder and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

“Fimmion. Oh, my girl. Thank you.”

“She’s all right.”

“And Caius?”

Dren had nowhere to go.

“Fimmion believes he didn’t make it.”

She said nothing, only waited.

“He was on the wall at the gate end. That’s where they came through and where it was worst. Everyone Fimmion asked gave the same account.” He covered her hand on his shoulder. “He chose the worst post. Nobody ends up at the gate by accident.”

“Was it,” she said. “Was it.”

“It was quick,” Dren said.

That was the one part he didn’t know.

She went down, not far because he caught her, and made a sound he’d once heard from himself in a Drenovar shack. He sat on the step, held her, and let it happen.

Over the top of her head he could see the street.

The street ran to a market burned black. Bodies lay along the near gutter. Something in a doorway burned steadily, and its smoke climbed straight in the still air.

For the first time since riding in, he looked properly.

He wasn’t going to find Sheo. Nothing in the street could carry a certain name. Four days of searching would only expose more bodies, while two hundred people moved toward a dead river and twenty-one waited on a doorstep.

Dren held Sheo’s wife and didn’t go looking.

They walked out that afternoon.

He put the two smallest children on the horse and led it. Arvyna walked at his shoulder. The old people stayed in the middle with the two young women beside them. They passed the machines and broken wall without challenge.

The officer’s clerk handed Dren a separate certificate as they passed: twenty-one named free civilians, released from the Sotha Bal military perimeter for protected passage under Drenovar Kvatch to Dry Fork. It carried the officer’s seal. Dren put it in his coat beside the Trovgar warrant, not inside it.

Each traveler took a skin from army stores and filled it. Two jars and the cook pot carried reserve water. Dren spent his remaining silver on five days of army biscuit, a sack of bruised grain, and fodder for the horse. The officer was generous with anything that didn’t cost another signature.

They made eight kilometers before dark, all the old people had in them, and camped in a fold of ground.

They softened biscuit in hot water and shared grain porridge thin enough to drink. It wasn’t much, but no one went to sleep with nothing.

Nobody complained. They’d come out of a barricaded room, and there was sky over them.

One old man, a Dry Fork patrolman nine years ago, spent most of the night describing the hyena on a spear. It was the same unexplained animal Dren and Sheo had found, and nobody could persuade him onto another subject.

It took four more days to cover the remaining ground. The old people managed a few kilometers at a time, the children needed shade, and Dren wouldn’t push either. They refilled at the patrol cistern and a shallow charted soak, rested the horse through the hottest hours, and fed it from the fodder sack. They reached Dry Fork late on the fourth afternoon, about two days behind Fimmion’s party.

Smoke turned Dren’s stomach until the glass showed orderly cook fires and people resting around them.

The wagon stood at the bridge with its tongue down. Reserve barrels were open, and washing ran from the wagon to the dead fig tree. Somebody had decided two hundred people were going to be clean.

A line went down into the riverbed under the second arch and came back up.

They went down empty and came up full, passing pots, skins, the iron cook pot, and somebody’s boot hand to hand from a hole in a river dead before anybody’s grandfather.

There was a heap of dug sand beside it the size of a haystack.

The two hundred Trovgar people sat in ten long chained curves around the fires, like rope coiled on a deck. They’d spent the column’s two days at Dry Fork digging. Four walking days and those two digging days had used about five full-ration days; roughly eight remained for the road. All three seed bags stayed sealed and dry under canvas.

They’d hauled the wagon through the southern detour in irons, found both intermediate water marks, and reached the bridge first.

Fimmion came out fast, then faster. Arvyna was already running badly, skirt in both hands, making a sound all the way.

Dren stopped the horse and let them have it.

In the last light, with two children holding his coat, he watched a woman get her daughter back under a bridge with no river, twenty-six kilometers from a town that had been full the week before.

Then he led the rest of the twenty-one free Sotha Bal survivors into camp. They wore no chains and settled where they chose.

Dren gave them the night for rest and mourning. They’d leave west at sunrise.

A man asked him that night when the chains were coming off.

He asked it well. He’d spent two days in the riverbed with a mattock and had earned the question. The ten Trovgar lines went quiet. The twenty-one free people listened from their own fire.

Dren had carried the keys against his chest since Veyloth.

Dry Fork was still a road junction, and patrols could call two hundred loose captives strays and take them back before Dren’s papers mattered. He’d promised freedom at safe water on the coast. This wasn’t it.

He didn’t explain.

He was tired. He’d chosen not to search for Sheo among the dead and spent nine years explaining himself to people who returned to work. Somewhere between the tavern and this bridge, he’d stopped trying.

“Not yet,” he said.

He got up and walked toward the bridge.

Anger followed him out of the firelight, low along the chains. He kept walking and didn’t turn around. He’d never done that to anyone before.

Two hundred and twenty-one evacuees, plus Dren: two hundred captives from the Trovgar lot still chained, twenty-one survivors from the Sotha Bal tavern free. One wagon, one sound horse with fodder left, a skin for every traveler, three reserve barrels, about eight days of eating grain set for the road ahead, three sealed bags of seed, and five hundred and sixty kilometers to a beach only Dren had seen.

He sat up on the parapet of the bridge with the last of the light going and looked west.
