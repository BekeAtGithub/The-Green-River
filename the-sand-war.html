The Sand War

He sat down on a stack of cut timber at the edge of the yard because the leg had had enough of him, and he watched them get ready to die.

That was the thing he kept arriving at and could not get around.

It was not that they thought he was lying. Some of them probably believed him. The woman at the fire had looked at the chart with her whole attention for about four seconds, and in those four seconds she had believed him, and then she had gone back to bundling reeds because a thing that might be true a long way off cannot compete with a thing that is certainly true and walking up the north road at you.

That was all it was. It was not important.

Nothing was important here except the next hour. Water was important. The wall was important. Whether the boy on the east corner had ever held a spear was important. Everything else, every idea a man could have, every place a man could describe, had to get in line behind hunger and behind fear and behind the column, and the line never moved, because there was always another hour.

He had been like that himself. He knew exactly how it worked. He had stood in that same dirt nine years ago worrying about a supply run and a quarterly allotment and whether Sotha Bal would eat that winter, and if a man had walked up to him then and started talking about an ocean, he would have nodded and gone back to work.

Knowing it did not help. It just meant he could not even be angry properly.

They would sooner do this. All of them together, panicking, starving, hauling dirt in baskets to make a wall a foot higher, and then fighting a war they were going to lose against a town that had more of everything. They had a plan for dying here. Every man on that wall could tell you where he would stand and who was on his left.

Not one of them had a plan for leaving.

Leaving was the mad idea. Leaving was the one that got you laughed at.

He went to Sheo one more time.

He waited until there was a gap, and he did not open with the coast, and he did not touch the strap on the bag.

“One thing,” he said. “Then I’m out of your way.”

Sheo didn’t say no. He didn’t have the time to say no.

“Your kids. Trovgar.”

That got him. Everything in his face moved about an inch.

“I’ll go and get them.”

“You’ll what?”

“I’ve got a horse in me and about ninety kilometers.” It was a lie about the horse. He didn’t have one yet. “I’ll go east and swing wide of the road and come up on the market from the south, and I’ll find them and I’ll bring them back to you.”

Sheo looked at him for a moment. “I sent a rider four days ago.”

“And?”

“And nothing. That’s four days.” He rubbed his face with the flat of his hand, hard, the way a man does when he is not going to let himself feel it. “Riders get held up. It’s ninety kilometers and there’s men on the roads.”

“Then let me go.”

“Go. Nobody’s stopping you.”

“And when I bring them back to you,” Dren said, “you give me an hour.”

Sheo went still.

“One hour, sat down, with the chart open. That’s the whole of it. I put your children in front of you and you give me one hour and you listen to the way out. And if you don’t like it, I’ll get on that wall with the rest of you and I’ll never say the word again.”

And there it was, out in the open, the thing he had promised himself he would not do.

Sheo’s voice came out low and flat and worse than shouting.

“You’re bargaining with me. Over my children.”

“That is not what I’m doing.”

“Stop.” He put both hands up this time. “Stop it. Dren. We are being invaded. There are three hundred men on the north road. My wall is not finished. My children are ninety kilometers away and I cannot go and get them because if I leave this town it falls tonight, and every hour you stand here talking to me about paradise is an hour I am not spending on the only thing I can actually do.”

He was shaking a little. Not with rage.

“Enough. Please. Enough of your nonsense.”

Then he turned around and went back to the wall, because the wall was there and it needed him.

Dren stood in the yard for a while after that.

He thought about getting up on that wall.

He worked it honestly, the way he had learned to work everything out on the beach, sitting with it and turning it over instead of jumping at it. He had a crossbow that nobody here had seen the like of and he was a good enough shot with it now, and he had about forty bolts. He could stand on the east corner and put down some men.

Twelve, if it went well. Twenty if it went perfectly and nobody got a ladder up near him.

Three hundred.

They were all going to die here. Not all of them, maybe, but enough that it made no difference to the arithmetic, and adding one fifty-something man with a bad leg to the top of that wall changed nothing at all except where his body ended up.

And if he was going to spend himself, he could spend himself on the two who were not here yet.

If he could not evacuate Sotha Bal, he would go and try Trovgar. Trovgar was a market town full of traders, people who moved for a living, people who understood a road and a cargo and a better price somewhere else. Traders might actually hear it.

It was thin. He knew it was thin.

It was more than he had an hour ago.

He tried three more people on his way through the town, because he could not help himself.

A man carrying a bundle of staves, who listened to nine words and kept walking. Two women filling sacks with sand, one of whom told him quite kindly that he should get inside before dark. And a man of about his own age sitting on a step sharpening a blade badly, who heard the whole thing, all of it, the coast and the water and the road around the green, and who nodded along the entire time and then asked Dren if he had anything to drink.

That was the closest he got.

The stalls were down at the south end, past the well.

He caught himself looking for Betty before he was halfway there and made himself stop. She had been old when he left. That was nine years ago. There was nobody here to ask about it and no point asking.

There were four horses in the pens, all of them young, none of them broken to much, all of them turning and stamping and throwing their heads at the noise coming off the wall. Somebody had watered them and nobody had brushed them in days.

There was no one at the stalls. Of course there was no one at the stalls. The stable man was up on the wall with a basket of dirt in his hands like everybody else.

Dren stood there a while, because he wanted to do it properly. He wanted to find the man and put it to him and pay him in front of witnesses.

Nobody came. Nobody was ever going to come.

So he took the quietest of the four, a dark bay with a good chest, and he got a saddle off the rail and did it slowly with his stick leaned against the post, talking to the animal the whole time the way he had talked to an empty cabin for eight years.

Then he opened the pouch.

He counted out four coins into his palm. Gold, worn smooth at the edges, with the face of some king stamped into them that not one living person on this continent had ever heard of. He had hauled them ninety days across a desert for no reason he could have defended.

He set them in a row on the top rail where a man would find them.

They were worth more than the horse, more than the stalls, more than the whole south end of that town. He had sat in a wrecked cabin and let a fistful of them run through his fingers and thought, this is the most money I have ever seen and it is worth exactly nothing.

It was the first time any of it had ever bought anything.

He rode out through the south gate, and the men on the gate did not ask him where he was going, because there was nothing out south and never had been, and a man riding into nothing was not their problem today.

He went east first.

Wide, well off the road, out into the flat where the ground went pale and hard and the grass gave up entirely. The road ran north of him with three hundred men somewhere on it and he wanted a lot of empty between them and him.

It was the country he had been born into and he had forgotten how much of it there was.

Nine years on a coast where the ground grew food by itself and it had gone out of him completely. Out here the air pulled the water off your lips. The horizon shook. There was nothing to look at in any direction but the same pale nothing going away from you, and the sun came off it and got in under the brim and into your eyes from below.

It was also worse than he remembered. That was not sentiment. He knew what he was looking at now. There had been scrub along this stretch once, thin stuff, enough to keep the dust down, and it was gone. There had been a pond track where the herders cut across and there was no track because there was nothing to cut across to.

The demon had kept working the whole time he was away.

He rested the horse properly, every hour or so, because he had one horse and no second one, and because his leg made him stop anyway. He would get down and stand on it and let it scream at him and then get back up.

He swung north in the late afternoon and picked up the old east line, and he rode into the dark and kept going, because there was a moon and the ground was flat and he could not make himself stop.

He gave it up somewhere in the middle of the night. Hobbled the horse, lay down in his coat on ground that was still giving back the day’s heat, and slept about three hours with the crossbow next to his hand.

He was moving again before it was properly light.

He saw the smoke about an hour after that.

It was out ahead of him and to the left, a column of it standing straight up in the still morning air before it flattened off and drifted, and it went up higher than anything he had ever seen go up.

He stopped the horse.

He knew a cookfire. He knew a kiln, and a burnt-out farm, and what a granary looks like when it goes. This was not any of them. This was the kind of smoke that means the thing burning is not a building.

He got the glass out with his hands not quite steady and put it on the smoke and followed it down.

Twenty kilometers, near enough, and the heat was already coming up off the flat and making everything crawl. What he got was bad and soft and shivering at the edges and it was more than enough.

The town was under it. He could see the shape of where it was, a long dark smear on the flat, and the smear was wrong, because a town from a distance is a lot of small square edges and this had almost none. It was low. Whole runs of it were just low.

Orange in three or four places, still working, still eating something.

He held it as long as he could and then he put the glass away and kicked the horse into a canter he had no business asking of it.

He came up on it from the south through the middle of the morning.

The first thing was the smell, and it reached him a long way out, a kilometer at least, coming down the wind. He knew it. He had learned it on his own leg lying in a swamp, and then again in the hold of a ship, and this was that smell laid over the whole world with wood smoke through it.

He tied a strip of cloth over his mouth and it did nothing.

They had built a wall here, and recently, and it had not been much. Packed earth between timber, a ditch in front of it, a gate frame with a tower over it. Somebody had put months into that.

It had come in. Not fallen over. Come inward, in a run about thirty feet wide, the timbers snapped off and driven back and the earth pushed out behind them in a fan, and everything that had been standing on top of it was down in the ditch underneath. Whatever went through there had gone through fast and had not stopped.

There were birds working the ditch. A great many of them. They went up when he came near and they did not go far.

He got down off the horse.

Past the wall it was streets, or it had been. Burnt frames standing with nothing in them. Roofs down into the rooms below. Whole rows gone to black stubs with the mud brick still standing in low walls a foot high, so you could walk along and see the shape of somebody’s house and the shape of the room and the shape of where the fire had been, and nothing else at all.

The market was ash to the ankles for a hundred paces.

And the people were everywhere.

He had told himself on the ride in that he had seen dead people. He had. He had buried eight of them with his hands and said words over them and thought about it for years.

That was not this.

Some were burnt and had gone small and dark and were not shaped like people anymore. Those were nearly the easiest. The rest were out in the open where they had been caught, and they had been done to. Arms gone. A man in a doorway with his face driven in so far that there was no face, only the back of a head and a shape. Two together at the mouth of a lane who had been chopped at long after there was any reason to. Things lying about the street that had come off somebody.

Not a battle. A battle is two lines and then one line runs. This had gone on afterwards, at leisure, on people who were already finished.

Dren got about forty paces in and went down on one knee in the ash and brought up everything he had eaten, and knelt there over it with his eyes streaming and the birds carrying on behind him, and it took him a long time to get up.

Days. This was days old.

Four days ago Sheo had sent a rider.

He was too late. He had ridden ninety kilometers through the night to be a week late, and it had been over before he ever walked into Sotha Bal, before he came down off the mountains, before he ever left the coast.

Then the other thought arrived, the one that got him back on his feet.

Caius. Fimmion.

He did not know their faces. That was the thing he understood standing there in the middle of it. He had left when they were small enough to be underfoot and they were grown now, and every one of these people was somebody’s child, and he had no idea what he was looking for.

He started looking anyway.

He went along the market row first, because that was where Sheo had said they would be, over a stall. He looked at every one of them. He made himself do it properly, one at a time, crouching where he had to, turning nothing over that did not need turning, saying something under his breath each time before he moved on because he could not just walk past a man and not say anything.

The young ones were the worst of it. There were a lot of young ones.

He worked the row, and then the lane behind it, and then the ground in front of the broken wall where the most of them were, and the sun came up over the top of the smoke and started cooking the whole thing, and Dren kept going through the wreckage of Trovgar looking for two faces he had never seen.

Somewhere in the middle of it he started counting, which he had not meant to do.

It ran by itself. He had spent eight years counting things with nobody to talk to. Fish in a net. Marks on a door frame. Men in a column, four abreast, in tens.

He came out the far end of the market row with a number, and the number was wrong.

He walked the rest of it properly then, the lanes, the yards behind the frontages, the ground inside the wall and the ditch outside it. It took him most of the day and it was the worst day of work he had ever done in his life.

Trovgar was a market town. Two thousand people. More on a trading day, and they had been under siege, so there would have been people in there from every farm and holding for thirty kilometers around.

He did not find two thousand. He did not find a quarter of that.

So the rest had gone somewhere.

He found where at the break in the wall, and he had walked over it twice without seeing it. The ground going out west through the gap was churned into a wide beaten road, forty feet across, packed flat and scuffed to dust. Not cart ruts. Not hooves. Feet, a great many of them, going out and not coming back, with the horse tracks running along both sides of it.

They had taken them.

He stood in the gap with his hand on a broken timber and, for the first time since he had seen the smoke, he had something that was not grief.

He had somewhere to go.

Vey Trading Camp was an hour north and he smelled it before he saw it, cook smoke and latrines and horses, all of it clean and ordinary and appalling.

It was a real camp. A palisade of cut timber that somebody had thought about, ditched, with a gate and a watch on the gate. Rows of tents in lines. A picket line of horses that were in better condition than anything in Sotha Bal. There was a forge going. There were men playing at something with stones in the dirt.

Two of them came out at him a long way off with spears low, and a third stayed back with a bow, which told him these were not farm boys.

“Off the horse.”

He got off the horse. It took him a while and the stick made it worse and he let it, because a man who is slow getting off a horse is not a man anybody is frightened of.

“Which side.”

“Drenovar,” Dren said.

The spears came up about a foot. That was all it took. Drenovar fed Veyloth, Drenovar had always fed Veyloth, and a man from Drenovar was a man on the correct side of a line that Dren had not known existed until three days ago.

“Long way from home.”

“Buying,” Dren said.

That was the whole of it. They walked him in.

What he had not expected was how pleasant they were. They wanted to talk. They had been out here for months and a stranger was better than stones in the dirt, and inside ten minutes he had been given a place in the shade against a wagon wheel, and somebody had taken his horse to water without being asked, and a man of about thirty with a burn up one arm was telling him how it had gone.

They were proud of it.

That was the thing Dren had to sit with. The wall had held for eleven days, which they told him with a certain respect, and then they had brought something up in the night that he understood from the description to be a ram under a roof of green hides, and gone in at the weak seam in one push before dawn. The man drew it for him in the dust with a finger. Where the ditch was shallow. Where they had put the archers. How the gate tower had come down with people still on it.

He was a good storyteller. He kept checking that Dren was following.

“And after,” Dren said.

“After.” The man shrugged. “After is after.”

Somebody else laughed at that and Dren laughed too, and the laugh came out of him easily and correctly and he hated himself for how easy it was.

They talked about the south for a while, another column gone down toward Sotha Bal, and Dren let that go past his face without doing anything at all, and asked instead whether the roads were safe for a man with money on him, which was the kind of thing a buyer asks.

Then he put it in.

“I heard you took a lot of prisoners.”

“A lot.”

“Selling any of them?”

The whole wagon-side went up. It was the funniest thing they had heard all week. The one with the burn slapped his knee and turned around and shouted something at the next fire over, and a man came across grinning with a skin and made Dren drink out of it before he would answer.

It was whisky, or their idea of it. Thin and hot and it tasted like the barrel had held something else first. He had eight years of a dead lord’s spirit in him and he drank it down and said it was good and they liked him for it.

“Not us,” the man said. “We don’t sell. They went west four days ago. All of them, the whole lot, walked out under escort.”

“West where.”

“Veyloth. Where else. There’ll be an auction, they always do it out of the same place, that big hall up the hill.” He wiped his mouth. “Census and Excise. You want the counter inside, they book you in, you can’t just walk up. And listen. Go soon.”

“Why soon.”

“Because it’s two hundred head at once and the price is going to be on the floor.” He was delighted to be giving good advice. “Nobody’s got barns for that many. There’s going to be some very good deals.”

Dren said that was worth knowing, and thanked him, and took another pull of the skin when it came round again.

He sat there in the shade of the wagon for another quarter of an hour, because leaving immediately would have been the wrong shape, and the men who had done that to Trovgar sat around him talking about the price of grain.

Then he got on his horse and went west.

It was eighty-odd kilometers and it took him the rest of that day and most of the next. He walked the horse more than he rode it. He had four gold coins’ worth of nothing in his pouch out there, because there was not a man on that road who could have made change for one of them, and it struck him somewhere in the second afternoon that he had crossed a desert carrying a fortune he could not buy a bag of grain with.

Veyloth came up out of the flat the way it always had, the wall first, then the smell.

He went in through the south slums because that was the way he knew.

They were empty.

He came up the same lane he had ridden Betty down nine years ago, the one that had been so packed he could not get through it at a walk, hands coming at his legs, a man with no shoes shouting at him about a wheel, children on a wall, washing strung so low he had to duck. He remembered holding his breath through it.

There was nobody in it.

The garbage was still there. That was the part that got him. Drifts of it in the corners and up against the walls, but it had gone grey and light and it had stopped smelling of anything except dust, and there was a crust over the top of it where the rain had come and gone a few times. Doors stood open. In one of them there was a stool set up like somebody had been sitting on it. Cloth on a line above the alley, hanging in strips, bleached to no colour at all.

It still smelled like the slums. Underneath, in the stone. It smelled like ten thousand people who were not there.

He rode through it slowly and the hooves came back off the walls at him and that was the only sound in the whole quarter.

He worked out most of it as he went. The uprising had come out of here. They had marched on Drenovar and killed a market full of people, including a young man at a farm stall, and Veyloth had put it down, and whatever had been left after that had been fed into a war one conscription at a time until there was nothing in the pot.

The lanes were the same lanes. The turns were the same turns. He knew the way and it was like knowing the way around a body.

Up through the middle, and the city started having people in it again, and they were all doing war. Carts of staves. A yard of men being shouted at in ranks. A woman nailing a notice to a post and a queue already forming under it. Nobody looked at him twice, because he was one old man on a horse and the city had bigger things on.

Then the official district, and the streets opened, and the stone got cleaner.

The fountain was still dry. Nine years and nobody had ever turned it back on. The bowl had sand in it now, a smooth drift of it up one side, and the yellow scum was long gone.

He tied the horse to the rail at the corner where the stalls were, and stood in the square a moment looking up at the hall with its columns and its high doors, and then he went in.

The ceiling was the same. The brown tide-mark where the rain got in was bigger and had a friend now. The shutters were still badly fitted and the light still came through them in stripes with the dust moving in it, and it was the exact same dust, and Dren stood in the doorway for a second with his hand on the frame.

A guard put a board in front of him.

“Name and source.”

“Drenovar Kvatch. Drenovar.”

The guard wrote it down without looking up and waved him at the queue.

It took an hour. He stood in it and did not mind. He watched the counters work, four of them going, and he learned the shape of the place again. Everything went through here. Requisitions, headcounts, tariffs, the lists on the wall, the notice about conscription quotas by district.

Then the queue moved and he came up to a counter and looked at the man behind it and stopped.

He saw the colours before he saw the face.

Turquoise. A robe of it, heavy and good and dyed right through, the colour of a bay at seven in the morning with the sun coming over the trees behind you. Over it a belt of worked gold. And on the hand that was moving the papers, a ring with a green stone in it, cut with faces so it threw the light, and the green of it was the green that comes up off shallow water in a jungle at the hour when the sun gets low enough to go under the canopy.

The two words Dren had said out loud in his life, both of them alone, both of them to nobody, and this man was wearing them.

He had gone up in the world. Nine years ago it had been a purple coat.

“Business.”

“The lot coming in from Trovgar,” Dren said.

The clerk’s pen stopped and he looked up properly, and something went across his face, and then he sat back.

“I know you.”

Dren said nothing.

“I do. I know you.” He was smiling now, and it was a real smile, a delighted one. He turned and half called it down the counter to the woman working the next one along. “This is the jungle man. This one. Nine years ago, stood right there, wanted to go into the trees and fetch supplies out.” Back to Dren, warm as anything. “I have told that story a hundred times. You have no idea. You are a story in this building.”

“Is that right.”

“Everyone said you would die.” He was still smiling. “You didn’t.”

“No.”

“Well.” The clerk looked at the coat, and the boots, and the beard, and the stick, and Dren watched the arithmetic start. “Well, and here you are, and you have done all right for yourself by the look of it, and you have come back to a very different city.”

Then it arrived.

The smile came off in one movement, like a hand wiping a table.

“You came in on Sotha Bal’s business.”

“That was nine years ago.”

“GUARDS.” He was up on his feet and his voice went right up into the stained ceiling and every counter in that hall stopped. “Guards. This man is from Sotha Bal.”

They came fast. Two of them, one from each end, and they had his arms before he had finished turning around, and the stick went down on the stone with a crack that carried.

Dren did not struggle. He had learned a long time ago in a swamp what panic did for you.

“Ahem.” He said it into the middle of the noise, flatly, at a volume for a room. “I am from Drenovar.”

“He is lying. He is a Sotha Bal man, I took his declaration myself.”

“You took it in a year when there was no war and no sides in it,” Dren said. “And I gave you a town I was buying for. Look at your own book at the door. I gave my name and my source not one hour ago and I gave the same one.”

“Anybody can say a word at a door.”

“Then check the record.”

The guards had him but they had stopped moving him, because he was not fighting and because he was talking like a man who expected to be listened to, and there is nothing in a building like that one which is more powerful than a citation.

“My name is Drenovar Kvatch,” he said. “I am the father of James Kvatch, of Drenovar. There is a deed of property standing in my name in this office. Seventeen Hlograth Avenue. You will find it under Drenovar residential, outer district. Go and look at it and then decide what I am.”

The hall was entirely silent.

The clerk stood there hating him for about four seconds, and then went to the shelves, because he had no choice, because forty people had heard a records citation given in a records office and there is only one thing you can do with one of those.

It took him a long time. Long enough for Dren to stand there with a man on each arm and his stick on the floor and count the stripes of light coming through the shutters.

Sheo never filed it.

That was the whole of it, and Dren understood it standing there. He had scribbled that deed out on parchment he found in a dead boy’s front room, with a borrowed pen, sitting in a chair he had not got up from in a week, and he had handed it over, and Sheo had folded it and put it somewhere safe and carried it eighty kilometers home to a town that was three weeks from going hungry. And then he had never made the two-day trip to Veyloth to stand in a queue for an hour and pay a fee to register a shack on the outskirts that neither of them was ever going to live in.

Nine years of a dead man’s name sitting in a drawer.

The clerk came back with a folder.

He did not apologise. He sat down, and he put the folder square on the counter, and he said, “Release him,” without looking at anybody, and the hands came off Dren’s arms.

A guard picked up his stick and gave it back to him. Dren thanked him.

“You are,” the clerk said, in a voice with a great deal of paper in it, “a legal resident of Drenovar.”

“I am.”

“Of a property at seventeen Hlograth Avenue.”

“Yes.”

“On which,” said the clerk, and here something came back into his face, and it was very nearly pleasure, “there is an outstanding balance.”

He turned the folder around so Dren could see the column, which was a courtesy, and pointless, because Dren could not read it.

“Twenty-seven thousand, five hundred and forty-two.”

“For a shack.”

“For a shack, over nine years, unoccupied and undeclared.” He ran the pen down it. “Assessment. Late assessment. Penalty on the late assessment. Compounding on the penalty. Non-residency surcharge, which is annual. Wartime levy, which is also annual, and backdated.” He looked up. “How would you like to settle it?”

And there it was. The oldest thing in the world. Dren had stood at this counter once and been told that he clearly did not have extra money and did not warrant any more of the man’s day.

He put his hand in the pouch and set three coins down on the wood.

They made a particular sound. Everyone at that counter heard it.

The clerk looked at them for a moment without touching them. Then he picked one up, and turned it over, and his thumb went across the face on it, and something happened to his mouth.

“What is this.”

“Gold.”

“I can see that it is gold.” He was holding it up close now. “Whose head is this?”

“I have no idea,” Dren said, which was true, and it came out easily because it was true.

They sent for a scale. Not the counter scale, a proper one, and a second man came out from a back room to work it, and the two of them weighed all three coins and then weighed them against each other and had a conversation in low voices that Dren was not part of.

They were the same. That was what the low voices were about. Not close. The same.

“These are struck,” the second man said, to the clerk, not to Dren. “Look at the edge. Look at the ground behind the head. That is not a hammer and a punch, that is not anybody’s hand, that is a machine doing it.”

“Value them.”

He valued them. It took a while, and there was more low talking, and in the end the clerk wrote a figure and turned it around and had to say it out loud anyway.

A little over ten thousand each.

Dren said nothing. Inside, he did the only arithmetic that mattered, and it came out at a number he had been carrying across a desert without knowing what it was.

They took two of the coins and a great deal of arguing over the third, and in the end they counted him back the difference in local money, a sack of small silver and copper so heavy it changed the way he stood.

He put the receipt inside his coat.

“Now,” he said. “The Trovgar lot.”

The clerk had been about to call the next man. He put his hand down.

“The Trovgar intake is being processed.”

“I want to buy.”

“Then you will need an appointment.”

“Fine.”

“Viewings are by appointment only, in rotation, and the list is not short.” He had it out already, a long ledger with a ribbon in it, and he was running his finger down a page with the particular slowness of a man who enjoys this part. “I have nothing this week. I have nothing next week. The soonest I can offer you is the fourth of the month after, in the afternoon, and I will tell you now that by then the sound ones will be gone.”

“That is six weeks.”

“That is the list.”

“And is there,” Dren said, “any way of moving up the list.”

The clerk looked at him.

Dren put one more coin on the counter.

The ledger closed. It did not even take a moment. There was no negotiation, no lowering of the voice, no glance to either side, because there was nothing shameful happening as far as anybody in that hall was concerned. This was simply what the system was for.

“There is a cancellation,” the clerk said, “this afternoon.”

“How fortunate.”

“Two hours. Take this to the yard gate on the north side and give it to the sergeant.” He was already writing it. He blotted it, and stamped it, and folded it, and held it out, and as Dren took it the clerk did not let go straight away.

“You want to know the funny part,” he said.

“Go on.”

“Nine years ago you stood there and asked me for supplies for a town of a thousand people and you did not have the price of a cart between you.” He let go of the paper. “And now look.”

“Now look,” Dren agreed, and put it in his coat, and went out past the queue into the light.

He stood in the square by the dry fountain for a while with his hand on the horse’s neck, not doing anything.

Nine years ago he had told that man the truth. He had told him there was ground out there and water out there and it was worth going to look, and he had been laughed out of the building for it.

Today he had put metal on the counter and been given the afternoon.

The yard was on the north side, outside the district wall, and he heard it before he got to it.

It was a long open lot with pens down both sides of it, timber and iron, roofed with old sailcloth in patches so that some of them had shade and most did not. There was a trough at each end. There were flies over the whole of it in a haze, and the smell was the smell of a great many people kept in one place in the heat for four days.

Two hundred, near enough. Crammed in by the dozen, twenty and thirty to a pen, sitting because there was not room for all of them to stand.

They were all young. That was the first thing he understood, and then he understood why, and had to stand still for a second and let it go past. There were no old people here. There had been old people in Trovgar.

The sergeant read his paper and walked him down the middle of it like a man showing off a yard of horses, and two soldiers came along behind because that was procedure.

“What are you after?”

“Just looking.”

“Look at that one, then.” The sergeant knocked on a rail with his knuckle and a man inside flinched without getting up. “Look at the back on him. Put him on a plough and he will pay for himself in a season.”

Dren said something noncommittal.

“Or that.” One of the soldiers, further down, grinning, tipping his chin at a woman sitting against the far rail with her arms around her knees. “That one’s wasted on a farm. You would not want that one on a farm.”

The other one laughed.

Dren looked at the soldier for slightly too long, and then made himself look away and keep walking, and the thing in his chest went down again, and he made a note that he was going to have to be careful for the next hour, because forty years of temper does not go anywhere just because a man has learned patience in a swamp.

He went along the pens.

He looked at every face he passed, one at a time. Some of them looked back. Most did not. A few came up to the rails, because a buyer is a way out of a pen and everybody there knew it, and they said things to him, and he had to keep walking.

He got to the end of the yard and had nothing.

He turned around and did it again, slower, and had nothing again, and stood at the bottom of the lot with his hand over his mouth.

He did not know what he was looking for. That was the whole problem and it did not get better by walking past it twice. He was looking for two children he had last seen at knee height, in a yard of two hundred filthy, sunburnt, starved young people who all had the same look on their faces.

“Do they have names?” he asked the sergeant.

“They have numbers.”

“Their names.”

“I have a lot number and a headcount.” The sergeant was not being cruel. He genuinely did not understand the question. “What do you want names for?”

So Dren walked back up to the middle of the lot, where the pens ran down both sides and he could be heard from either end, and he took a breath and put his voice out the way he used to put it out across a market.

“IS THERE ANYBODY HERE,” he called, “WHO IS THE CHILD OF SHEO. OF SOTHA BAL.”

It went right down the yard and came back off the wall at the end.

Everything stopped. Two hundred heads came round.

“SHEO OF SOTHA BAL,” he called again. “SPEAK UP.”

“Here.”

It came from his left, a man up at the rails with his arm through. “Here. That’s me. Sheo. That’s my father.”

“Me.” Another one, three pens down, already up on his feet. “Sir. Over here, sir, that’s me.”

A third, further off, shouting something.

And a fourth, and then two more, and inside about six seconds there were eight or nine men in that yard with their hands through the rails calling out that they were the son of Sheo of Sotha Bal, because a name was the only thing any of them had left to sell and a buyer looking for one particular man was the best thing that had happened in that lot in four days.

The sergeant started laughing.

Dren stood in the middle of it and let it run and did not feel anything about it at all, because he would have done exactly the same, and because he had done exactly the same in a hall in this city with a story he could not prove.

When it quieted he said, “Then tell me my name.”

The yard went quiet in a different way.

“Any of you,” Dren said. “Sheo’s children know who I am. Tell me my name and I will take you out of here today.”

Nobody said anything. One of them tried, and got as far as a sound, and stopped.

The hands came off the rails, a few at a time, and the men went back down to sitting, and Dren stood in the middle of the lot in the heat and understood that he had just made two hundred people watch a door open and shut.

Then a voice from the back of a pen on the right said:

“You’re dead.”

Dren turned around.

“You’re dead,” the voice said again. “My father put a board up for you. It’s still there.”

She got up off the ground at the back of the pen and came forward through the others, and they moved to let her, and she got her hands on the rail.

“You’re Dren.”

He had been looking for two young men. He had been looking for two young men in a market full of the dead and then in a yard full of the living, for two days, and he had gone past this pen three times.

She was about twenty-five. She was filthy, and her lip had been opened and closed badly, and there was a rag knotted round her forearm that needed changing four days ago. She had Sheo somewhere in the shape of the eyes and nowhere else at all.

“Fimmion,” he said.

Something went out of her legs and she held onto the rail.

“Your father sent me,” he said, which was near enough. “Four days ago. I have come from Sotha Bal.”

“Is he alive.”

“He was four days ago. He was standing in his own yard shouting at people about a wall.”

She made a noise and put her forehead against the timber.

He gave her a moment. He gave himself one too.

Then he said, “Where is your brother.”

She did not lift her head.

“Fimmion. Where is Caius.”

“On the wall,” she said, into the wood.

Dren stood there.

“He was on the wall when it came in. The gate end.” Her voice was completely flat and he recognised it, because he had heard it come out of his own mouth in a shack in Drenovar. “I didn’t see it. People told me after. I’ve asked four people and they all said the same thing so I think it’s true.”

“Fimmion.”

“I haven’t said it out loud yet,” she said. “That’s the first time I’ve said it out loud.”

She was crying without any change in her face, which is a thing people do when they have been at it for a while and stopped noticing.

Dren put his hand through the rail and she took hold of it, and the two soldiers stood there and let them get on with it, and even the sergeant had gone quiet.

He had come ninety kilometers and then eighty more to bring Sheo back two children.

He was going to arrive with one.

After a while he let go of her hand, and he straightened up, and he turned around and looked at the yard.

Two hundred people. All of them from a town that no longer existed. All of them young, all of them with nothing behind them and an auction in front of them, and not one of them with a single reason on this earth to say no to a man who offered them somewhere else.

He had spent the last week of his life begging people who still had a wall to stand on.

“Sergeant,” he said. “Who do I talk to about price.”

“Depends what you want.”

“The lot.”

The sergeant looked at him.

“All of it,” Dren said.

They set a trestle table up at the top of the yard under an awning, because that was where the sales were done, and the clerk came down from the hall himself with the ledger under his arm, and Dren understood from the speed of it that a buyer taking a whole intake in one transaction was not a thing that had happened before.

Word went round. That was the part he had not accounted for. By the time they had the scale set up there were nine or ten people under that awning, two merchants who had come for the same lot, a couple of officers, a man in a good coat who never said what he was, and behind them along the rail a slow gathering of soldiers with nothing to do.

The clerk priced it in front of everybody, out loud, which Dren later understood was for his own protection.

Eight hundred a head for the sound ones. Four for the ones carrying injuries. There was a whole conversation about a dozen of them at the far end who were not going to be sound again, and the clerk wrote them in at two, and Dren sat there while men argued about what a person was worth and kept his hands flat on the table.

It came out at a hundred and fifty-one thousand, and change.

“Fifteen coins,” Dren said, “and the rest out of the silver.”

“Wait.”

It was one of the merchants. He had been getting redder for some minutes.

“He cannot take the lot. I have been on that list five weeks and I have carts sitting outside this minute for forty head, and he cannot simply walk in and take an entire intake because he happens to be carrying.”

“He has an appointment,” the clerk said serenely.

“So do I. Mine is Thursday.”

“Then this is not your appointment.”

“There has to be some allowance made,” one of the officers said. “There are people waiting on this intake. You cannot let one man clear a yard.”

It got loud. Two more joined in. The clerk sat with his pen up and let it, and Dren sat very still with fifteen coins under his hand and did the only thing he could do, which was nothing.

Then a voice from the back of the awning said, “No need.”

Everybody stopped.

He had been standing there the whole time and Dren had not picked him out, because he was not dressed for it. Grey, sixty-odd, a plain coat over a plain shirt, a working man’s hands. There was nothing on him at all. He looked like somebody’s uncle and every officer under that awning had gone straight-backed.

He came forward and looked into the yard for a moment and then at the ledger.

“Let him have Trovgar,” he said. “Sotha Bal has fallen. I have had no word yet but I sent enough men to be sure of it, and when it comes in you will have a fresh intake inside the month, and better stock than this, because they have been eating.” He nodded at the merchant. “Bring your carts back in three weeks and take your pick.”

The merchant said that was very good of him. The officers agreed that it was sensible.

Dren sat at the table with his hands flat on the wood.

He did not move his face. He was aware, with total clarity, of exactly how far he was from his sword, and of the fifteen coins under his palm, and of two hundred people behind him in the pens, and of a woman at a rail forty feet away, and that every single one of those things depended on his face not moving.

So it did not move.

He had held a leg in salt water and not screamed the third time. He could do this.

“Now,” said the man in the plain coat. “What was the name? Dren, was it?”

“Drenovar Kvatch.”

“Kvatch. Drenovar.” He pulled a stool over with his foot and sat down on it, unhurried, and put his forearms on his knees, and looked at Dren with an interest that was entirely genuine and much worse than anger. “Satisfy me on something, because I have been standing here five minutes and I cannot make it come out. Two hundred head. Nobody buys two hundred head. What is the purpose of them?”

“Labour.”

“Labour for what?”

“I am opening a manufactory.”

“A manufactory.” The man smiled a little. “Of what?”

And here it came, the question, and Dren had known it was coming since the camp.

He reached into the bag and took out the hourglass and set it on the table.

It was a good one. A frame of dark wood, well turned, two glass bulbs of a size to sit in two hands, the sand in the bottom one gone pale gold. It had come off a shelf with a rail on it in a dead lord’s cabin and it was the only object on that entire ship that Dren had understood the moment he looked at it.

“This,” he said.

The whole awning leaned in.

“I invented it,” Dren said. He turned it over. The sand started. “You set it going, and it runs out, and it runs out in the same time, every time. That is the whole of it.”

The man in the plain coat picked it up and turned it in his hands.

“And what does it do?”

“It runs out.”

“Yes. And?”

“And you know how long a thing has taken.” Dren shrugged, and made the shrug small and pleased and stupid. “It is for household use, mostly. Cooking. My wife could never get a thing off the fire at the right moment, and I got tired of hearing about it.” He let a bit of a laugh into it. “Boiling. Steeping. That sort of thing. Some of the traders in Drenovar have taken to them for the stalls, so that a man cannot say he has been waiting longer than he has been waiting.”

There was a pause under the awning.

“Cooking,” said the man in the plain coat.

“And I have had a great deal of interest in it,” Dren went on, warmly, leaning in a little, because now was the moment to be tedious and he could feel it. “That is the thing. It seems small. I thought it was small. But there is no household in the world that does not boil something, and once one house on a street has one, the rest of the street wants one, and I have had orders I cannot fill by hand, which is the whole of my difficulty, and which is why I am standing in a yard in Veyloth buying two hundred people instead of sitting at home like a sensible man.”

He was aware of the merchant with the carts losing interest first. Then one of the officers looked away down the yard.

“Keep that one,” Dren said. “Genuinely. Take it. You would be doing me a service. I want it in the hands of men who will use it hard and tell me where it fails, because the glass is the trouble, the glass is always the trouble, and if you were to send me word in six months as to whether the seal has held I would count that as a favour.”

The man in the plain coat looked at the hourglass in his hands. The sand was about a third down.

“How will this serve me?”

“Honestly?” Dren said. “It will not, much. It is for kitchens.”

And that was it.

Something went out of the man’s face. Not suspicion. Attention. He had come over to that table because a stranger buying two hundred people was the most interesting thing in the province, and he had been handed an egg timer and a small businessman with a problem about glass seals, and there was nothing there for him at all.

He set it down on the table, still running.

“The coins,” said the clerk.

They had them out on a tray by then and the second man from the back room was going over them one at a time, and the two merchants had drifted round to look, because merchants will always come and look at money.

“These are not from anywhere I know,” one of the merchants said.

“Whose head is that?”

“Look at the edge on it.”

“They are all the same,” the back-room man said, to the table generally. “That is what I have been telling him. Not similar. The same. Same weight, same width, same depth of strike, all fifteen. I have never handled coin like it and neither has anybody in this city.”

They all looked at Dren.

This was the one that could kill him and he had four days on a horse to think about it.

“Eastern trade,” he said.

“East.”

“Out past Trovgar.” Dren turned his hand over, easily. “I have buyers out that way. Beyond the market, further out than the market goes, three of them, and they pay in that. I asked the same question you are asking and I got a lot of talk I did not follow about a mint, and I stopped asking, because a man who pays in gold can keep his secrets.” He looked around the table. “That is the other reason I need the labour. They have put in for a quantity I cannot come near.”

And the entire awning relaxed.

Because there was a notice pinned inside that hall, and it had been pinned there for a decade, and it said Trovgar, The Far East Market. Neighbouring trade. Every man under that awning had grown up knowing that things came in from out east, from people nobody had ever met, and that the routes were bad, and that was simply the shape of the world. Dren had handed them their own map back.

Not one of them asked another question about it.

Nine years ago he had stood in that hall and told the truth, which was that there might be something out there worth walking to, and they had laughed at him until he left.

Today he had told them that his gold came from the east and that he made kitchen equipment, and they had sold him two hundred people.

The clerk wrote it all out. It took an hour and a half.

A bill of sale, itemised by pen. A transit warrant, because two hundred head do not simply walk out of a city, and that was another four hundred, and Dren paid it out of the silver. A movement declaration naming a destination, and Dren gave Drenovar, and the clerk wrote Drenovar, and nobody in that yard would ever check.

Then the seal, and the stamp, and the clerk turned the paper round and pushed it across the trestle with two fingers.

The man in the plain coat had stood up to go. He picked the hourglass up off the table on his way past, and looked at it once more, and put it in his coat pocket.

“Kvatch,” he said. “When your manufactory opens, send word to this office. I will want to know how it goes.”

“I will,” Dren said.

And he went off down the yard with two hundred people watching him go, and Dren sat at the trestle table with a piece of paper in front of him.

He had one gold coin left and about half a sack of silver.

He had a horse.

And he had two hundred human beings, listed by pen and by condition, in his name, with a seal on it.

They chained them that evening.

He had not thought about it. That was the thing he had to stand there and take. He had spent an hour and a half being clever at a trestle table and he had never once thought about how two hundred people get from a yard in Veyloth to anywhere else, and the answer was a smith and four soldiers and a cart of iron, working down the line by lamplight.

Wrist to wrist, on a running chain, twenty to a line. Ten lines. A ring at each end and a lock on the ring.

It took most of the night. He made himself watch all of it.

The sergeant gave him the keys on a wire loop at the end of it, ten of them, and Dren stood in the lamplight with them in his hand and they weighed about as much as a good knife.

“Don’t lose those,” the sergeant said. “Smith’s not coming out to the wastes for you.”

The quartermaster sold him the rest in the morning and did not do him any favours.

A wagon, four wheels, sound enough, with a broken brake that Dren argued the price down over. Sacks of coarse grain. A cask of salt. Seed grain in three bags with the mouths sewn, which cost more than the food did. Mattocks, two axes, a maul, coils of rope, an iron pot, a roll of canvas, a bundle of waterskins, and three barrels for water with the hoops gone loose that he had to stand there and watch a man soak tight before he would take them.

It came to one gold coin and most of the silver.

“No draught animals?”

“No,” Dren said. “I have two hundred.”

The quartermaster thought that was very funny.

He spent the night in a room above a place off the square and did not sleep in the bed, which was the softest thing he had been on since a dead lord’s cabin. He went down at some point and sat on the lip of the dry fountain in the dark with the sand drifted up one side of it, and looked at the hall across the square, and turned the keys over in his hands.

In the morning they brought them out of the pens and put them in the road.

Two hundred people in ten lines, in the grey light, in the street outside the north yard, standing in chains because he had bought them.

The clerk came out with the warrant folded and handed it up to him on the horse, businesslike, no ceremony left in it now that the money was counted.

“Keep that on you and keep it dry. If any of ours stop you out in the wastes, and they will, you show them that and you say nothing else. Two hundred head, in transit, sealed and paid. Nobody argues with a seal.”

“And if they want to see the seal twice?”

“Then you show it twice.” The clerk stepped back. “Good fortune with your manufactory, Kvatch.”

They went out through the west gate.

The guard on the gate counted them. It took him a while. He walked the length of the column touching every fourth person on the shoulder and saying the number out loud, and when he got to the end he said two hundred, and wrote it in a book, and that was the last thing Veyloth ever had to do with any of them.

A kilometer out, Dren pulled the horse round and rode back down the line to the wagon and did the arithmetic.

He had known it would be bad. He had not known the shape of it.

The grain would go eight days if he was careful and nine if he was hard about it. The water in three barrels was one day for two hundred people and that was the number that mattered, that was the only number that mattered, and it meant he was not crossing anything. He was going from water to water, and every leg of it had to be short enough that two hundred starved people in irons could walk it before they started dropping.

Dry Fork first, and Dry Fork was the bet.

There was no well at Dry Fork. There had never been a well at Dry Fork. What there was, was a dead river.

It had run once, long enough ago that nobody alive had seen it, and it had left its channel behind and an old stone bridge still arching over the empty middle of it, and the road crossed there, which was the only reason there had ever been a checkpoint on that ground at all.

And under the sand in that channel there was water. Not much and not close, but there. The patrol had kept a soak dug out under the second arch for as long as anybody could remember, a hole in the riverbed with stones laid round the lip and a board over it, and Dren had watered animals out of it every single year for twenty years, going down and coming back, and it had never once been dry.

Nobody had been at Dry Fork in years. He had stood in those tents himself a week ago.

Which meant the soak had been sitting open under a bridge in a desert for years, and sand does what sand does.

He had two axes, a maul, and four mattocks in that wagon, and he had two hundred people.

They would dig it out or they would not.

After that he would work it out.

He got up on the wagon board where they could see him and told them.

He told them there was a coast, and it was west, and the ground grew food on its own, and there was a river running into a bay with more fish in it than in every water any of them had ever seen. He told them nobody owned it. He told them it was a long way and that he would not lie about how long. He told them that when they got clear of the roads he was taking the chains off, and that they were not going to be anybody’s property when this was finished, and that he had a paper in his coat that said otherwise and they would all have to live with that paper for a few weeks yet.

He said it badly. He had said it well in his head about four hundred times on a beach and it came out badly.

And nobody argued.

That was what undid him. He stood on a wagon board waiting for the thing that had happened every single time, the polite face, the going back to work, the man asking which way west and then losing interest, and it did not come. Two hundred people looked up at him and waited to see if he was finished.

Not because he had finally said it right.

Because they had nothing behind them. Because the alternative on offer that morning had been a cart to somewhere else. Because a man in chains does not get to have an opinion about the road.

He had spent nine years learning how to be believed and it turned out the trick was to buy people first.

He got down off the wagon board and did not feel good about anything.

They went west along the flat for most of that day, and then he turned them south, wide, well out past where the ground rose, because the direct road went to Sotha Bal and there was an army sitting on Sotha Bal.

That night he sat down by the wagon and asked the question.

“Who knows Dry Fork?”

It went down the lines and came back with almost nothing. They were Trovgar people. Trovgar was ninety kilometers the other side of Veyloth and Dry Fork was a bridge and three tents on a road to a place that had stopped existing before most of them were born.

Two men had heard of it. Neither could have found it.

“I can find it.”

Fimmion, from the third line, with her wrist up because the chain came with it.

“My father took me. I was nine or ten. He had to go and sign something and my mother made him take me because I would not stop asking.” She thought about it. “There is a bridge with no river under it. That is the whole of what is there. A stone bridge over a ditch full of sand, and three tents, and a fig tree that is dead.”

“It is still dead,” Dren said.

“Then I can find it.”

He looked at her for a while.

“I have to go to Sotha Bal.”

“I know you do.”

“I have to know about your father. And your mother.” He said the next part carefully. “I cannot take two hundred people into that. There is a column sitting on it and I do not know what is left and I am not walking you into the middle of it to find out.”

“Then go.”

“Three days. South, and stay west of the ridge, and keep the high ground on your left the whole way, and do not go near the road for any reason. Three days to the bridge.” He said the next part plainly, because she was going to find out anyway. “There is a soak dug under the second arch, in the riverbed. It will be full of sand. Take the mattocks off the wagon and start on it the hour you get there and do not wait for me.”

“All right.”

He went down the lines after that and told it to all of them, line by line, in the dark, so that it was not one girl carrying it on her own. Follow her. Keep the lines together. Do not go near the road. There is water at the end of it and it is under the sand and we are going to have to go and get it.

Nobody was going anywhere and everybody in that camp knew it. Two hundred people on ten running chains, twenty to a line, and a line goes where the line goes. All ten of them go where the wagon goes, and the wagon does not move unless they pull it. They could not scatter if they had wanted to. They could not lie down without agreeing on it first.

Which was not the part that kept him standing there with his foot in the stirrup for longer than he needed to.

Then a man in the fourth line said it out loud, because somebody always does.

“What happens if you do not come back?”

The whole camp heard it. Fimmion turned her head.

Dren stood there in the dark and looked at two hundred people chained in ten lines in the middle of a waste, three days from the nearest water, with eight days of grain in a wagon they had to pull themselves and one man on a horse who knew where any of it was.

“Then you want to be at that bridge, and you want to be digging,” he said.

It was the honest answer and it was not much of one, and he did not dress it up.

He got up on the horse and rode away east into the dark.

He smelled Sotha Bal before he could see it, which by now he was used to.

He came in from the northwest in the middle of the morning and stopped on the rise a kilometer out and got the glass on it, and he sat on the horse and looked for a long time.

It was over. It was nearly over. There was still fighting in two places, small and ugly, three or four men at a time in a lane, the kind of thing that happens at the end when there is nothing to hold and people keep swinging anyway.

The wall was gone in three separate runs. Not breached. Taken apart. There were two machines standing back on the flat, big timber things on wheels with counterweights hanging off them, and beside them a heap of round stone the size of a man’s chest, and the ground between the machines and the wall was pocked all over where the shot had come short.

Sotha Bal had built its wall higher with baskets of dirt carried by hand.

Dren had seen the thing they had brought to it.

Through the glass, the difference was obscene. Veyloth men in mail, in helmets, with shields, coming on in ranks with sergeants walking behind the ranks. Sotha Bal men in whatever they owned. He watched a Sotha Bal man run at a shield line with an eight-foot pole and get taken off his feet by three of them and it took about a second and a half.

They had been eating. That was the whole of it, in the end. One side had been eating.

He put the glass away and rode down.

Two of them came at him about three hundred yards out, on foot, from behind a burnt cart where they must have been posted, and they came fast and they came shouting, and Dren had the crossbow up out of the sleeve on the saddle before he had decided anything.

It was the same as it had ever been. Same weight against the shoulder, same catch on the crank, same picture at the end of the bolt. Eight years of mornings at a hide by the tree line, waiting for something to come through and put its head down.

He shot the first man in the face at forty feet.

The man stopped like he had walked into a beam and went down backwards and did not move at all afterward.

And Dren was already cranking, because the thing about a crank is that it does not care what has just happened. Turn and turn and turn. It takes as long as it takes. The second man was coming and the horse was going round underneath him and the crank was at half and it was not going to be finished, and there was nothing left to do with the last two seconds except use his voice.

“I’M FROM DRENOVAR.”

The man did not stop for that. Somebody else did.

“HOLD.”

It came from over by the cart, an older voice with a lot of use in it, and the running man went sideways and stopped with his spear still up and stood there heaving with his eyes going from Dren to the body and back.

Dren finished the crank. He did it slowly and he did it looking at them, and he laid the bolt in, and he did not raise it.

They came up around him then, five or six, and it was bad for a moment. They looked at their man on the ground and they looked at Dren, and one of them said something about him and the older one told him to shut his mouth.

“You shot Kess.”

“He was running at me.”

“He was doing his job.”

“So was I,” Dren said, “and mine involves not being run at.”

Nobody had anything to say to that. Two of them went and stood over the body. One of them crouched down and then stood back up again fairly quickly.

“What is that,” the older one said, meaning the crossbow.

“A bow.”

“That is not a bow.”

“It is a bow with a crank on it.” Dren let it lie across the saddle. “Takes about twenty seconds to wind. You would have had me.”

That, oddly, was the thing that settled them. A weapon that takes twenty seconds to reload is a weapon they understand the limits of, and a man who tells you the limits of his own weapon is not planning anything.

“What is your business here?”

“Slaves,” Dren said. “What is the count?”

And the whole group of them went quiet in a way he had not expected.

“There is no count,” the older one said.

“There is always a count.”

“Not here.” He looked back over his shoulder at the town, and there was smoke coming off it in about nine places, and the noise of the last of it was still going on in the lanes. “There are no prisoners out of Sotha Bal. Not one.”

“Why not.”

“Because they would not stop.”

He said it flatly, and then he kept talking, which Dren had not asked him to do.

“We called it. Twice on the first day and again this morning. Terms and everything, proper terms, put it down and you keep your lives, and my officer went up under a cloth to say it. They shot at the cloth.” He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. “There were old men out there this morning. There was a fellow came at our line with a hoe. A hoe. And you cannot take a man alive who will not have it, you can only stand there and let him kill you or you can do the other thing, and we did the other thing all day.”

One of the younger ones said, “They knew. They knew from the second day it was finished.”

“Then why?”

Nobody answered him. He was about nineteen and he had asked it like he genuinely wanted somebody in that group to tell him, and not one of them could.

Dren sat on his horse above them and something went through him and he was ashamed of it before it had finished going.

Because he knew why. He had stood in that yard six days ago and watched them build it. They had a plan for dying here. Every man on that wall could have told you where he would stand and who was on his left, and not one of them had a plan for leaving, and leaving was the mad idea, and leaving was the one that got you laughed at.

He had told them. He had stood in their street and told them and they had told him to get out of the sun.

And there it was for about half a second, warm and disgusting: I was right.

He put it down as fast as he had ever put anything down in his life.

“Nobody at all,” he said.

“Well.” The older one shrugged. “There is the tavern.”

“What tavern.”

“South side. Big place, mud and sand, no timber in it worth the name, so it will not take a light. Somebody barricaded it up on the first night and it is still barricaded.” He said it without much interest. “There are people in it. We could burn the roof off it if we had to and we have better things to do. They will come out when the water goes.”

Dren rode in through the army.

That was the strangest thing he did that day. Nobody stopped him for a hundred yards at a time. There were men sitting down in rows eating. There was a farrier working. There were two lads carrying a wounded man on a door and arguing about the grip. It was a workplace at the end of a shift.

He got as far as the broken wall before an officer came out and put a hand up.

He was young. Thirty, maybe, clean, with a good coat and a sword that had not been out of the scabbard, and two soldiers behind him who were both older than he was.

“You. What is your business.”

Dren took the warrant out and handed it down.

The officer opened it and read it, and Dren watched him read it, and watched him get to the number and stop.

“This is a transit warrant.”

“It is.”

“For two hundred head.”

“Sealed and paid,” Dren said. “Out of Census and Excise, Veyloth, four days ago. Look at the stamp.”

“I can see the stamp.” He turned it over. “This says the intake is out of Trovgar.”

“Does it.”

“It does.”

Dren got down off the horse.

He did it the slow way, the way that takes a while with a bad leg and a stick, and every second of it the officer had to stand there and wait, and when he was down he walked up into the man’s space and took the warrant out of his fingers.

“Do you know what I have got out there in the wastes?” He was not shouting yet. “I have got a wagon and a hole in the ground where two hundred people are supposed to be. I paid a hundred and fifty-one thousand at that counter. I have a seal on this paper. And every single time your people take a town, my property comes out of it in pieces.”

“Sir, this warrant is not for Sotha Bal stock.”

“IT IS FOR TWO HUNDRED HEAD.” That was the shout, and it went out across the broken wall and men fifty yards away turned round for it. “TWO HUNDRED. THAT IS THE NUMBER. THAT IS WHAT I PAID FOR AND THAT IS WHAT I AM SHORT.” He had the paper up in the man’s face now, one finger on the number. “A hundred and eighty. Where are they? Who is running my floor? Who is turning the glass? Do you have the faintest idea what I have got sitting in Drenovar waiting on this? I have got buyers out east. I have got orders. And I have come out here on the word of your office to find out that your army is incapable of taking a town without killing every useful pair of hands in it.”

“Sir.”

“Do not sir me. Get me your general. I sat down with him under an awning four days ago and I gave him a piece of my own manufacture out of my own hand, and I will tell him to his face what his officers have cost me.”

He was aware, distantly, of one of the two soldiers behind the officer. The older one. He had turned his head slightly to the side and he was looking at nothing at all with an expression of the purest possible enjoyment.

Nobody else moved to stop it. That was what Dren understood while it was happening. There were forty men within earshot and every one of them saw an angry rich stranger in a foreign coat holding a sealed paper and shouting about money, and there is no army in the world where that is anybody’s job to interfere with.

“There is,” the officer said, “the tavern.”

Dren stopped.

“There are people in the tavern on the south side. Twenty or so. We have not gone in.” He was talking quickly now. “If you can get them out of there on their feet, and they are subdued, and they walk out under your warrant, then they are yours and I will sign for it. That is the best I can do.”

“Twenty.”

“Against a hundred and eighty, I understand, but it is twenty more than nothing.” He took a breath. “And I will tell you now. If they come out and run, we are not chasing them into the wastes for you. We are chasing them into the wastes for us, and we will not be bringing them back.”

“Then they will not run,” Dren said.

He walked the horse down through what was left of the town to get there.

He had come up this street six days ago and there had been forty people in it building a wall.

He did not look at very much of it. That was a decision he made at the top of the street and kept. He looked at the road in front of the horse and he looked at where he was going, and he let the rest stay at the edge of his eye, and even at the edge of his eye it was a great deal.

The tavern was where they said it was. A long low building, mud and sand rendered over, one storey, small windows up high. Somebody had done a proper job on it. The door was gone behind timber and stone and what looked like the better part of a cart, driven in tight, and the high windows were blocked with sacking and furniture.

There were four Veyloth men sitting in the shade across from it who had clearly been sitting there for two days.

Dren got down and went up to the barricade and put the flat of his hand on it.

“SHEO,” he called. “ARE YOU IN THERE. IT IS DREN.”

Nothing.

“SHEO. IT IS DREN.”

He could hear it now, through the timber. Movement. A lot of small movement of the kind a lot of people make when they are trying not to.

Then a voice came through the door, and it was not Sheo.

“...Dren?”

It was a woman. It was close to the wood, and it was quiet, and it was steadier than it had any right to be.

“Is that really you, Dren?”

He put his forehead against the timber.

“Arvyna.”

“Oh,” she said. “Oh, it is.”

“It is me.”

“They said you were dead. Sheo made a board.” Her voice did something on the last word and came back. “He would not let anybody take it down.”

“I know. Arvyna, listen to me.”

“Are they still out there.”

“They are. They are across the road and they are not coming in.” He kept his hand flat on the wood. “Listen. You are going to open this and you are going to come out, all of you, and nobody is going to touch you, because I have a paper that says you belong to me.”

A long pause.

“I do not understand.”

“I know you do not.”

“Dren, what have you done?”

“I will explain every bit of it,” he said. “I promise you. But it has to be now, and it has to be all of you on your feet and walking out slow, and nobody runs. If anybody runs they will go after them and I cannot stop that. Do you understand me? Nobody runs.”

He heard her turn away from the door and start talking to people, low and fast, and he heard somebody arguing, and he heard her put a stop to it.

It took a long time to unbuild the barricade. He listened to them do it from the other side, one piece at a time, and the four men across the road stood up and watched with their hands not on anything.

The door came open into the dark.

They came out into the light in a line, blinking, filthy, holding onto each other.

Seven old people. He counted them without meaning to. Two of them had to be carried out between others and one of those was a woman who could not have weighed anything at all.

Eleven children. The oldest about twelve and the youngest being carried.

Two young women, one of them with a butchering knife she had been holding by the door and did not want to let go of, and Dren said her name gently, not knowing it, and said please, and after a while she put it down on the step.

And Arvyna last, because of course she was last.

She had gone grey. She was thinner than she should have been and her hands were cut all over from the barricade and she stood in the doorway of that tavern and looked at him with two days of not sleeping in her face.

Twenty-one people.

That was what was left of Sotha Bal.

Dren stood there and waited.

He kept his eyes on the doorway behind her. He waited through all of them coming out, and then he waited a bit longer, and then he went on waiting after there was no reason to.

He leaned in.

“Where is Sheo,” he said, very quietly.

Arvyna looked at him.

There was nothing in her face at all. Not grief. Grief has a shape. This was the other thing, the one underneath it, the one for when nobody has told you and nobody is going to. She had been in a room with no windows for two days with eleven children and seven old people and a barricade, and out there the whole world had ended in a noise, and she did not know, and there was nobody left to ask.

She shook her head. Once. Small.

Dren nodded.

He turned around and looked at the officer, who had walked down after him and was standing at a distance with his hands behind his back.

“Twenty-one,” Dren called.

“I will sign for twenty-one.”

“I paid for two hundred.” He came a few steps up the road. “I want that understood before I put a name to anything. Twenty-one out of two hundred. I am short a hundred and seventy-nine head and I expect to be made whole.”

The officer had the decency to look uncomfortable.

“I cannot issue against a warrant. That is not mine to do.”

“Then what can you do.”

“I can put in a word of reference at the census.” He said it earnestly. He had thought about it on the walk down. “With my name on it. Stating what you found here and that the shortfall was operational and not your doing. That will carry. That will carry with the assessor, genuinely, I have seen it carry.” He nodded, agreeing with himself. “You have my word.”

Dren looked at him for a moment.

“All right then,” he said. “I am putting my trust in you, officer.”

“You have it.”

The man was pleased. He went off up the road calling for a clerk and a board to write on, and behind him his soldiers were already spreading out again into the lanes, going house by house, making sure of it.

Dren turned back to Arvyna.

“I found Fimmion.”

Her hand came up over her mouth.

“She is alive. She is three days west of here with two hundred people and she is walking, and she is the one leading them, and she was arguing with me about the road last night.” He was smiling and he could not help it. “She is filthy and she is thin and there is nothing wrong with her that food will not fix. She will be at Dry Fork before we are.”

Arvyna made a sound and folded forward and he caught her arm and held her up, and one of the young women came and took her other side, and she got a hand onto Dren’s shoulder and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

“Fimmion,” she said. “Fimmion. Oh, my girl. Oh, thank you. Thank you.”

“She is all right.”

“And Caius?”

Dren did not have anywhere to go.

“He did not make it,” he said.

She did not say anything. She just kept looking at him, waiting, because there is always more and everyone knows there is always more.

“He was on the wall,” Dren said. “At the gate end. That is where they came in and that is where it was worst, and he was up there when it went, and every account of it says the same thing, so it is true.” He put his hand over the top of hers on his shoulder. “He was where the worst of it was. That is not an accident. Nobody ends up at the gate end by accident.”

“Was it,” she said. “Was it.”

“It was quick,” Dren said.

That was the only part he did not know.

She went down. Not far, he had her, but she went, and she made a noise he had heard once before out of himself in a shack in Drenovar, and he got down onto the step with her and put both arms round her and let her do it in the doorway of a tavern in a town that no longer existed.

Over the top of her head he could see the street.

It ran down to the market and the market was black to the ground. There were shapes in it. There were shapes all along the gutter on the near side, and something burning steadily in a doorway that had been burning long enough to have stopped being anything, and the smoke was going up straight because there was no wind at all.

He looked at all of it, properly, for the first time since he had ridden in.

He was not going to find Sheo. That was simply true and he knew it sitting there. There was nothing out in that street he could have put a name to. If they had laid every one of them out in a row and walked him down it he could not have done it, and if he spent four days turning them over he would only end up certain of something he could not be certain of, and he had two hundred people digging in a dead river and twenty-one on a doorstep and eight days of grain.

So he sat on the step and held Sheo’s wife while she came apart, and did not go and look.

They walked out that afternoon.

He put the two smallest children up on the horse and led it, and Arvyna walked at his shoulder, and the seven old people went in the middle with the two young women on either side, and they went out through the broken wall past the machines and the heap of stone and nobody said anything to them at all.

The officer had his clerk hand up a paper as they passed. Twenty-one head, subdued, released to warrant. Dren put it in his coat with the other one.

They filled everything that would hold water before they went, out of the army’s own barrels, because the officer was feeling generous about anything that did not cost him a signature. Skins, two jars off the tavern floor, the cook pot with a cloth tied over the top of it.

They made eight kilometers before dark, which was as much as the old ones had in them, and camped in a fold of ground with a fire going.

There was no food. The wagon was three days west and anything in Sotha Bal a person could eat had been eaten a week ago. What they had was water, and a horse, and each other.

Nobody complained about any of it. They had come out of a barricaded room and there was sky over them.

One of the old men talked most of that night to nobody in particular about a hyena on a spear he had seen at Dry Fork twenty years ago, and could not be persuaded onto any other subject, and Dren let him go.

It took them two more days to cover the rest of it, because of the old ones and because he would not push them, and they came up on the fork late in the afternoon of the second.

He saw the smoke first and his stomach went over, and then he got the glass on it and saw that it was cook fires, a lot of them, spread out and orderly, and around them people sitting in groups on the ground.

The wagon was drawn up at the head of the bridge with its tongue down. The barrels were off it and standing open, and there were lines of washing strung between the wagon and the dead fig tree, which meant somebody had decided that two hundred people were going to be clean.

And there was a line of people going down into the riverbed under the second arch and coming back up out of it.

He watched them do it twice before he understood what he was looking at. Down empty and up full, hand to hand, the whole length of the bank, a chain of them passing pots and skins and the iron cook pot and somebody’s boot up out of a hole in a river that had been dead since before anybody’s grandfather.

There was a heap of dug sand beside it the size of a haystack.

They had been at it for two days.

They were sitting around the fires in the only shape they could make, because a running chain does not let a man sit where he likes. Ten long curves of people laid out around the light, like rope coiled on a deck.

Sixty kilometers out of Veyloth, in irons, hauling that wagon themselves the whole way, on almost nothing. And they had got there first.

Fimmion came out to meet them a long way from the camp, walking fast and then not walking, and Arvyna was already going, going badly, holding her skirt up, making a noise the whole way.

Dren stopped the horse and stayed where he was and let them have it.

He sat up there in the last of the light with two children hanging onto his coat and watched a woman get her daughter back in the middle of a desert, under a bridge with no river beneath it, twenty-six kilometers from a town that had been full of people the week before.

Then he clicked at the horse and brought the rest of them in.

A man asked him that night when the chains were coming off.

He asked it well. He had been down in that riverbed with a mattock for two days and he had the face of a man who thought he had bought the right to the question, and the fires went quiet along the lines waiting on the answer.

Dren had the keys in his coat. He had been carrying them against his chest for four days.

He could have told him the truth, which was that they were two days off a road and anybody who came across two hundred loose people out here would walk them straight back in as strays and there would not be one thing Dren could do about it. He had said exactly that to Fimmion in the dark and he had meant every word of it.

He did not say it.

He was tired, and he had buried a friend he could not find that morning, and he had spent nine years explaining himself to people who went back to work, and somewhere on the road between that tavern and this bridge he had stopped.

“Not yet,” he said.

Then he got up and walked back to the bridge.

It started behind him before he was out of the firelight, low, going down the lines and along them, and he kept walking and did not turn round, and that was the first time in his life he had ever done that to anybody.

Two hundred and twenty-one people. One wagon. One horse. Eight days of grain, a hole in a dead river, and about six hundred kilometers of nothing between here and a beach that only one of them had ever seen.

He sat up on the parapet of the bridge with the last of the light going and looked west.
