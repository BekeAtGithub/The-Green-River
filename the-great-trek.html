The Great Trek

They stayed two days at the bridge, which was one more than Dren wanted and one fewer than the old people needed.

The first night was for the dead.

Arvyna and Fimmion sat up against the wagon wheel with a blanket over the two of them and did not sleep at all. Dren heard it going most of the night, on and off, quiet and then not quiet, and then quiet again for an hour, and then one of them would say a name and it would start over.

In the morning Fimmion came and asked him to help with something, and the two of them went up onto the flat above the channel and built two piles of stones.

One for a man on a wall at the gate end of a market ninety kilometers east. One for a man in a street in a town that no longer existed, who had no body anybody could find, and about whom nobody would ever be able to say a single certain thing.

Arvyna put a board up for Sheo. She did not have a knife that would cut it and she could not write, so it went up blank, and Dren stood there looking at a blank board over an empty pile of stones and thought about a board with his own name on it standing in a town that was ash.

Sheo had carved that one himself. Would not let anybody take it down.

The Sotha Bal people came up and stood around it, all twenty of them, and the two young women held the smaller children, and somebody said some words.

And forty feet away, down the bank, two hundred people sat in ten chained lines in the sand and watched a funeral for people they had never met.

That was the part Dren felt on the back of his neck the whole time.

It started properly that afternoon, and it started the way these things do, with one man who had had enough.

“What is this place.”

Dren was at the wagon checking the barrels.

“Dry Fork.”

“What is Dry Fork.”

“A bridge with no river under it.”

“Then what are we doing at it?”

By then a good many of them had got up, as far as the chain let them, and it was going along the lines, and it had two days of digging in it and four days of walking before that and a market full of their own dead behind all of it.

They wanted to know why a man buys two hundred people and walks them into an empty place and stops.

They wanted to know where the farm was. That was the one that kept coming. A man buys labour because he has ground, and there was no ground here, there was nothing here, there was a dead tree and three rotted tents and a hole in the sand.

They wanted to know if he was going to feed them.

One of them, further down, said it out loud in the way that people say the thing everybody has been thinking. That he had bought two hundred people cheap and marched them out past the last road on the map, and that there is only one reason a man does that.

Dren stood at the wagon with his hand on the rail and listened to the whole of it.

He had it ready. He had had it ready for nine years. There is a coast. It is west. The ground grows food on its own and nobody owns it and I have lived on it for eight years and I have the chart in my coat and I will show it to any one of you who wants to look.

He had said that in a hall in Veyloth to a clerk who laughed at him.

He had said it on a porch in Ral Koda to the best friend he ever had, who told him there was nothing out there.

He had said it in a yard in Sotha Bal to a man building a wall, who put both hands up and asked him to listen to what it sounded like.

He had said it in the street to a woman filling jars, who told him he wanted to get out of the sun.

He had said it standing on a wagon board to these same two hundred people six days ago and it had come out badly and not one of them had believed a word of it, and they had walked anyway, because they were chained and it did not matter what they believed.

That was the whole lesson, in the end. It had taken him nine years and an ocean to learn it and it was very simple.

It did not matter what they believed.

“We leave in the morning,” Dren said. “West. Bring the barrels up full.”

And he walked away from them.

Somebody threw something at his back. It fell short, because a man on a running chain cannot get his arm all the way through the swing.

They went west out of Dry Fork at first light with the wagon in the middle of the column and the horse on a lead and the sun coming up behind two hundred and twenty-one people.

The road west out of that intersection was a track and then a suggestion and then nothing at all.

Dren rode ahead of it most mornings with the needle in his hand and the chart flat on the pommel, and that was the entire plan, and everybody in that column found out inside a week exactly how thin it was.

He had walked this ground once. Alone, nine years younger in the legs than he was now, going the other way, with a month of food on his back and no reason on earth to hurry. He had taken a bearing every single morning of it and he had written down what he found and where he found it, because he had spent eight years on a wrecked ship teaching himself to be a man who wrote things down.

The chart was a piece of the dead lord’s paper with the back of a chart of some other world on the other side of it. On it, in Dren’s own hand, in marks that were not writing because Dren could not write, were forty-odd things.

A stream out of the green. A stream out of the green. A rock like a fist. A stream. Bad ground, go south. A seep at the foot of the range under an overhang, two days from the last stream.

Nobody else could read a line of it. It did not matter. Nobody else could have read it if it had been written properly.

Two hundred and twenty-one people were walking across a desert on the strength of a man’s memory of a walk he took nine years ago, written down in a private alphabet, and every one of them knew it by the fourth day.

The first leg was the killer and Dren had known it would be.

From Dry Fork west to the foot of the range was a day and a half for him alone. It took the column four. From there around the southern end of the mountains was nine days for a man with a full pack, and Dren had put fifteen on it in his head and been wrong about that too.

Twelve or thirteen days of dead country before there was any water he trusted.

The wagon was the thing that nearly finished them in the first week.

Twenty people in front on ropes and twenty behind on the tail, changing over every hour, hauling a four-wheeled wagon across ground that had never had a wheel on it. It bogged in sand and it hung up on rock and twice it went over on a slope and had to be unloaded and righted and loaded again with the sun straight overhead.

On the sixth day a wheel split. They spent a day and a half on it with an axe and a length of the ship’s rope and a piece of the old bridge that Dren had thrown in the back at Dry Fork for no reason he could have explained at the time, and when it was done it was not a wheel so much as a shape that turned, and it turned for the rest of the journey.

The chains were worse than the wagon.

Iron in that sun goes to something you cannot hold. By the third day everybody in that column had their wrists wrapped in whatever they could get, strips of shirt, strips of the sacking off the grain, and the ones who had nothing bled onto the links and then the links stuck to them.

Twenty people on a running chain do not walk at twenty speeds. They walk at the speed of whoever is worst, and when somebody went down, and people went down constantly, the four either side of them stopped whether they wanted to or not, and the line concertinaed and somebody at the back got pulled off their feet.

They learned it. That was the thing Dren watched happen and did not have any part in. Inside a week those lines were moving like something that had been drilled. They called steps on the bad ground. They put the strong ones on the outside ends. They carried the weak in the middle where the chain had slack. When one went down, the whole twenty knelt, all at once, without a word from anybody.

Nobody taught them that. They worked it out because it was that or be dragged.

They came up on Ghor Vhuul on the ninth day.

Dren had planned it that way, because of what was under it.

It came up out of the flat looking like a low grey ridge and then it stopped looking like a ridge. Walls. Whole streets of them, standing to shoulder height, some to the second floor, all of it pale and blown out and half swallowed, and running through the middle of the town a wound in the ground fifty feet across where the hill had come down and taken a third of the place with it.

Nine hundred people had lived here. It had gone in a night, years ago, and what was left of them had walked to West Outpost, and West Outpost could not hold them, and they had gone on to Sotha Bal.

One of the old men from the tavern stopped dead at the top of the road in.

He had been born here. He had walked out of it as a young man with what he could carry, and he had walked to West Outpost, and then to Sotha Bal, and he had been in that tavern for two days with a barricade against the door while an army took the last town that would have him.

And now he was standing at the top of his own street again, with a rope over his shoulder, being marched west by a stranger.

He would not go down into it. He sat on a stone at the top of the road and waited for them, and would not talk about it after.

The cisterns were where Dren remembered.

Three of them, cut down into the rock under what had been the market floor, roofed over, and two were cracked through and dry and the third was not. It was foul. There were things in it. They boiled every drop through cloth over three days and the whole column drank it and about forty of them were sick from it and two of them did not stop being sick.

They filled everything. Barrels, skins, jars, the pot, a dozen clay things picked up off the floors of dead houses.

They buried the first two at Ghor Vhuul.

An old woman off the tavern line who had been carried out of it in the first place, and a man of about thirty from the fourth chain who had come out of Trovgar with something wrong inside him that nobody had looked at.

Dren came down the line with the keys in his hand.

That was how it worked, and it kept working that way, and it was the thing about that trek that he never got used to and never stopped dreaming about.

When somebody died, they were still on the chain.

So the whole line stopped, and the whole line waited, and Dren walked the length of it with the wire loop out and found the right lock and knelt down in the sand and opened it and passed the running chain back through the ring on a dead person’s wrist, one link at a time, with nineteen people standing over him watching him do it.

Then he shut the lock again on the ones who were still alive.

Every time.

They went around the southern end of the range in a great slow arc, and that was where it turned bad in earnest.

The range came down out of the north like a wall of broken teeth, grey and bare, and where it met the green at the far end the two of them went into each other and there was no way through and never had been. So they walked around it, with the mountains up on the right, day after day, on flat pale ground that gave back the heat all night.

Dren had it at nine days. It took them fourteen.

He cut the ration on the third day of it and again on the seventh.

The grain went. Of course the grain went. Eight days of grain for two hundred and twenty-one people is eight days no matter how you cut it, and after that it was whatever came off the land, and the land was the wastes.

They ate lizards. They ate a snake and fought about it. There was a bird that nested in the rock at the foot of the range and one of the Trovgar men could take them with a stone, and they got eleven birds out of that range over five days and it was the best day anybody had.

They ate the horse on the eleventh day.

Dren did it himself, out of sight, up in the rocks, and he stood with his hand on its neck for a while first, and he was not sentimental about animals and it took him longer than it should have.

It fed two hundred people for one day and a bit.

He walked after that, like everybody else, and that was the end of him riding out ahead in the mornings with the chart on the pommel, and it slowed the column by a good deal, and it also did something to the way the column looked at him that he had not expected and did not deserve.

The sandstorm came on the second week of the arc.

They saw it two hours out, a brown wall standing up along the whole southern horizon and coming on without any hurry at all, and there was nowhere to go and nothing to get behind.

They put the wagon broadside and got everybody down on the lee of it in the lines, and then they lay in the sand with cloth over their faces and their arms over the cloth for most of a day and a night while it came over the top of them.

You cannot chain two hundred people and then tell them to scatter. That was the thing Dren lay there and thought about with his face in the dirt and a chain of twenty people to his left going nowhere. They had to lie in it. All of them, in rows, in the exact place they happened to be standing when it hit, for eighteen hours.

Four died in the storm. Two were old and two were not, and one of the ones who was not was a girl of about nine off the Sotha Bal group who had been walking beside Arvyna the whole way and had got separated from her in the last minutes before it landed.

They dug them out in the morning. The wagon was buried to the axle on the windward side and half the water had gone gritty and there was a line of people lying in the sand who did not get up when the rest did.

That was the morning Arvyna came and found him.

He was at the wagon with the chart out, working out how far back the storm had put them, and she stood in front of him until he looked up.

“Tell them where we are going.”

“They know where we are going.”

“They do not know anything. They think you are a madman who bought them to walk them to death and half of them have stopped thinking anything at all.” Her voice was not raised. That was the trouble with it. “Adia died last night eleven feet from me and I could not get to her because there were nineteen people on her chain and I had to lie there and listen. So you will stand up in front of them today and you will tell them what this is for.”

“I have told them.”

“Then tell them again.”

“I told them at the gate.” He put the chart down. “I told them at the bridge. I told your husband in his own yard with an army on the road and he asked me to listen to what I sounded like, and I told a clerk in Veyloth nine years ago and he laughed until I left the building, and I told a woman in a street who told me to get out of the sun.” He was not shouting either. “Nobody has ever once done anything because I told them. Not one person, in nine years. They do things because they are made to.”

Arvyna looked at him for a long moment.

“Do you know who you sound like,” she said.

“Say it.”

“You sound like the men who sold them to you.”

Dren did not have anything for that.

“Sheo would not know you,” she said, and she went back to the lines.

He stood at the wagon a while.

Then he rolled the chart up and put it in his coat and gave the order to move, and he did not go and speak to them, and he knew exactly what he was doing and he did it anyway.

That was the worst thing he ever found out about himself, and he found it out at the foot of a mountain range with thirty-one days still to walk.

The seep saved them and it very nearly did not.

It was the last mark before the green. He had put it on the chart nine years ago at the foot of the range under an overhang of rock, and he had drunk out of it himself and filled everything he had, and it was two days past the last of the water they were carrying.

They came up on it on the afternoon of the second day with about four hours of drinkable water left in the whole column.

It was dry.

Not low. Dry. A stain down the rock and a bowl of cracked mud at the bottom of it with the crack pattern gone white, and it had been like that for a long time, and there was no version of standing in front of it that made it be anything else.

Dren went down on his knees in front of a dry rock in front of two hundred people.

He heard it start behind him. He had heard it start at the bridge and he knew the sound of it now.

And then he stopped listening to it, and put his hand flat on the mud, and thought about a hole under the second arch of a bridge at Dry Fork.

He got up and turned round and told them to bring the mattocks.

They dug at the foot of that rock for eleven hours, in shifts, in the dark, by firelight, twenty people at a time going at a slope of stone and compacted grit with four mattocks, an axe, a maul, and a great many hands.

They went down about nine feet.

It came in slow and it came in filthy and by dawn there was standing water at the bottom of a hole that two hundred people had dug with almost nothing, and it filled about as fast as they could take it out, and they stayed at that rock for three days drinking it dry and letting it come back.

The water had not gone. The water had gone down. Nine years is a long time in a dying country.

Dren sat up on the overhang the second night and looked at the fires below him and did the arithmetic that he had been avoiding for a week.

They had left Dry Fork with two hundred and twenty-one.

They saw the green on the thirty-fourth day.

It came up as a dark line along the whole northern horizon, low as a fingernail, and it was not low, and every single person in that column understood what it was before anybody said the word.

It was in the air first, actually. That was what Dren noticed. Two days before you could see it there was something different in the wind at night, something wet and green and rotten, coming down off the north over ninety kilometers of dead ground, and he lay in his coat and smelled it and was nine years younger and up to his shins in black water.

The column got very quiet about it.

It took three more days of walking for the line to become a wall, and every one of those days it got taller, and the talk went round and round the lines and came back to Dren through Fimmion, who was the only person still bringing him things.

“They think you are taking us in.”

“We are not going in.”

“I know that. I am telling you what they think.” She walked beside him a while. “There are men on the fourth chain saying you bought us for the jungle. That there is something in there that wants people and you are the one who fetches them.”

“And what do you say?”

“I say you would not have made us dig for eleven hours if you were going to feed us to something.”

“That is not much of a defence.”

“It is what I have got,” Fimmion said.

He told them, in the end, because it was the one thing he could tell them that they would believe inside a week.

He stood up on the wagon board with the wall a day off his right shoulder, a hundred floors of it going up out of the flat and running east and west past the ends of the world, and he said: we are not going in. We are going along it. We walk beside that for about three weeks with it on our right hand the whole way and then it ends, and past where it ends there is water and after the water there is the sea.

Nobody believed the part about the sea.

They believed the part about not going in, because the next morning they turned west with the wall on their right, and the morning after that they were still turning west, and after four days of walking beside the most frightening thing any of them had ever seen and not once turning toward it, a thing settled in that column that no speech would have done.

He had been right about the jungle leaking.

Every few days a stream came out of the green and ran twenty or thirty yards into the sand and died there, and they filled everything every time and drank until it hurt and washed, and for the first time since Dry Fork water stopped being the thing that was going to kill them.

Food came off the edge, too. Not much and not safely. There was game that came down out of the trees at dusk to those same streams, and Dren took four things with the crossbow in three weeks and each one was a festival. There was green at the boundary that he knew and a great deal that he did not, and he was very hard about that, because he had watched what he ate for a week at a time on a beach before he would trust it and he was not going to guess with two hundred people.

It was still not enough. Nobody on that trek was ever not hungry.

But the dying slowed right down along the seam, and that was the first stretch where a day went by and nobody used the keys.

The insects had them the whole way.

Twenty or thirty yards off the green there was nothing much. Fifty yards in, at a stream, at dusk, there was everything, and two hundred people at a watering point is a thing the jungle had never been offered before.

They were bitten to pieces. Faces swelled. Eyes closed up. Some of them got the shakes off it afterwards and two never came right.

And Dren walked through it.

That was the thing they noticed and he did not notice them noticing for a long time. He stood in the middle of a cloud of it at the water’s edge with his sleeves down and a cloth round his neck, filling barrels, and he did not swat, and he did not flinch, and when a hornet came at his forearm he looked at it the way a man looks at a fly on bread.

He was aware of it, distantly, the way you are aware of weather.

It was nothing. That was the whole of it. It was a bad hour beside a stream and then you walked away from it, and he had done a month of it in the dark with no bottom to the water and no end to the trees and nowhere to lie down, and there was not one thing this place had left that could reach him.

One of the Trovgar men asked him once, straight out, how he did it.

Dren thought about it and said, “I already went in.”

Somebody asked him what the river was called.

They had come to the biggest one on the chart, three weeks along the seam, a real river coming out of the green and running west and holding together instead of dying in the sand, and they followed it for four days because it was going where they were going.

“I do not know,” Dren said.

“You have a mark for it.”

“I have a mark for a river. That is not the same as knowing what it is called.” He looked at it a while. “There is a river nine years east of here that people call the Green River. I walked into it and I was carried a long way in the dark and I came out somewhere else. I have never once been able to tell you if it was this water or not.”

The man waited for more.

“You can call it the Green River if you want,” Dren said. “Everything here is going to need a name off somebody.”

The green ended on the fifty-eighth day.

It came down in stages the way it had for him, a hundred floors to fifty to twenty, then scrub, then a line, and past the line the ground went open and pale and stayed that way, and the column came out from under the last of it and stood in the flat looking at nothing much at all.

Dren sat down on the boundary with one hand back in the shade and the other flat on the hot ground, the way he had the first time.

Nobody understood why he was doing it and he did not explain.

They turned north.

And two days after that, the arguing about the sky started.

It began with a man on the second chain who had good eyes, and it went round the lines for three days and got worse instead of better, and it was, Dren thought, the single most reasonable thing anybody on that trek ever argued about.

The land ran out ahead of them.

You could see it doing it. The ground came along, pale and flat and ordinary, and then at a certain distance it simply stopped, and past where it stopped there was blue, and the blue went all the way up, and it went left and right as far as the eye could take it, and there was nothing in it. No hill. No dust. No far edge.

It was sky. Obviously it was sky. Sky is the blue thing.

So the ground ended and the sky started, and there were people in that column who had walked fifty-eight days and buried thirty-odd and dug a hole nine feet into rock, who now stood in a line at dusk looking at the western horizon and quietly worked out that the old man in the foreign coat was walking them off the edge of the world.

Fimmion brought him that one too.

“They want to know what happens when the ground stops.”

“It does not stop.”

“I have told them that.”

“Tell them again.”

“Dren.” She stopped walking. “Tell them what it is.”

And he thought about it, and about a man lying face down in white sand with a leg going green, laughing at the sky at the end of the world, absolutely certain he had crossed the whole thing and come out into another Ral Koda.

It had taken a leaning shape and a moving edge and about ten minutes of the worst confusion of his life to get it.

“No,” he said. “They can have it the way I got it.”

“That is cruel.”

“It is the one good thing I have got left to give anybody,” Dren said, “and I am not going to spend it on a sentence.”

They came over the last of the ground on the sixty-first day, in the late afternoon, with the sun going down in front of them.

The column stopped without anybody calling a halt.

It stopped in pieces, chain by chain, as each line came up over the rise, and then two hundred people stood along the top of a low sand ridge in absolute silence and looked at the Turquoise Coast with the light on it.

The tide was most of the way out. There was a mile of wet sand shining like a floor, and the bay behind it going green and then blue and then dark blue in bands out to the point, and the whole of it moving, coming in and sliding back and coming in, and the sound of it arriving up the beach at them a moment late.

And it did not stop. That was the thing. They stood there and waited for it to stop and it went on out and out and out to a line so far away that it was not a place, it was just where the looking gave up.

Somebody at the front said, “That is water.”

Nobody answered him.

They went down onto the beach in the last of the light and the chains came off nothing, and they walked out over that wet sand in ten long lines with the whole sky burning behind them, and Dren stood up on the ridge and let them go on ahead of him and watched two hundred people who had been dying for two months walk into the shallows in irons and start shouting.

Most of them drank it.

He had known they would. He had let them.

He came down the beach through the noise of two hundred people finding out about salt water and did not say one word about it to anybody.

They made fire on the sand that night above the tide line, a lot of fires, and it was the first night since Veyloth that the column did not lie down in its lines and go straight to sleep.

Dren went up into the trees at the top of the beach with the sword and came back down with his coat full.

Hairy brown balls off the tall trees with the bare curved trunks. A great bunch of yellow fingers on a stem that took two people to carry. A spiked knob out of a nest of blades that looked like it would take your hand off.

He sat down at a fire and put them in the sand in front of him, and about forty people came and stood in a ring to watch a man cut open a thing none of them had ever seen.

He did the hairy one first, because he knew how it went now, and when the shell cracked and the water came out over his hands he got a cup under it and passed it round, and there was a lot of noise about that.

Then the yellow ones, and he showed them the trick of it, that the outside is bitter and awful and the inside is not.

Then the spiked knob, and that was the one that did it. That was the one where a woman on the third chain sat down in the sand and put her hands over her face.

They asked him about his coat.

He took it off and gave it to them and it went round the fire and came back, and he let them have the shirt too, and the boots, and they turned the linen over and over and rubbed it between their fingers and could not work out how it had been made, and neither could he.

He got the crossbow out and let them wind it. He got the glass out and put it on the moon and had to sit there for an hour while every person at that fire looked through it and then looked at the back of it and then looked through it again.

And he tried to tell them about the ship.

He had been dreading this part for two months and it went about as well as he expected. He said there was a thing up the coast, made of wood, bigger than the biggest building any of them had ever stood in, and it had come across that water with people in it from somewhere that is not on any map anybody here has ever seen, and it had been shot at with iron and it had come apart and washed up here, and he had lived in it for eight years.

They listened to the whole thing very politely.

“It is up the coast,” Dren said. “Three days. You will see it.”

And that was fine. Nobody argued and nobody believed him and it did not matter in the slightest, because in three days there would be a ship.

The walk north up that beach was, for everybody except Dren, the best week of their lives, and for Dren it was the second time.

Firm wet sand at the tide line the whole way. Flat. No thorns and nothing overhead and the wind coming off the water all day. The chains still on, so the ten lines went along the beach together in ten long curves with the sea on their left, and it turned out that a chained line of twenty people is a very good thing to have when you are learning to fish with a net.

The tide taught them.

It went out on the second morning and left a mile of shining ground with things living in it, and Dren showed them the holes and what makes them, and two hundred people went out over the flats on their hands and knees in ten chained lines digging clams out of the sand with their fingers.

They got crabs. They got something with a shell and claws that fought back and that one of the old women knew what to do with because it was not so different from something in a lake she had known sixty years ago. They got a fish trap working out of the folded net and the rocks at the point.

They ate, on that beach, for the first time since Trovgar, until they were full.

The mood turned all the way over in about four days. Dren watched it happen and had nothing to do with it.

What did it was not the food. It was the fruit trees going past on their right, one after another after another, day after day, all of them loaded, none of them belonging to anybody. You can be told that a country has food in it. Walking beside eighty kilometers of it is a different argument.

On the fourth day, somebody sang.

They came up on the ship on the seventh.

Dren saw it first, from a long way off, because he knew exactly where to look, and he stopped walking and did not say anything and let the column come up level with him.

It was still leaning. Still exactly where it had come to rest, black against all that pale, the masts down and the hull up out of the water at the bow end and the whole enormous thing sitting in the shallows like something that had been dropped.

The lines stopped one at a time, all the way back down the beach.

Then two hundred people started walking again, faster, without being told to, and the ten chains came up that beach at a pace nobody had managed since Dry Fork.

They stood in the shallows around it and looked up.

Nobody said very much at all. One of the old men from Ghor Vhuul, who had built houses his whole life, went and put both hands flat on the hull and stood there with his head back.

“Who made this,” he said.

“I have no idea,” Dren said.

“Where did they go?”

“I buried the ones I found.”

Dren let them have two days.

He took them through the hole in the hull in groups and showed them the hold and the barrels and the deck of iron tubes, and he watched a hundred and eighty people fail to understand cannons exactly the way he had, and he took the ones who wanted it up to the cabin with the windows and the silk, and there were people who cried in that room and could not have told you why.

Then on the third morning he got them all together on the sand and told them there was one more thing.

“One task,” he said. “One. And when it is finished I will take the irons off every one of you and you can start.”

He pointed at the ship.

“We are going to bring her out of the water.”

They thought he had lost his mind, and they were nearly right.

He had been working on it for eight years without ever once admitting that was what he was doing. Every storm that came through the wet season, he had lain in that cabin listening to her grind on the sand and known that one of these years the sea would take the rest of her apart, and there was not a thing one man could do about it.

He had a hundred and eighty now.

They spent six days stripping her.

Everything out. Every barrel and crate and cask up out of the hold and along the deck and down through the hole and up the beach above the tide line. The iron balls, all of them, hand to hand in a chain. The powder casks, carried by four people who had been told exactly nothing about what was in them and treated them with enormous respect anyway because Dren did.

The tubes were the problem. Twelve of them, each one longer than a man and thicker through the body, and they got eleven out over four days with rollers cut from the trees and ropes and forty people on each, and the twelfth had gone through the deck years ago and Dren left it where it was.

Then the rigging. Every block, every tackle, every foot of line still worth anything, unrove and coiled and laid out on the sand, and Dren went along it working out what he had, because the ship was going to be pulled out of the water by her own gear.

He had watched that tide for eight years.

He had counted it in the dark in the hold hanging in the netting waiting to drown, and then he had counted it because it was the only thing in the world that happened on a schedule, and then he had counted it for so long that he knew things about it he had never had anybody to tell.

It was not the same every day. It went in a cycle, and about twice a month it came up higher than usual and went out lower than usual, and in the wet season those big ones were bigger again.

He had the day. He had known the day for two weeks. He had been walking them up that beach against it.

They dug for three days.

A slipway, out of the wet sand ahead of the bow, thirty feet wide and going up the beach above the high water, and they laid it with rollers, and they laid the rollers with the last of the gold stuff out of the crocks and every scrap of tallow and fat in the camp.

They set the tackles to the six biggest trees at the top of the beach, doubled, and Dren spent an entire day on that and would not be hurried, because he had learned about mechanical advantage from a crank on a bow and then from a hoop of barrel iron and then from eight years of being one man alone with heavy things.

Six purchases. A hundred and eighty people on the falls.

They came on at the top of the water in the afternoon.

The sea did the lifting. That was the part Dren could not make any of them understand beforehand and did not have to explain afterwards, because they felt it. The tide came up over the flats and up the slipway and got under her and she moved, on her own, an inch, and a sound came out of that hull that stopped every person on that beach.

Then they hauled.

It took a bit under two hours and it was the loudest two hours of Dren’s life. A hundred and eighty people on six lines, chained in their tens and twenties so that a whole chain came back on the fall together, going at it in time because one of the women from Trovgar had started a count on the second pull and never stopped it. The blocks screaming. The rollers going down into the sand and coming up. Somebody down and up again. And the ship coming, a foot at a time, then two, out of the water she had been sitting in since before any of them were born.

The tide turned under her at the top and started going out and they kept hauling, and that was the hour that hurt.

They put her up above the high water line by the length of herself, and when the last of it was done she went over onto her side properly and settled into the sand with her whole flank open to the beach and her keel to the sea, and a hundred and eighty people fell over where they stood and lay in the sand laughing.

She was out.

Dren did the chains that evening.

He went down the ten lines with the wire loop in one hand and a torch in the other and opened every lock himself, and it took him well over an hour, because the locks had been in salt and sand for sixty-eight days and his hands were not what they had been.

He said the same thing at every one of them.

“I am sorry it was this long.”

Nobody cheered. What people did was hold their own wrist. He saw it all the way down the line, over and over, a person getting their hand back and then taking hold of the place where the iron had been and standing there like that.

He had the chains coiled and put up inside the hull, out of the weather.

He did not say why and nobody asked him.

Then he got up on a barrel with the ship behind him and the fires going and said the last thing he had.

“You can go home.”

He let that sit until it had gone all the way to the back.

“I mean it. You know the road now. You walked every step of it and you know where the water is, and there is more food on this beach than you could carry, and if you want to go, we will load you and you can go, and nobody here will stop you or think anything of you for it.”

He looked at them.

“And I will tell you what is at the other end, because I have seen it and you have not. Trovgar is ash. I walked it for a day and I counted. Sotha Bal is worse and there is nobody in it. West Outpost is finished and everybody knew it before this started. Ghor Vhuul you saw for yourselves, and we drank out of it.” He stopped for a second. “Everybody you knew is either dead or standing on this beach.”

Nobody moved.

He had made room in his head for a few. He had worked out on the walk north what he could spare them, how many skins and how much of the dried fish, and he had decided well in advance that he was not going to argue with a single one of them about it.

Not one.

He stood up there and watched it go round the fires, and it was not him they were looking at. They were looking at each other. Up and down the same ten groups of twenty they had been locked into since Veyloth, checking faces, counting.

And there was the answer, and it had been in front of him for sixty-eight days.

They were Trovgar. They had come off the same streets and out of the same market, and they had watched the same wall come in, and they had gone into the same pens. Then they had walked five hundred and sixty kilometers on one chain. They knelt together every time one of them went down, because there was no other way to do it. They lay in a row through a sandstorm for eighteen hours in whatever place they happened to be standing. They put the strong ones on the ends and carried the weak in the middle where the slack was, and they went nine feet into rock in the dark with four mattocks, and every time somebody stopped walking, nineteen of them stood over Dren while he knelt in the sand and took that person off the line.

Whatever a family actually is, once you have taken the houses and the town and the names off it, that was the thing standing on that beach.

Going home meant walking sixty days back to a burnt market in order to be the only person in it.

And the twenty out of Sotha Bal had worked it out a long time before any of this. They had come out of a barricaded room into a street with nothing left upright in it, and they had not needed telling twice.

“So I would say we stay,” Dren said. “I would say we start here.”

He put his hand back at the hull behind him.

“There is your first building. There is fresh water at the river and there is fish in that bay and the trees will feed us while we learn the rest of it, and there is seed grain in the wagon that has come five hundred and sixty kilometers without being eaten, which is a thing I want somebody to remember.”

Somebody down at the front asked what it was called.

“It is not called anything,” Dren said. “Nobody has ever named it. There has never been anybody here to do it.”

Somebody said it should be named after him.

“No,” Dren said, and he said it fast, and it came out harder than he meant it to.

He stood up there a while.

He had never put a board up. That was the thing that got him, standing on a barrel in front of a hundred and eighty people with the sun gone and the fires up. He had put a board over eight strangers out of a hold and stood at the end of the row and said he was sorry. He had built two piles of stones at Dry Fork for Sheo and for Caius. He had walked past a hundred graves on the north road out of Drenovar with names carved into them.

He had never once done it for his own son. They had put James in a hole outside Drenovar with a few hundred other people and Dren had sat in his shack for a week and then walked into a jungle, and nine years later there was still nothing anywhere in the world with that boy’s name on it.

“James,” he said.

It came out quieter than the rest of it and he had to say it again.

“James Beach. My son. He was twenty-six and he sold vegetables and he was better than me.” He looked out at the dark where the water was. “He never saw anything like this. He never got anything. So it is his.”

And that was the name.

They put a board up the next morning at the top of the beach above the tide line, and Fimmion cut the letters into it, because Fimmion could write, and it turned out she was the only one of the whole two hundred and twenty-one who could.

Dren stood and watched her do it and could not read a single mark she made.
