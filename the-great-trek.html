The Great Trek

The column had spent two days at Dry Fork by the time Dren came in from Sotha Bal. Fimmion had kept them digging under the bridge while he was gone. They'd already walked about four days from Veyloth. Dren gave them one more night.

The night was for the dead.

Arvyna and Fimmion sat against the wagon wheel under one blanket. They'd go quiet for a while, then one of them would say Caius or Sheo and the other would start again. Dren heard them until near dawn.

At first light Fimmion asked him to help carry stones onto the flat above the channel. They made one pile for Caius, who'd last been seen on the wall at the gate end of Trovgar, and another for Sheo, who was missing somewhere in the wreck of Sotha Bal.

Arvyna set a board over Sheo's stones and left it blank. Fimmion could write. She'd kept market accounts for years. She wouldn't put her father's name over an empty grave while none of them knew he was dead, and Arvyna wouldn't ask her to.

Sheo had carved Dren's board himself nine years earlier. He hadn't let anybody take it down.

The twenty-one people who'd come out of the tavern stood free around the stones. The younger women held the smallest children. Farther down the bank, the two hundred people taken from Trovgar sat on ten running chains and watched Fimmion mourn two men most of them had never met.

Dren stayed by the wagon. He could feel their eyes on him while he checked the water barrels for the third time.

One of the Trovgar men called over, “What is this place?”

“Dry Fork.”

“What is Dry Fork?”

“A bridge with no river under it.”

“Then why are we here?”

People got up as far as the iron allowed. They'd pulled the wagon for four days, dug for two, and left a market full of their dead behind them. They wanted to know where Dren's farm was, why there was no house, and why a man would buy two hundred people to take them past the last road.

Someone asked if he meant to feed them. Someone else said there was only one use for prisoners marched where witnesses couldn't follow.

Dren put both hands on the wagon rail and waited until they ran out of breath.

“There's a coast west of here,” he said. “Fresh water. Fish. Ground that grows food without anybody praying over it. I lived there eight years, and I walked the road back. I'll show the chart to anyone who wants it.”

“Can anyone but you read it?” the first man asked.

“No.”

“I can read,” Fimmion said from the third line.

Dren took the rolled sheet from his coat and handed it to her. She opened it over the wagon bed. On the back of a foreign sea chart he'd drawn bent lines, circles, bird tracks, and a shape like a fist. None of them were letters.

“This isn't writing,” she said.

“I know. That hook means a bad slope. Three cuts means water under stone. I made marks I'd remember.”

“So you were wrong. I can read. I just can't read you.”

A few people laughed. It wasn't much, but the sound loosened something in the camp.

Dren pointed west. “We leave this morning. When we reach safe fresh water at the coast, the Trovgar irons come off. That's the promise.”

“Why not now?”

He looked toward the road. “Because Veyloth still has patrols and a paper saying you belong to me. Out there they'd call you strays and take you back. At the coast there won't be anybody to hand you to.”

They left Dry Fork after sunrise. Day zero, Dren called it when Fimmion asked where to begin her tally. Two hundred chained evacuees from the Trovgar lot, twenty-one free survivors from the Sotha Bal tavern, and Dren walking outside the count.

Fimmion wrote the two numbers on the blank back of a foreign sheet. She kept them separate from then on.

Every evacuee carried a skin, though some had more patches than hide. The three wagon barrels were the reserve. Dren wouldn't open one while there was water left in skins, and he wouldn't let anyone drink straight from a hole until they'd settled, strained, and boiled what they could.

The food grain had started in Veyloth as roughly thirteen days at trek ration. The four walking days and two digging days had used about five. Eight remained in the food sacks. Three smaller sacks of seed sat apart under canvas, their mouths sewn and a blue strip tied around each one so nobody could mistake them for dinner.

The west road became wheel ruts, then one rut, then bare ground. Dren rode ahead with the needle flat in his palm and his private chart on the pommel. He'd crossed this country once in the other direction, alone and nine years younger. Memory and forty-odd scratches on paper were what he had.

The water marks held. A road cistern on Day Two. A bitter soak on Day Four that took six hours to clear. A tiled tank under a fallen roof on Day Six. A rock catchment on Day Eight. No leg was longer than two days, but every source was lower than Dren remembered, and the barrels grew lighter even while he tried to save them.

Every line chose someone to mind its skins. At a stop those ten people went first, set the containers in order, and counted cups while the rest waited in shade if there was any. The barrels came off the wagon only when a source failed to give enough. On Day Four the soak kept slumping into itself, so people cut the bottoms from cracked jars and sank them as collars while others skimmed sand. They got drinkable water before dark, barely.

The wagon nearly broke the trek before thirst did. Rope teams hauled from the front while others kept the tail from slewing. It sank to the hubs in soft sand, hung its belly on stone, and went over twice. Each time they unloaded every sack and barrel in the heat, righted it, and loaded it again.

On Day Six a wheel split. They lost most of a day shaping a brace from bridge timber Dren had thrown aboard at Dry Fork. Rope held the rim together. What came out wasn't much like a wheel, but it turned.

The ten chained lines no longer looked alike by then. Fast people shortened their steps. Strong people worked toward the ends. When someone fell, those nearest called it and the line went down together before the iron could drag anybody. The free Sotha walkers carried children between the lines and took turns at the wagon ropes.

No one had ordered that arrangement. It was simply how they kept one another upright.

Ghor Vhuul rose out of the flat on Day Nine. At first it looked like a gray ridge. Then windows appeared in it, and streets, and the broad scar where Mount Gor Luut had come down nine years before and buried a third of the town.

One of the old men from the tavern stopped at the road. He'd been born there. After the slide he'd carried what he could to West Outpost, then gone on to Sotha Bal when the outpost couldn't keep all of them. Now he stood above his own street again with a wagon rope over his shoulder.

He sat on a stone and refused to go farther. Nobody pressed him.

The next morning he was still there when the smell of boiled pulse drifted uphill. He stood, looked a long time at the scar across town, then walked down his old street alone. Dren saw him stop where the brewery wall had been and begin counting doorways that no longer existed.

The market cisterns were where Dren remembered. Two had cracked empty. The third held black water, beetles, and something dead at the bottom that they brought up with a hooked pole.

They skimmed it, let the grit settle in jars, poured the clearer water through boiled cloth and charcoal, then boiled it again. The fires ran all night and the next day. Even treated, it tasted of old stone and rot, but it filled every skin and all three barrels.

An hour later the old man came back shouting for mattocks and led them to a wall beside the slide. Behind it was a granary built into the dry side of the hill, its door sealed by fallen stone.

They opened it by noon. The outer sacks had rotted or burst. Farther in, grain and dried pulses still lay waist-deep in bins. Weevils moved through it and mold had taken the top handspan, but the rest could be winnowed, roasted, and boiled. At thin ration it was about two weeks of food.

The old man sat in the doorway while they worked. He remembered hauling sacks into that room before the slide and had assumed the hill crushed it with everything else. When the first pot came off the fire, he ate three spoonfuls, handed the bowl to a child, and went back for another load.

The free Sotha group buried one of its own at Ghor, the old tavern woman who'd been carried out of the barricade. She'd never worn Dren's iron. No key came near her.

The second death was a Trovgar man from the fourth line. Something inside him had been wrong before Veyloth and finally quit. His line stopped. Dren opened the end lock and fed the running chain back through the dead man's wrist ring while the others held it off the ground. Then he locked the shortened line again.

Fimmion changed her sheet that evening: one hundred ninety-nine from the Trovgar lot, twenty from the free Sotha party. Two dead. Two hundred nineteen evacuees still walking.

They left Ghor on the morning of Day Twelve and made one continuous turn around the southern end of Mount Gor Luut. The gray range stayed on their right while they bent south, west, then north toward the jungle seam.

Dren had done it alone in nine days. He allowed the column fourteen. By the time they cleared the last broken shoulder, they'd used seventeen.

There was water on the arc, though none of it was generous. A spring under a ledge on Day Fourteen gave one cup at a time. They chipped mud from a rock basin on Day Seventeen and found an old herders' tank on Day Twenty. The barrels covered each gap and were nearly empty when they reached the next source.

The Ghor food took work. They winnowed insects from the grain in blankets, scraped mold away, and boiled the pulses twice. It still loosened half the camp's bowels. A ration that looked decent in a bowl came back out of some people before dark.

The sandstorm struck on Day Twenty-One. They saw a brown front two hours south, set the wagon broadside, wrapped faces, and lay down behind it.

The free Sotha party could have spread out. They stayed near the lines to hold cloth over children and help keep the chains from tangling. The Trovgar captives had no choice about formation. Ten shortened lines lay in rows wherever the iron let them lie and took sand for most of a day and a night.

Four people died. Two were old people from the free Sotha group. Adia, nine years old and free like them, lost Arvyna's hand when the first wind knocked everyone flat. She was eleven feet away, but the air was dirt and Arvyna couldn't crawl into it. A Trovgar woman in the seventh line was dead when they uncovered her.

Fimmion's count after the burials was one hundred ninety-eight from the Trovgar lot and seventeen from the free Sotha party. Six dead since Dry Fork. Two hundred fifteen remained.

Arvyna found Dren at the wagon with the chart open.

“Tell them where we're going.”

“They know.”

“They know you point west. Adia died beside me and I couldn't reach her. Those people had to lie in rows because you keep them in iron, and you're checking scratches on paper.” Her voice stayed level. “Stand up and tell them what any of this is for.”

“I've told them.”

“Tell them again.”

“I told them at Veyloth. I told them at the bridge. I told Sheo in his yard and he asked me to hear how it sounded.” Dren folded the chart harder than he needed to. “I've spent nine years telling people. They don't move because I speak. They move when they're made to.”

Arvyna watched him for a moment.

“You sound like the men who sold them.”

He had no answer.

“Sheo wouldn't know you,” she said, and went back to the dead.

Dren waited until Arvyna reached the burial line, then called the column up and moved them on.

They caught brown storm runoff in every pot on Day Twenty-Two. Two narrow gullies farther around kept the skins going.

The three seed sacks rode in plain sight under their blue ties. On Day Twenty-Four, when the Ghor ration had become a handful of pulse and husk, a Trovgar man put his finger against one.

“That is grain.”

“Seed,” Dren said.

“Seed is grain to a man who's dying.”

Dren started to tell him what a coast without seed would mean. Arvyna cut him off.

“Don't preach over food you aren't chained beside. We keep it while people can still stand. When they can't, we open one.”

Fimmion carried the question down all ten lines and through the free camp. Nobody argued. The sacks stayed sewn for the moment.

By the end of Day Twenty-Four, five more Trovgar captives had died. Fimmion named each one on her sheet and kept the two columns separate.

The horse went on Day Twenty-Five.

Dren led it up among the rocks and did it himself. He stood with a hand against its neck first. He'd never been soft about animals, but the knife took a while to move.

They boiled what wouldn't roast and cut the rest into strips. More than two hundred people got one real meal and something to chew the next day. Dren walked from then on, which slowed them. Nobody complained about losing the rider.

On Day Twenty-Seven, three people couldn't rise at dawn. Arvyna looked at Dren. He opened one seed sack without making her ask. They cooked it all. Two sacks remained under the canvas with their blue ties, smaller now in a wagon that was otherwise going bare.

By then the wagon carried more sick people than food. Every morning brought an argument over room. A fever case could ride, a person with split feet could ride until noon, and anyone still able to curse Dren had to walk. People traded places without asking him. When a chain reached the wagon, the riders climbed down and the weakest in that line climbed up.

The Ghor sickness kept working through the camp. A man slipped on shale and broke his neck. Heat, infected feet, and hunger took eleven more before the last water mark.

They reached that last mark on the afternoon of Day Twenty-Nine. The barrels held about four hours of drinking water.

Dren had drawn three cuts for a seep under an overhang. Nine years earlier he'd filled both his casks there.

It was dry.

Not low. Dry. A stain ran down the rock into a bowl of white-cracked mud. Dren knelt and pressed his palm to it. The mud was cool under the top crust.

People started shouting behind him. He thought of the soak under the second arch at Dry Fork, stood up, and called for the mattocks.

They dug eleven hours. Four mattocks, an axe, a maul, broken bowls, bare hands. The chained lines worked in shifts small enough to fit the cut while the free walkers hauled spoil. By midnight the hole was over Dren's head. Near dawn they reached nine feet.

Water silvered the bottom.

The woman beside Dren touched it with two fingers and started laughing. Someone above shouted to ask what she'd found. She held up a hand black with mud and the whole cut answered at once. For a minute the diggers had to brace themselves against the walls until the noise passed.

It came through grit, filthy and slow. They dipped it into jars, let the worst settle, strained it, and kept the pot boiling. The first cups went to the people who'd stopped sweating. The hole refilled about as fast as they emptied it.

Thirst had made fights at every weak source before this one. Here, people sat with empty cups and watched the level crawl back. A Trovgar woman kept the order by tapping each container with a spoon. When somebody tried to crowd past, his own line pulled him down before Dren moved.

Nine Trovgar captives died between finding the dry bowl and leaving it. Three went before the water came. Six drank and never recovered. The free Sotha count held at seventeen.

On the second night Dren sat above the fires while Fimmion read him her figures. One hundred seventy-two from the Trovgar lot, seventeen from Sotha Bal. Thirty-two dead. One hundred eighty-nine evacuees alive.

The water hadn't vanished. It had dropped nine feet in nine years.

They rested through Day Thirty-One and left at dawn on Day Thirty-Two. The first night out, Dren smelled wet leaves and rot on the north wind. It was faint enough that he thought memory had made it. The next night half the camp smelled it. Whatever carried that odor was no more than a few tens of kilometers away.

They saw the green on Day Thirty-Four.

It lay across the north, dark and thin as a fingernail.

Not low. Far away.

The line climbed for three days. Two more Trovgar men died on that approach, one in his sleep and one with the wall already filling half the sky. On Day Thirty-Seven the column reached it.

Fimmion brought Dren the count before she brought the rumor. One hundred seventy from the Trovgar lot. Seventeen free Sotha survivors. Thirty-four dead since Dry Fork—thirty from the captive lot and four from the tavern party. One hundred eighty-seven evacuees stood under the jungle wall.

“They think you're taking us inside,” she said.

“I'm not.”

“I know. They don't.” She nodded toward the fourth line. “The story down there is that something in the trees eats people and you're the man who brings its food.”

“What did you tell them?”

“That a feeder wouldn't have dug nine feet for us.”

“Weak defense.”

“Give me a better one.”

Dren climbed onto the wagon.

“We don't go in,” he told them. “Tomorrow we turn west and keep this on our right. The tall country runs about three weeks. Near the far end it comes down low. A few days after that we reach water so wide you can't see across it.”

He showed them the line on his chart and the bearing on the needle. He told them where the small streams should be, how long the worst dry gaps had been nine years ago, and which marks were guesses. For once he didn't try to make the story sound certain.

The sea still sounded like fever talk. Turning west did not. The next morning they put the green on their right and walked beside it.

Streams leaked from under the trees every few days. Most ran twenty yards into the pale ground and disappeared. The water parties filled skins first, then jars, then the reserve barrels. They settled what was cloudy and boiled what smelled wrong. For the first time since Ghor, a cup could be given without somebody measuring the next two days against it.

Food took more people and more nerve. Dren knew the coastal plants, not this high edge, so they watched birds and small animals before touching anything. Trovgar farmers tested roots on their lips, then tongues, then in bites no bigger than a nail. Children dug pale grubs out of dead wood. Two women made insect baskets from split reed and smoked the catch over dung fires. Hunters set snares on the game tracks and Dren used the crossbow only when he was sure of the shot.

Some evenings the work produced a pot with meat in it. Most evenings it made a root broth full of legs. One boy asked whether the legs counted as meat and was told to stop improving dinner.

They found sour red fruit, nuts that had to be roasted twice, three kinds of edible leaf, and tubers that made the cook pot smell like wet rope. It kept people moving. It didn't fill them. Hip bones sharpened and the two blue-tied seed sacks stayed under guard in the wagon.

The insects came hardest at water. Faces swelled. Eyes closed. Fever shook whole sections of the camp after dusk. Dren stood in the cloud with his sleeves down and a cloth around his neck, filling skins while hornets worked around his hands.

A Trovgar man asked how he could stand it.

“I already went in,” Dren said.

Three captives died before they reached the broad river on Day Forty-Seven. The river came out of the jungle and held west instead of sinking into sand. They followed it through Day Fifty. Two more died while it ran beside them.

“What's it called?” someone asked.

“I don't know.”

“You've marked it.”

“I've marked water.” Dren squatted at the bank. “The Green River back east runs east along the wall. I went into it, got carried in the dark, and came out on this side without knowing where. This may be a western branch of the same water. It may be another river altogether.”

“So which do we call it?”

“Call it the western branch until somebody proves us wrong.”

The river bent into the trees on Day Fifty-One. The column kept west. One more Trovgar captive died before the country changed.

On Day Fifty-Eight the jungle lowered near its end. The hundred-floor canopy stepped down to fifty, then twenty. Scrub spread between the last tall stands, and pale ground showed through it. The green hadn't ended. It had begun turning a corner Dren recognized.

He sat on the boundary with one hand in shade and one on hot dirt. Then he got up and pointed west.

“Three more days.”

They continued west. They did not turn north.

Blue appeared ahead the next evening where the pale ground seemed to stop. A man with good eyes called it sky. By the following day people were arguing because the blue lay below the sky and still had no far edge.

The last death came on Day Sixty, a Trovgar woman whose fever had started at the river. Fimmion's figures became one hundred sixty-three from the Trovgar lot and seventeen from the free Sotha group. Forty-one dead. One hundred eighty evacuees remained.

“They want to know what happens when the ground stops,” Fimmion told Dren.

“It doesn't.”

“I told them.”

“Tell them again.”

She stopped walking. “Tell them what it is.”

Dren remembered white sand under his hands, a leg going green, and a dark shape leaning where the world should have ended. It had taken the moving edge to make him understand.

“No,” he said. “Let them see it.”

“That's mean.”

“A little,” Dren said. “Keep walking.”

They came over the last rise late on Day Sixty-One.

The column stopped in pieces as each shortened chain reached the top. One hundred eighty evacuees stood along a low sand ridge and looked west.

The tide was out. Wet sand shone for nearly a mile. Beyond it the water went green, blue, then dark blue toward the point, every band moving. A slow sound reached them after each white edge folded onto the beach.

The water didn't narrow into a river or close against another bank. It went out until distance took it.

Someone said, “That's water.”

No one answered.

A few stepped back from the ridge. Others knelt and stared as if lowering their heads might bring the far bank into view. The old man from Ghor held both hands out to measure it, gave up, and let them fall. Wind came off the bay cold enough to lift shirts from damp backs.

They went down. The ten ragged Trovgar lines crossed the wet flat with the free Sotha walkers among them, and Dren stayed on the ridge until shouting began in the shallows.

Several people drank. They spat and cursed. One tried again, as if the first mouthful might have come from a bad patch.

“All bad patches,” Dren told him when he came down.

The man wiped his tongue on his sleeve. “You could've said.”

“Would you have believed me?”

That got an ugly laugh, then another. The noise spread across the shallows.

Dren let them stand in it until the light began to go. Only after they'd reached the sea did he turn north. They followed the water with the ocean on their left and the jungle on their right. Half a kilometer along, a clear stream crossed the beach.

They made the first secure fresh-water camp above its mouth. Dren tested the water, filled a cup, and drank it where everyone could see. Then he took the wire loop of keys from his coat.

The Trovgar lines had begun with twenty apiece. Now some held seventeen, some sixteen, and none held twenty. Dren started at the first. He opened every wrist iron himself and worked down all ten shortened lines while people held the loose chain out of the sand.

Several locks were packed with grit. He washed them in the stream, worked wagon grease into the keyways, and tried again. At the third line Fimmion held the torch while Arvyna watched from outside the iron. When Fimmion's cuff opened, she set it down carefully instead of throwing it. Dren didn't know whether that was restraint or exhaustion.

One hundred sixty-three captives came out of the iron. The seventeen survivors from the free Sotha party had never worn it.

“I'm sorry it took this long,” Dren said whenever his hands slowed enough for words.

No one cheered. They rubbed the skin under the cuffs. Some walked a few paces away and came back. There was nowhere they had to stand anymore.

Dren coiled the prison chains beside the wagon. He didn't put them aboard.

At the stream he made people drink a cup, sit, then take another. Most hadn't reached the coast dangerously dry, but Dren remembered what his body had done when he filled an empty stomach too fast. By the next afternoon they could drink as they pleased.

The first food came in cups. Dren remembered the barrel of dried fruit in the ship, both hands shoving sweetness into his mouth, then the hours curled on the boards while his gut tried to turn itself inside out. He wasn't going to watch one hundred eighty people learn that lesson at once.

He split hairy brown nuts for their water, mashed yellow fruit thin with fresh water, and put fish broth on the fires. Each person got a little. Then they waited. An hour later they got more.

Some wouldn't wait. They found shellfish in the falling tide and fruit under the trees and ate until Dren caught them. By midnight the camp had vomiting, cramps, and a run on the bushes. Nobody died, but the people who'd laughed at his little cups stopped laughing.

The next morning he increased the portions. He did it again that evening and over the days that followed. The very weak drank broth while the rest learned clams, crabs, roots, and fish traps. Full meals came slowly.

Once the fires were steady, Dren told them about the ship.

“It's forty kilometers north. A wooden thing bigger than any building you've seen. People crossed this water in it, it wrecked, and I lived there eight years. There are tools, canvas, dry stores, and a roof.”

“How long?” someone asked.

“I did it alone in three days. In our condition, call it a week.”

“Our condition,” Arvyna said. “You're still talking like it's an order.”

Dren looked at the chains beside the wagon.

“It isn't. The stream is fresh and there's food here. Anyone who wants to stay can stay. I'm asking you to come north because the ship gives us shelter and tools.” He had to stop and begin the last part again. “I'm asking. I won't put iron on anyone, here or anywhere.”

Fimmion stood by the fire with her count sheet in her hand. “Then give people the night to answer.”

They argued after Dren lay down. Some wanted three days at the stream. Some wanted never to move again. People who'd spent sixty-one days unable to choose the next step took the choice seriously and loudly.

By morning they'd decided to go together. Not for Dren. Nobody wanted to break apart at the first safe camp after surviving the road as one group, and a roof with tools forty kilometers away was worth seven short days.

They turned north on Day Sixty-Two, ocean left, jungle right. The firm wet sand made good walking, but they were weak and Dren kept the stages short while their bodies accepted food again.

The tide taught them where dinner hid. At low water they dug clams from breathing holes and lifted crabs out of pools. One old woman knew what to do with a clawed thing because she'd eaten something like it from a lake sixty years earlier. They set the folded net between rocks and built fish traps at two points.

The fruit trees continued on their right for the full forty kilometers. Dren named what he knew and left the rest alone. By the fourth day the sick stomachs had settled, bowls came back clean, and someone sang while they walked.

The ship appeared on Day Sixty-Eight.

Dren saw the black lean first and stopped. The others came level with him until all one hundred eighty stood along the tide line looking north.

The hull rose out of the shallows, bow high, masts down, its flank broken open. It looked less like a building than a piece of a town dropped into the bay.

People began walking again. Not in ten lines. Families, friends, and former chain-mates went up the beach at the fastest pace they'd managed since Dry Fork.

The old man from Ghor put both hands against the hull and leaned back to see the top.

“Who made this?”

“I don't know,” Dren said.

“Where are they?”

“I buried the ones I found.”

Days Sixty-Eight and Sixty-Nine were for rest and food. Dren took small groups through the break in the hull. He showed them the dry half of the hold, the cabin windows, folded silk, and the deck of iron tubes. Soot marked the open mouths, and fitted racks held round iron balls beside them.

Some people cried in the cabin. Others lay on the tilted bed and laughed because a bed had no business feeling like that. Meals grew to full bowls under Dren's eye. By the second evening even the weakest could keep broth down.

The sealed food stores were thinner than Dren remembered and still looked rich beyond reason: dried fruit, salted meat, grain, and hard cakes that had survived in waxed cloth. He opened one container at a time. The rest stayed sealed against the first bad season. Outside, fishing crews kept the pots supplied so the wreck wouldn't feed the camp by disappearing.

Dren had prepared pieces of the wreck without admitting to himself what they were for. During his first years there he'd moved selected tarred line, sound blocks, folded canvas, and the small black-grit casks into high dry compartments. They'd stayed out of nine wet seasons. Much of the exposed rigging had rotted, but what he'd stored was sound.

On the morning of Day Seventy, he asked everyone to meet on the sand.

“The storms grind this ship lower every wet season,” he said. “If we pull it above the highest water, it can be a storehouse and shelter. I want your help.”

Someone looked from him to the hull. “Pull that?”

“With rope and blocks. After we empty it.”

“Do people who say no still eat?” Fimmion asked.

“Yes.”

“Do they still sleep under the roof?” Arvyna asked.

“Yes. Nobody owes me work for taking off iron that shouldn't have been on them.”

Arvyna faced the others. “Then he's asked. We can answer.”

The talking took an hour. A builder wanted to inspect the keel. Two people said the hull would split. A woman who'd hauled the wagon since Veyloth asked whether madness became sensible if enough rope was involved. In the end, they chose to try. The ship would belong to the settlement, not to Dren, and anyone too sick or unwilling to haul would have another job or none.

They stripped the ship for six days, Days Seventy through Seventy-Five.

Barrels, crates, casks, ballast, loose timber—everything movable went through the break and above the tide line. Iron balls passed hand to hand. Dren told four careful people to keep the small black-grit casks level, dry, and away from fire. He still didn't know what the grit did. Its sealed casks and the soot inside the tubes were warning enough.

The builder from Ghor rigged a timber ramp through the hole. Two crews worked inside, two on the ramp, and the rest made orderly piles on dry ground. It was the first work since Veyloth in which anyone could leave a crew without pulling nineteen people sideways. They changed jobs often, stopped for meals, and learned the ship a room at a time.

The twelve iron tubes took most of the work. They rolled eleven out on cut poles with ropes around their frames. The twelfth had crashed through the deck when the ship wrecked and stayed where it was. Nobody cared enough about iron to die under it.

They unrove the surviving line, checked every handspan for rot, and sorted the pulleys. Dren brought out his stored gear last. The tarred rope bent without cracking. The blocks turned after they cleaned and oiled the sheaves.

For three days, Days Seventy-Six through Seventy-Eight, they built the slipway. They cut a trench thirty feet wide ahead of the bow and carried it past the highest weed line. Straight trunks became rollers. Broad timbers went underneath as runners. Pots of rendered fish oil and the ship's remaining tallow made the wood slick; the honey-colored medicine from Dren's old crocks stayed with the medical stores.

Six of the largest trees stood back from the beach. They wrapped each trunk in timber and rope, then reeved six purchases through paired blocks. Each hauling rope ran through the blocks several times before coming back to shore. Dren couldn't have named the rule behind it. He knew that a shorter pull on many parts made a heavier thing answer.

“Six sets of rope and blocks,” he told the crews. “Pull together. If one goes slack, stop. If any timber speaks, get clear.”

They cut harnesses from canvas and soft line, broad across shoulders and hips. No prison chain touched the work. People chose crews. Those who couldn't pull carried water, tended rollers, watched the anchor trees, or stood ready with chocks. The children had the critical office of staying where falling timber couldn't find them, which several regarded as a poor use of talent.

Dren had watched the tides for eight years. The next larger pair would come on Day Seventy-Nine, one near dawn and one late in the day. The timing was luck, and he used it.

At the first high water, the sea lifted the low side of the hull. The six purchases came tight. A woman from Trovgar called the pull, and the harness crews leaned together.

The ship moved an inch.

A long wooden groan came out of it and every rope stopped.

“That's her complaining,” Dren shouted. “She did it to me for eight years. Pull.”

The count started again. Blocks screamed. The bow climbed onto the first rollers. People at the sides drove chocks behind each gain while other crews dragged free rollers around to the front. By the time the tide fell away, they'd moved the hull forty feet and brought more than half its weight onto the slipway.

The anchor watchers kept palms against the six trunks and called out every shift. Twice a wrapping crept and the whole beach stopped while it was reset. The harness ropes stretched dark with sweat. Nobody used the old marching cadence. The caller found a slower count that let every crew breathe on the same number.

They secured every purchase, wedged the rollers, and lay down where they were. Food and water went along the six crews. Nobody discussed giving up; they were too tired to waste breath on a proposal that bad.

The second tide came under the stern near evening. They reset the falls, checked the canvas harnesses, and hauled again.

This pull was shorter and worse. The sea gave the stern one last lift while the bow rode the oiled runners. A roller split. Three people went down in the same harness and the others stopped before anyone was dragged. They changed the timber, stood, and took the rope again.

Near the top, the hull stuck hard enough to drag grooves under the chocks. The old man from Ghor crawled beneath the raised bow, found a runner shoved crosswise, and beat it straight while Dren swore at him to come out. He emerged grinning, sand in his teeth, and told Dren a house only falls once. Then the crews leaned back into the harnesses.

At the last count the hull slid above the high-water line by its own length. It settled onto one side with its broken flank open toward the camp and its keel toward the sea.

It was the first deliberate journey the ship had made since Dren found it nine years ago. It had taken two high tides, six purchases, and nearly every set of willing hands on the beach.

People dropped out of their rope harnesses and stayed in the sand. Some laughed. Some were sick. The woman who'd called the count had no voice left and kept moving her lips anyway.

The ship was out.

That night Fimmion read the final trek tally beside the fires. One hundred sixty-three survivors from the Trovgar lot, herself among them, and seventeen from the free Sotha party. One hundred eighty evacuees had reached the ship. Dren still wasn't in the number.

He stood on a barrel with the hauled-out hull behind him.

“You know the road back,” he said. “If anyone wants it, we'll pack food and skins when you're strong enough. I won't tell you there's no one to return for, because I don't know that.”

Faces lifted around the fires.

“Trovgar burned. I saw it. Sotha Bal fell while I was there. Sheo is missing, not proven dead. Caius is presumed dead because four people placed him at the gate when it came down. I never reached West Outpost, and Veyloth still has people in it. There may be survivors in farms and camps all along the road.”

He put a hand against the hull.

“I think we start here. Fresh water at the river, fish in the bay, timber and fruit behind us, and two sacks of seed still sewn in the wagon. When we're able, we go back for everyone we can reach.”

People looked at the ship they'd moved and at one another. A builder broke the silence by asking where to put the first cooking shelter. Someone else wanted a watch at the river camp. The argument moved on from whether they would stay to what needed doing before daylight.

Someone near the front asked, “What do we call this place?”

“I called the water turquoise when I was alone here,” Dren said. “I don't know any name for this stretch of coast in our language or on any chart from home. The ship has writing on it, but that may only be the ship's name.”

“Name the beach after yourself.”

“No.” The word came out hard enough to quiet the nearby fires.

Dren had raised boards over eight strangers from the hold. At Dry Fork he'd stacked stones for Caius and for a missing friend whose board Fimmion had refused to mark. He'd never put up anything for his son.

James lay outside Drenovar in a common pit. Dren had left that pit, walked into the jungle, and spent nine years finding a place the boy would never see. There wasn't so much as a scrap of wood with his name on it.

“James,” Dren said.

He had to say it again.

“James Beach. My son. He was twenty-six. He sold vegetables, and he was better than me.” Dren looked toward the dark water. “He never saw anything like this. So this part is his.”

By morning, everyone was using the name.

Fimmion found a sound board in the unloaded stores and a carpenter's chisel sharp enough to cut it. She wrote JAMES BEACH above the tide line. She'd left Sheo's board blank because he was missing. James wasn't.

Dren watched each letter go in. He couldn't read a mark of it.
