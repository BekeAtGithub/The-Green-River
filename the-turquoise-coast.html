The Turquoise Coast

He tried to stand. His good leg took it; the other didn’t, and he went down on his hands. So he crawled.

The dry sand blistered his palms. It was white and fine and softer than anything he’d touched, and he could only bear it for a few seconds at a time. Hands down, drag, hands up. Elbows when his palms couldn’t take it.

The beach ran white in both directions, farther than he could see, with the trees stopped at its edge in a clean green line. It was soft and open and warm. He was dying in the middle of it. Twenty feet on, the sand turned dark, firm, and cool, and crawling got easier.

The water touched his hands. He put his face in it and drank.

It came back at once. He gagged, spat, gagged again, and kept spitting after there was nothing left, eyes streaming and throat burning.

Salt.

He raised himself on his elbows and looked out to the horizon. There was more water than in the world he knew and not one mouthful was any good to him. He laughed again, briefly.

The fresh river was behind him. He turned and crawled back to its mouth, where the current still pushed through the salt, then lay in the shallows and drank until his stomach hurt.

After a while he noticed what was missing. Nothing landed on his face or crawled into his ears. Nothing worked at the corners of his eyes or the back of his neck. Clean wind moved off the open water with no haze in it and no hum. He covered his ears from habit, took his hands away, and still heard none of them.

The wind dried his sweat. He hadn’t been dry in a month. Under it was another sound, big and slow, coming in and going out without stopping and never quite the same twice. He had no word for it. He lay there listening, and after everything, the sound nearly finished him.

Then the salt found his leg.

It went up through him like something driven in with a hammer. His back arched off the sand and his ears rang, a high hard tone that blotted out the water and the wind and everything else, and he made a noise he didn’t recognize as his own.

It took a long time to come down. When it did, he lay panting and studied the bite. Nothing had hurt like that, not the thorns or hornets or the teeth themselves. In his experience, pain that sharp meant something was happening, though he couldn’t have said what.

He lowered the leg into the water on purpose. He screamed into the sand, pulled it out, and tried twice more. By the third time he could hold it there. The wound looked no better and no worse. He had no other evidence. For the moment, that would have to do.

The shape was still out there. While he lay in the shallows, the water had drawn back and exposed a wide reach of wet sand. He had no explanation, so he used it.

He got onto the knee of his good leg and shuffled out. The remaining water was shallow for a standing man, not for him; in places it reached his chest. He crawled through those stretches with his mouth up and the bad leg dragging. The shape grew the whole way.

By the time he reached it, the shape had become a thing standing over him. It leaned hard to one side, its top higher than anything he’d stood beside, higher than the towers on Veyloth’s wall. Thousands of pieces of wood had been cut, shaped, and fitted with scarcely a gap. Iron banded it. Ropes thicker than his arm hung from it. Tar blackened the boards, and below the waterline something hard, gray, and living crusted them.

People had made this. A great many people, over a long time. Dren came around the leaning side and found a hole big enough to walk through. Its edges were splintered and driven inward, every broken piece bent in the same direction. Whatever struck the ship had come from the open water and come fast.

He looked at the damage a moment longer, but had nothing to make of it. Beyond the hole he could see barrels, stacked and lashed before the wreck rolled some onto their sides. Crates stood behind them. Barrels meant something was inside. Dren put a hand on the wood and pulled himself into the dark.

Light came in behind him and got about ten feet and quit. The floor wasn’t a floor, it was a slope, the whole ship lying over on its side, and everything that had been stacked in here had gone down to the low end and piled up against the wall.

The smell hit him before his eyes adjusted. Wet rot, tar, salt, something gone sour underneath all of it, and running through the middle of it something sweet and sharp that made his head go light.

Black water stood on the low side. Everything there was swollen, split, and furred over: crates come apart, sacks gone to slime. The lean had lifted the other half of the hold clear, and cargo lashed to that wall remained dry above the waterline.

Getting up to the dry cargo was the problem. He couldn’t stand, and his knee slipped whenever he tried to crawl up wet boards. The wall was strung with rope, lashings running through iron rings to hold the barrels and crates. Rope he understood. He climbed hand over hand and dragged the bad leg, stopping wherever the cargo net gave him a place to hook an elbow and breathe.

At the first barrel he put his fingers under the rim and pulled. It didn’t move. He braced a shoulder against it and pulled until something tore under his nails, but the lid stayed put. It was set deep into the rim, and the hoops held the barrel shut as if the whole thing were one piece of wood.

The cargo up here had survived because it was sealed. Everything broken lay below. Going down was easy—too easy. Dren released the rope, the slope took him, and he hit the black water much harder than he meant to. It rose over his chest and stank of everything that had rotted there.

He felt through loose staves, soft as bread, and sacking that came apart in his hands. A crate folded when he leaned against it. Then his fingers found an edge beneath the water.

He pulled up a long curve of flat iron, a hoop left after its barrel had rotted away. More hoops lay tangled below where the wood had vanished and left the iron. He chose the straightest, shoved it down his shirt, and hauled himself back up. The climb took both hands and his one good leg. Every few feet he rested with his forehead against the boards.

At the top he drove the flat end between lid and rim, put his weight on the curve, and used his body as the rest of the lever. The wood cracked. He worked around the barrel until the lid came away in pieces. Inside were small dark things, piled almost to the rim.

He picked one out and looked at it. Wrinkled. Dry. Sticky. He had no idea what it was. He put it in his mouth anyway because he was past the point of caring what killed him.

It was sweet.

Not a little sweet. It went straight up the back of his skull, pulling his face tight and bringing water to his eyes before he swallowed. He put in a handful.

He was crying before the second handful, but he didn’t stop eating. He sat on the slope of a dead ship with his ruined leg stretched before him, shoving the fruit in with both hands. He chewed and sobbed, swallowed and reached for more. Hunger kept his hands moving long after the first rush of sweetness had passed.

His stomach turned. Everything came back onto the boards, and he stayed bent over it, shaking, ribs cramped and gut twisting until he couldn’t straighten. He’d eaten almost nothing in twelve days and then given his body more than it could hold.

He lay with his cheek on the wood until the cramps let go. Then he ate three, slowly, waited, and ate three more. Those stayed down.

He kept to three at a time for the rest of the day. He cried on and off while he ate, and after a while the crying had nothing to do with food. He let it happen until his ribs hurt for a different reason.

Later, when he could move again, he took the iron hoop along the dry wall. The next barrel held orange things, soft and leathery and nearly as sweet. Another was packed with grain. A third held something hard, dried, and salty that he had to gnaw. There were crates of it, then more barrels behind those, continuing into the dark past where the light reached.

Casks stood among them. One had split along a seam, and the sharp smell running through the hold came from there. Dren knew that smell. He dipped a finger and tasted it. The liquid burned all the way down. It wasn’t whiskey, but it was near enough that he knew what he held: sweeter and rounder, with another taste beneath it for which he had no name.

Whoever these people were, they made drink. He sat with that small familiar fact for a moment, then left the cask alone. On an empty stomach it would have put him flat.

He sat on the dark slope with sticky hands, a cramping stomach, and salt burning in his leg.

He wasn’t going to die today. He hadn’t been able to say that since he stepped between two trunks at the river.

He looked back into the dark end of the hold, where the light stopped and the barrels kept going.

He’d get to it, but not today.

The water came for him that night.

He heard the water before he understood it. The hold’s sound changed, a slow filling beneath the creak of timber. When he put a hand down where he’d been sitting, his fingers went into water.

It was coming up.

He hauled himself to the high side and hooked an arm through the netting before thought caught up. Below, black water climbed the slope. It covered the ruined crates, then the place where he’d found the hoops, and kept rising until it nearly reached his feet.

The ship was sinking; he had no other answer. It had waited until he crawled inside and now meant to take him down. There was nowhere higher he could reach in darkness, so he hung in the netting and waited to drown.

The water stopped. It stayed high for a long while, then went down as slowly as it had come. By morning the low side was black mud and standing puddles, and the ship hadn’t moved. Dren understood none of it. That afternoon the water came again.

By the third time he stopped fearing it enough to watch. The water came in, stood, and went out. It returned hours later. Since he’d entered the jungle, nothing else had kept a schedule. He didn’t understand this one, but he could count it. He began marking each rise and fall against the light, and counting gave him something to hold.

The next days belonged to his gut and the fever. Most food passed straight through him, one way or the other, and he learned to crouch on the low side where the returning water carried the mess away. Cramps folded him onto the boards and kept him there. He’d sweat through one tide, soak his shirt, and shake through the next.

He kept eating: three at a time, then five, then grain soaked in fresh water. He carried the water from the river mouth in a small corked cask, which took most of a day to work out and made him prouder than it should have. Little by little, the food stayed in him. He could feel strength returning by the hour.

The leg kept worsening. Salt had hurt enough to feel useful, but the wound gave no sign it had helped. The red remained above his knee, the smell clung to it, and he couldn’t bear weight.

Men back home poured whiskey into cuts. Dren had seen it and once had it done to his hand; he still remembered the man who held him down. He didn’t know whether it worked. He only knew people did it.

He poured the spirit into the bite. It burned worse than the salt.

He fell sideways with his fists shut and teeth locked. The pain lasted longer than the salt had. When he could hear again, he was down the slope with no memory of getting there.

He did it once more the next day because fear had left him no better idea. Then he searched the crates properly with the hoop. He found folded canvas, heavy and clean where it had been packed tight; a chest of fine linen shirts; coils of light line; a pot of black, tacky stuff; and wax-sealed crocks of honey.

He tasted the honey, then spread it on the bite because it was sticky and held clean cloth in place. He had no better reason. The spirit had left the exposed meat pale and raw, so he stopped pouring it in. He boiled fresh river water, let it cool, washed the wound, and dressed it with honey on linen. He changed the cloth whenever it soaked through.

Nothing improved quickly. For nearly three weeks the fever broke and returned. Some mornings the red had crept higher. The wound drained yellow and brown, glued itself to the dressing, and opened again when he pulled the cloth free. He crawled for food and water, slept wherever exhaustion caught him, and more than once woke surprised to be alive.

Then the fever broke and stayed gone. He couldn’t say the sea, the spirit, or the honey had saved him. He’d kept the wound covered and himself fed, and he’d been very lucky. The smell faded over the following week. The red came down slowly from his thigh and knee.

The hole remained. It closed over months, slowly and ugly, leaving a dead patch in the middle of his calf where the muscle never came back.

He found the stick without looking for it, up under the netting where it had wedged when the ship went over. A shaft of pale hard wood about as long as he was tall, with an iron head fitted onto the end of it, a hook on one side and a point on the other.

He had no idea what it was for.

He turned it over a few times and decided what it was for.

The first time he got up on it, he made four steps before sitting down. The next day he made six. He practiced on the sloping boards, learning where to plant the iron point and how much weight the bad leg would accept before folding. A week later he worked through the hole and onto the sand.

He stood in the open on two feet, leaning on the pale shaft while the wind came off the water. He wore a stranger’s clean shirt. There was food in him and a fresh dressing under his trousers. For the first time since the bite, he could choose where to go without crawling.

He was going to keep the limp. He knew that already. Every step he took for the rest of his life was going to go through that leg and the leg was going to charge him for it.

He didn’t much care. He’d take it.

He told himself he was looking for a better stick. He used that excuse for days while he ate, slept, changed the dressing, and got a little farther up the beach each morning. A straighter branch would be useful. That was all he was doing.

He’d spent well over a month in the ship’s bottom without going past where the light stopped. He knew it and kept finding work in the bright end of the hold.

The problem he could admit to was light. Ten feet in, the hold went black, and he wasn’t going to crawl through a wreck he couldn’t see. He could solve that problem with his hands. Solving why he dreaded the dark end could wait.

The pot of black, tacky stuff took a flame and held it; he’d known that from the smell. He wound a strip of canvas around a broken stave and worked tar into it, then sat with the finished torch in his lap. He had nothing to light it with.

He found what he needed the next morning in a small wooden box inside a chest, wedged into the corner where the ship’s lean had thrown it. Flint. A curl of steel. A pinch of char cloth gone to dust. The tools fitted his hand without needing explanation.

He turned the pieces in his hands. The ship’s wood was cut strangely, its rope laid strangely, and its cloth better than anything he’d owned. Yet its people had lit fires exactly as he did. He struck the steel, and the torch caught on the first try.

The dark end of the hold reached farther than he’d guessed. His torch gave him only a room at a time: barrels still lashed in their chocks, a wall of crates, and the black crust left by sacks that had rotted away.

The first body lay half in the mud on the low side. Dren saw its shape before he understood and stopped moving. He held the torch farther out. Bones lay as the person had fallen, a long shape no one had disturbed. Mud-colored cloth remained in patches, one arm reached away from the ribs, and boots had lasted better than the man.

He’d been eating, sleeping, and healing thirty feet away without knowing. He stood there until the torch began to spit. Then he said a few words, not quite a prayer because he had none that fit, but enough to mark that he’d seen the dead man. Going on felt wrong. Going back felt worse.

A door stood sideways in the wall at the hold’s end. The world here had turned a quarter over: floors became walls, and each doorway was a puzzle. Dren hooked his shaft wherever it held and hauled himself through with the torch in his teeth.

Above the hold, another long deck ran through most of the ship. Twelve enormous iron tubes sat in heavy wooden frames on small wheels, each longer than Dren was tall and thicker than his body. Wrist-thick ropes held them against the walls. One had torn loose when the ship rolled, crossed the tilted deck, and punched straight through the boards below.

At home, iron meant a knife, a pot, or a hinge, each repaired until repair was impossible. Here it had been spent as if it cost nothing. Dren laid both hands on one tube and felt the cold through his palms. The open end was wide enough for his head. He held the torch near it and found only a black throat stained with soot.

Dozens of perfectly round iron balls stood in a fitted rack beside the tubes. Each was heavy enough to make him use both hands. Square openings with hinged covers ran at even spaces along the ship’s side, one for every tube. He looked from a tube to an opening, then back to the balls, trying to make the three parts into one purpose. Whatever they did, a great many people had wanted them badly. They weren’t for food, water, shelter, or warmth.

A row of small, sealed casks was lashed down at the end of the deck. They were heavier than they looked and made no sound of liquid when he rolled one with his boot. Whatever they held, it wasn’t drink. He’d open one outside in daylight.

Three more bodies lay on this deck. Two had fallen together against the wall. The third had come apart beside the fourth tube, its bones scattered the way loose things slid when the ship rolled. Among its ribs sat a short iron point with a thick spine, where no such iron belonged. Dren knew a weapon wound when he saw one. He looked back at the tubes, square openings, and iron balls. They moved a step closer to sense, then stopped.

These people hadn’t died of the sea.

The next door had been barred from the inside. He pried until the bar tore loose from one bracket, then squeezed through sideways. The narrow room beyond had racks along both walls, and every rack was full.

Swords stood in rows, each in a slot cut to its shape. Long-handled axes and mace heads hung beside a dozen bows built into wooden stocks with cranks at the side. Some iron had rusted, but most hadn’t. A dull black coating covered the surviving blades, and whatever it was, it had done its work.

Dren took down a sword. Sheo had put the last one into his hands in James’s front room while telling him not to go. The river had taken it with everything else, and Dren had felt naked since. He ran his thumb along the flat of this blade, turned it in the torchlight, and studied the grain in the steel and the fit of the guard. It was better than his old sword. Better than anything he’d held.

Standing in a dead man’s armory with a dead man’s blade, he felt some small part of himself return to his chest. A belt hung from a peg nearby. He buckled it on, settled the sword at his hip, and didn’t take it off again.

Farther forward, in cramped sleeping rooms and a passage crushed by fallen timber, he found four more bodies. Two lay in narrow bunks, one beneath a broken chest, and one at the foot of a ladder. With the four already behind him, that made eight. He counted again to be sure.

Back in the armory, a fitted wooden case on the end shelf held three of something else.

He got one out. It was an iron tube fixed to a wooden stock, a little longer than his forearm and shaped to sit against the shoulder like a bow-stock. Near the closed end, a small hole had been bored through the top. A curved lever hung beneath. When he pulled it, a spring drove an iron claw forward. The claw gripped a stub of blackened cord.

He worked the lever several times and watched the claw snap down. Nothing happened. He raised the torch and looked into the open end. Soot again. Dren turned the little tube over, felt where his hands naturally settled, and looked from it to the twelve enormous tubes below.

Small and large, the same thing made twice. He still had no idea what it did.

He put it back exactly as he’d found it, closed the case, and kept the sword at his hip.

He kept going up.

At the high end of the ship, all the way back, he found a door finer than the others, carved and closely fitted. It resisted until he got the iron hoop under its edge. When it opened, he put the torch through and stayed in the doorway.

Light. Real light came through a wall of small panes set in lead, most of them still whole. It threw squares of sun across a room that had rolled onto its side and somehow stayed beautiful.

The floor was polished. A table had been fixed to it with brass at the corners, and shelves had rails to keep their contents in place. A real bed stood on what was now the wall above him. Its frame had held, and the weight of the mattress pinned the bedding there. Dren climbed to it and touched cloth so cool and smooth it made the linen shirts feel like sacking. He sat on the wall turning a sheet through his hands, unable to name what he was touching.

There was no one in the room. The bed was made.

With the sword on his hip, he went through the rest slowly. He unrolled one of the stiff papers from a rack across the tilted table and held the torch over it.

It was a drawing. Shapes with edges, blue on one side and pale on the other, lines running out from them, little marks all over it in a hand that wasn’t any writing he knew.

Not a drawing. The edges were too particular. Nobody invented a coastline that fussy.

It was a map.

He looked first for the Glare, the biggest thing in his world. It wasn’t there. Neither was the wall of green. He put a finger in the middle of the paper and moved from one careful shape to another, searching for anything he’d seen or heard described. Nothing answered. Either somebody had spent a great deal of time drawing a very careful lie, or every place Dren knew lay beyond the paper’s edge.

A brass bowl hung inside a ring so it stayed level however he tipped the frame. Under glass, a needle rested across the bowl. Dren turned it. The needle stayed where it was. He turned faster, and this time it swung, wavered, then settled toward exactly the same patch of empty wall. He tried once more before taking his hands away and stepping back.

A wooden frame held two glass bulbs with sand in the lower one. He understood that and was almost relieved. Beside it lay a brass tube with glass at both ends. He looked through the wrong end first, and the room shrank and rushed far away. He nearly dropped it. After turning it around, he braced one hand against the window frame and raised it again.

The tree line, four hundred yards off, came and sat down in front of his face.

He put the tube down on the table very carefully, the way you put down something that might be listening.

The hoop opened a small locked chest beneath the table. It held hundreds of gold and silver coins stamped with strangers’ faces. Dren ran a hand through them and let them fall. It was the most money he’d seen and worth exactly nothing.

He sat on the tilted floor of the captain’s cabin, in sunlight, with silk in one hand, and began to laugh. He’d spent more than a month in the bilge, sleeping on wet boards above a dead man, eating with his fingers and crawling down to the black water to be sick. All that time, there had been windows and a bed above him.

“I’ve been living down there with the rot,” he told the empty room, and set himself off again.

He brought the bedding down off the wall that night and made a place in the corner where the floor met the window frame, and he lay down on cloth that a dead lord had slept on, with the sea coming in and going out below him, and a sword within reach, and food in his stomach.

He had the best sleep of his life.

He rolled one of the heavy casks down out of the ship a few days later and opened it on the sand, in daylight, well away from anything.

Inside was fine, dry black grit with a faint shine and a foul smell he couldn’t name. He rubbed it between his fingers, touched one grain to his tongue, and spat. It wasn’t food, drink, or medicine.

He put the lid back on, rolled it into the shade under the hull, and stopped thinking about it.

For a while, he simply lived. He woke in a bed, ate, washed the leg with cooled river water, and changed its dressing. He slept in the afternoon because nothing stopped him, woke when he pleased, and ate again. The bite hurt less by the week. The sharp pain left first, then the deep pulse, until what remained was an ache that rose whenever he did too much.

He arranged the cabin to suit himself. He righted what would stand and wedged what wouldn’t. He cleaned salt from the surviving panes and found where the morning sun landed, then moved his bedding there. Over several weeks he hauled the sound crates from the hold one at a time. There was no hurry. Each trip used the leg, gave him a task, and left one less thing below for the tide to reach.

He cut one mark into the door frame each day. After a month in the jungle without knowing a date and weeks below counting the water, he wanted a number on a wall. He checked the marks each morning the way a man checks that a coin is still in his pocket, though there was nowhere to spend a coin and nobody to ask what day it was.

Weight returned to his ribs and face. Then it kept coming, past where he’d been when he left, because he was eating like a lord and moving like an invalid. His beard grew long. Burned skin peeled and browned. The patch in his calf stayed numb, and the limp became ordinary enough that he stopped hearing every bad step.

By any measure he’d been taught, he was rich. He owned fine cloth, iron, dry food, and more gold than he could lift without effort. None of it bought company. He talked most of the day to the room, the leg, and the man in the mud whom he began greeting each morning on his way past.

Months passed before he properly saw the water’s color. The first time he’d looked at the bay, he was dying in the sand. After that, it was the place where salt had blinded him with pain, where the water rose into the hold, and where he waded because he needed something. Once trouble loosened its grip, he could stand in the shallows without asking the water to feed, clean, or spare him.

He was standing in it up to his knees one morning, doing nothing in particular, when the sun came up over the trees behind him and got onto the bay.

Close in, over the white sand, it went pale and bright and lit from underneath, blue and green at the same time and neither one of them, so clear he could count his own toes through it. Farther out it deepened in bands until it went dark and serious past the point. The color moved with the water, washed up the beach in a hissing sheet, and slid back to leave the sand shining.

He’d seen a stone that color exactly once, set into a ring on the hand of a man who came through Sotha Bal with money in a bad year. Dren had watched the hand whenever it crossed the table and looked much longer than was polite. The stone seemed impossible there. Here the same color covered miles.

The whole bay was that stone, miles of it. He stood there awhile.

“Turquoise,” he said.

He laughed at himself for naming another place aloud when there was no one to tell. He’d done it once before, standing shin-deep in rot under a hundred floors of green and mostly out of his mind. He’d been right then too.

He got in the habit of taking the glass onto the high side of the hull in the evening. With his back against the rail, he swept the sea from left to right, slowly enough not to skip anything. Every evening gave him water, then more water, then the line where it became sky. He never found a mark upon it.

Then he turned to the coast and searched the trees. He looked for smoke, cloth tied to a branch, a straight cut in wood, or the movement of someone trying to hide. In half a year he found no human work on the coast except the ship beneath him.

Once he stopped using the glass only to search for people, other details came forward. Tall trees with bare, curved trunks carried bursts of leaves and clusters of hairy brown balls beneath their crowns, a dozen to a tree. Broad-leafed plants grew in low stands farther from the sand, each holding bunches of yellow fingers. At a clearing’s edge, spiked knobs sat near the ground in nests of stiff blades, gold at the bottom and green above.

There were red things and purple things he couldn’t see clearly enough to judge. Farther down the beach, orange fruit loaded one tree until its branches bent toward the ground. Dren swept the line twice more, then sat with the tube across his knees.

The fruit itself wasn’t new. Dren had seen plenty of it overhead in the jungle while he starved. He’d even stopped once and thought about it. He hadn’t known which kind would feed him and which might kill him, and he’d been too weak to lose a day to a bad guess. Here sealed barrels waited behind him, so he kept putting off the test. Through the glass, at least, he could watch first.

For the better part of a week he sat in the shade at the trees’ edge and watched what ate what. Birds pecked the orange fruit open. Something small with hands ran up a bare trunk, twisted down a hairy ball, and knew exactly where to strike it. Whole flocks came for the yellow fingers, tearing the skins and fighting over the pale insides. None of that proved a human stomach would agree, but it gave him somewhere to start.

He brought one of the yellow things back and turned it over in his hands. The skin was thick and soft. He tried a corner with his teeth, found it bitter and awful, and nearly threw it away. Then the skin split where he’d bitten, showing something pale and soft inside. He touched that part to his tongue, waited, and took a bite.

He sat on the sand and ate half. When nothing happened by morning, he ate the rest and said nothing for a while.

The hairy ball took most of an afternoon and the sword. He cut away the coarse outside, found a shell beneath it, and tried three places before the steel cracked through. Clear water spilled over his hands. He got his mouth under it quickly. It was sweet and somehow cool after a day in the sun. White meat lined the shell, and he scraped it out with the sword point, testing a little that day and eating the rest after another night’s wait.

The spiked knob looked like it would take his hand off and tasted like the best thing anyone had ever made.

He tested each new thing the same way: touch it to the tongue, swallow a small bite, then wait a day before eating more. Some greens fed him. Others numbed his mouth and went onto the list he kept in his head under don’t. He dug tubers, tried slivers raw, and learned which ones only became food after time in the coals. A few trials ended with cramps and a day near the low side of the hold.

By the end of the first wet half he trusted a dozen foods. He knew where each grew and which stand recovered fastest after cutting. One track ran from the ship into the trees, then a second and a third. His list would pass thirty, but not for years.

The place fed him without seeming to run out. Sometimes that fact made him sit down. He’d nearly died a few days upriver with food overhead, and he remembered noticing it. Plenty of fruit high up, he’d thought before walking on, because a plains man who couldn’t name one green thing from another had no safe way to choose. He had seen food and still not known what to put in his mouth.

Without any particular day to it, he understood what he’d found. He had entered the jungle looking for supplies, water, usable ground—anything a person could carry home. A clerk in a purple coat had laughed at him, so Dren had gone himself. Now he knew.

He stood on the sand one evening and looked at it properly, the way a man looks at ground he’s thinking of buying.

Miles of flat shore lay firm behind the sand. A fresh river ran hard from the trees all year, carrying more water in a day than Veyloth saw in a season. Fish broke the shallows. Dren had caught them as a boy in the creek at Ral Koda, back when Ral Koda had a creek, and this bay held more than every stream where he’d ever put a hand.

Timber stood in every direction. The dark soil grew food without a plow, and the cleared patches held no stones to break a blade or a back. The sun stayed warm through the year. Rain came hard, passed, and left everything growing. There was no one to fight for the ground.

A city could stand here, not merely a village, and begin eating from its own shore. Dren had spent his life among people who would have wept for a tenth of what he could see.

Nobody had sent him or believed him, but he had found the answer and then spent months eating it before he could say so aloud.

“This is it,” he said, to nobody. “This is the place.”

The beach went quiet again. “Who am I telling?” he said.

He started planning that night and didn’t really stop for months.

Sheo came first. He’d stood in James’s front room calling the trip suicide, then handed Dren a sword, a bottle, ribbons, and everything else he could spare. Dren put a house for him above the water on black soil, with his wife and children inside. He gave it a broad roof for the wet half and a yard near the river. The children would learn the place faster than Dren had. They’d be up the tall trees in a day, and he’d never get them down.

Over months he laid out the settlement house by house. He placed neighbors together, left lanes between them, and kept the lowest ground clear for floods. He never once placed a house for himself. When he noticed, he let the omission stand and widened the plan.

West Outpost could come, every last family. Then Drenovar and Sotha Bal, which deserved the coast more than anywhere. He found room for the seven at Dry Fork, sitting in dust and guarding a road to nothing. In his head he brought the lot of them and gave them the shore.

For weeks he decided whether wells were needed, where boats might come in, and which fruit stands to leave or clear. He walked the beach counting paces and marked ground that didn’t belong to him for people who didn’t know it existed.

Every version of the plan struck the same wall.

Twenty-five kilometers separated Drenovar from the trees. Then came a month of the other thing. He’d crossed it once, alone and carrying everything he owned. It had taken his sword, pack, tent, water, six weeks of his life, and most of a leg. He’d come out only because a flood picked him up and threw him toward the coast.

He pictured Sheo’s children under that canopy and stopped. You didn’t walk children through it. You didn’t take anyone’s mother into waist-deep swamp with hornets in the air and thorns below. Graves would begin in the first week, and whoever remained would be right to hate the man who led them there.

He stopped there each time. On bad nights he went one step further: he couldn’t get back either.

He had no idea where he was. The storm had thrown him into dark water, the current had carried him for hours, and he’d followed it past a waterfall and canyon for days. He didn’t know the distance. He didn’t know the direction. He wasn’t even certain it was the same river he’d entered. If it wasn’t, walking upstream could lead somewhere else entirely.

To test it he’d have to go back into the green on this leg, into the place that had eaten a fit man with a full pack and returned him in pieces.

He’d be dead in a week. Two, if he was lucky, and dead farther in.

Suppose the miracle came twice and he walked out onto the north road. He could stand in Drenovar with his beard to his chest and tell them: paradise, thirty days through the wall, past the graves, through the place that gave nobody back. He could already see the faces turned toward him and then away.

He knew exactly what he’d have been told, because he had already been told it, on a porch in Ral Koda with a bottle going back and forth between them.

There’s nothing out there, Dren.

He’d said you don’t know that, and Sheo had said I know what I’m looking at, and that had been the end of it.

Beyond the known ground lay a jungle and an ocean, not the demon Dren had imagined on the porch in Ral Koda. He had settled the old argument, but no one back home would take his word for it.

As far as Dren knew, nobody in any settlement speaking his language knew this coast existed. He had found it with no route he could describe in or out. After sitting with that for a long while, he looked at the ship.

Most evenings he climbed the hull with the glass, swept the sea’s horizon, then followed the long pale coast south until haze took it.

Somebody had come here from somewhere, and they hadn’t walked. He didn’t know what he was looking for on the horizon, but he kept looking.

Eight years went by, and most of them were good.

There was no winter. Dren waited for one through the first year, watching for the slant of light and the first cold night, but neither came. The sun kept nearly the same hours and the air stayed warm after dark. Instead the coast had a wet half and a dry half. Rain divided one part of the year from the other, and soon that was enough to mark time.

He kept cutting daily marks into the door frame for a year and a bit. The row went down one side, crossed the bottom, and started up the other. Then he missed days, then a week. One morning he stood there with the knife and realized he hadn’t cared about the date for some time. He stopped cutting. After that he counted wet seasons, not always accurately.

Storms came in the wet half, black and loud, with the sea standing up and rain going sideways. During the first, Dren wrapped himself around a cabin beam and waited to be killed again. The wreck didn’t shift, but the storm found plenty to damage. Water came through seams, line chafed, wedges loosened, and canvas tore.

After each blow he cleared standing water, drove the wedges tight, patched the broken panes, and warmed tar to work into exposed wood and rope. At the start of every dry half he laid damp canvas and line in the sun, checked the lashings, and replaced what had gone soft. The ship kept leaning where it had landed, but it lasted because he tended it.

He moved the best tarred rope, lighter line, pulley blocks, folded canvas, and sealed black-grit casks into the highest, driest rooms. Everything sat on raised timbers away from the deck. He still didn’t know what the grit did. He knew damp ruined things, and every wet season he checked the cask seams and waxed them again.

He ate from the jungle and kept stored food for injury or a failed season. The old dried fruit changed once its barrel was open; before the first wet half ended, it smelled wrong, and he buried what remained. Then he scrubbed the sound barrels with hot river water and put them back to work.

He sliced his own fruit and dried it on racks in the sun or high over a smoky fire. Some kinds molded, some turned to leather, and a few stayed good for months. He packed those into the cleaned barrels and sealed travel portions in waxed cloth the way the ship’s stores had taught him. By the third year, every barrel of dried fruit aboard held his own harvest.

He understood the crossbows in an afternoon. They were bows, and the crank merely made him stronger than his arms allowed. Crates of bolts waited in the armory. He hunted the tree line in the mornings, learned where animals crossed the good ground, and built a blind there from palm and canvas. Traps took longer. For months he found them sprung, empty, or dragged away, until he learned where to put his hands and how little sign to leave.

The ship carried hooks, line, and a folded net, while the bay held more fish than he could eat. At low water he waded out with the net and often returned with more than he wanted. He salted the extra and laid it on racks beside the fruit. Some spoiled before he learned the work. Later batches dried hard and clean enough to last a season.

He built a fire pit above the tide line and cooked there most nights. The sea came in and went out while he ate, and the ship leaned black against the last light behind him. On clear evenings he carried his meal onto the hull and watched the empty horizon through the glass.

He buried all eight in the second year. He’d lived with them a long time by then. He greeted the first man on his way through the hold and stepped around the three beside the iron tubes as if they were broken cargo. During one wet season, that began to sit wrong.

Over a week he brought them out one by one, up from the hold or forward rooms, along the tilted decks, and through the hole. He wrapped each set of bones in canvas before lowering it to the sand. Carrying them was slow on the bad leg, but digging took longer. He cut eight holes in the soft ground above the tide line with a plank, an iron scrap, and his hands.

He stacked stones over the graves and set a board at each head. Their names were beyond him. He didn’t know them, and he couldn’t read the writing that might have supplied them. The boards stayed blank. When the row was finished, he stood at one end and said he was sorry. He told them they too had been a long way from home and that somebody had remembered enough to put them in dry ground.

As he worked, he thought of the north road out of Drenovar: little mounds, stacked stones, names carved into boards for people who’d entered the jungle. He’d passed a hundred without stopping to read one. Now he wondered who had dug each hole and whether any of those people had cursed the ground as much as he did.

His wife returned to him in small things and at odd hours: a turn of phrase, the particular sound she made when annoyed, the way she moved a bowl out of his reach when he picked at the food too early. A memory could stop his hands halfway through gutting a fish. He’d mourned her for years before leaving, and time had worn the worst edges smooth, though it had not removed them.

His grief for James was unfinished. Dren had spent a week in the boy’s shack with his face in his hands. He’d cried for him on the rock in the jungle, drunk and exhausted. Then survival had taken every hour, and Dren had let it. Two weeks after seeing James thrown into a common pit, he’d been waist-deep in swamp water screaming at hornets. On the coast there was no next danger. Whenever James came to mind, Dren reached for a net, a whetstone, anything that needed his hands. He did that for months.

In the middle of an ordinary afternoon, Dren would remember the boy wrapping a woman’s vegetables by feel, never looking down, proud of the stall he had built for himself. He’d remember walking up and saying how are you doing, son, then wasting the visit on grain quotas, Veyloth, and a man at Dry Fork. He could hear his own voice carrying on while James worked. Dren would sit wherever he stood until the memory loosened, sometimes for an hour, sometimes until the light changed.

Sheo hurt differently because Sheo might still be alive. Dren pictured him going home with the cart, returning to the north road to ask after a man nobody had seen, then waiting through a season and another. To Sheo, Dren was the dead one, a man who’d walked north and never returned. By now he would have stopped waiting. Somebody would have put up a board, and Sheo might have carved the name himself. Dren spent years looking for a word for missing a man who was missing him, and never found one.

He learned the ship as he would a house, including which boards complained before rain and which doors swelled shut in the wet. During storms he sorted the captain’s bound books, loose sheets, and packets tied with ribbon. He couldn’t read a word. Still, after enough seasons he recognized marks that returned and could tell one hand from another.

One word appeared on papers and packets, cut into the beam over the cabin door and painted beneath the growth on the stern. He found it in enough places to decide it was the ship’s name. He worked out a pronunciation that was probably wrong and used it anyway, first as a joke and then as naturally as the name of any house.

He studied the charts for years. He spread them across the fixed table, weighted their corners with coins, and used the glass to follow coastlines too small for his eyes. He learned every shape by heart without knowing what land any shape represented. On long rains he could close his eyes and trace them in the air.

Sometimes he sat in the sun with one of the three small iron tubes. He worked the lever, watched the claw fall, and studied the open end, the hole on top, and the little pan beneath it. He tried the black cord in different positions. The stock wanted his shoulder, and when he sighted along the tube at the tree line he felt close to an answer. Then the pieces separated again in his mind. He never reached it. After an hour he’d return the thing to its fitted case exactly as he’d found it and go mend a net or sharpen a blade.

He grew older. He’d been forty-something on the porch in Ral Koda, squinting into the white and arguing with Sheo about a demon. Gray appeared at his chin first, then spread through his beard. His hands hardened, the skin at his eyes creased, and his back began charging for mornings spent bent over nets and traps.

The leg recovered enough to carry him all day, though it carried him crooked. The bone had never broken; the damaged meat had knitted around a hollow that stayed weak. He kept the stick because he liked it. In wet weather the leg stiffened and ached, and he accepted the price.

Most days he was happy, which surprised him whenever he stopped to notice. He ate and slept well, stayed warm, worked in daylight, and kept a fire at night. There was always something useful to mend, catch, dry, or move before rain. He’d had worse years in houses full of people.

One thought remained and grew louder with time. It always came in the same sentence.

I should go back and tell them.

The sentence came while he salted fish, climbed for fruit, or lay full-bellied in the dark listening to rain strike the hull. It waited behind good days as readily as bad ones. At last he sat on the sand, put both choices in front of himself, and argued them aloud because there was nobody else to argue with.

He could stay. He would eat, fish, and grow old in the best place he’d known until one morning he didn’t rise. There would be nobody to wrap him in canvas or dig a ninth hole, so he wouldn’t join the eight behind the sand. The wreck would lean until storms and rot finally brought it down. Nobody at home would learn what he’d found, and the fruit would keep dropping onto an empty coast.

Or he could go back: up a river that might be the wrong one, through the jungle on an old bad leg, and probably die under the green. He could already picture another nameless mound beside the north road, assuming anyone found enough of him to bury. On the sliver of a chance he came through, he would stand gray-bearded in Sotha Bal and describe paradise beyond the wall to people who’d look at him as he would once have looked at such a man.

He sat with those choices, went to bed, and checked his traps in the morning. For a long while he told himself he was staying. He never noticed when he began drying more fish than he could eat.

The needle frightened him longer than anything else aboard. He had stepped back from it on the first day and kept his distance for most of a year. It sat in its brass bowl on the captain’s table. Whenever he passed, he turned the frame as a person might press a bruise to see if it still hurt. The bowl remained level, the needle swung, and its point returned to the same patch of wall.

Eventually he tired of fearing a bowl. He carried it down the beach, into the trees, out to the blind, and home again, holding it level in his palm and watching for a change. He took it out at night. He covered it with canvas. He tried it in hard rain, then carried it to the bottom of the hold where no daylight reached. He set it on a stump and walked around it in a circle. The needle didn’t care where Dren stood or what he could see.

For a season he compared it with the sun most mornings. He marked where the light rose, checked the needle at midday, and checked again as the sun went down. The pattern held until he understood: the needle pointed north. North itself wasn’t new. Dren had used sunrise, sunset, roads, and signs all his life. A needle that found north under cloud, in a storm, or at the bottom of the dark hold was new.

He sat down when the lesson landed. In the jungle, every trunk and thorn had pushed him aside until he lost the direction he meant to hold. He’d tied ribbons, run blind, and later found another traveler’s ribbon without knowing where either trail led. From the rock he had walked every angle, looking for one difference in the green, because he had no fixed reference.

With the bowl in his hand he couldn’t have walked straight through solid trees. He still would have needed to turn around trunks, pools, and thorn walls. But he could have measured each turn, kept working back to one bearing, and known whether hours of struggle had carried him forward or in a circle. For a long time that thought returned whenever he looked at the needle.

Afterward he used it like a knife, a tool too useful to leave on the table. He checked the places he already knew first. At the wreck, the open sea lay west. The beach and the green edge beside it ran north and south, near enough, for as far as he could walk. When storms hid the sun, the same facts remained.

The charts taught him something their writing didn’t. Through long wet afternoons, he followed strange coastlines with a finger. Blue stayed on one side, pale land on the other, and tiny marks repeated along the margins. He learned how their makers had drawn the world from above, as if the roof had been lifted away and a bird could look straight down.

Maps weren’t unknown at home. Clerks and officers kept them, and Dren had seen roads, districts, and allotments inked on paper. Census and Excise could bury a desk in documents about places. He had never made a map or carried one on a journey. He’d only seen such sheets behind counters; nobody had handed him one before a walk.

A place to Dren was the time needed to reach it and what happened on the way. Sotha Bal was two days and a dry stretch. Dry Fork lay north along a road with little shade. Ral Koda was a day beyond the checkpoint and glare in your eyes. On the ship’s charts, distance and every turn of land held still against the same directions whether anyone walked there or not.

He studied that arrangement for years before thinking to use it on home. One wet season, with rain hammering the panes and no dry work outside, he turned a chart over and began his own.

The ship supplied ink, fine pens, and the blank backs of sheets no reader Dren knew could use. He put Veyloth in the middle because Veyloth sat in the middle of everything he knew. The road went south to Sotha Bal. The Glare filled more paper than he expected. Drenovar went north, then the jungle edge above it.

He used the needle to square the drawing. His memory still supplied the distances, badly, but each place could now sit against an actual bearing. He no longer had to draw north merely as the top of whatever road he happened to remember.

He sat back. The drawing was wrong, and he spent the rest of the season finding the fault. It was the tree line.

He’d drawn the jungle as a bar across the top of the page, running off both edges, because that was how its southern face had looked from the north road. He remembered standing beneath it with his neck tipped back. Green ran forever left, forever right, without a break. The line on his paper copied what his eyes had told him.

Paper couldn’t hold forever. Every coastline on the ship’s charts ran on for a distance, bent, met another line, or ended. Not one escaped the sheet on both sides and asked the reader to pretend it continued without limit. Dren put down the pen.

The jungle had more than one edge. He knew because he was sitting on one. Here its western edge stopped at the beach and ran north–south beside the water. The green ended cleanly while sand and sea continued. Somewhere to the south, that edge had to turn and meet the east–west face above Drenovar. After that he couldn’t stop looking south.

He’d swept that coast for years without knowing what he wanted from it. Now he was useless for a month, climbing the hull at every change in the weather and staring until his eye watered. Forty kilometers was usually beyond the glass; haze swallowed the view first. Three or four mornings a year, though, the air went hard and clear and distant things sharpened. On those mornings he braced the tube before sunrise and followed the coast as far as it allowed.

The green got lower. Not all at once. The hundred-floor trees gave way to shorter growth. That growth became patchy, with brown showing between. At the limit of the glass, dark green stopped and pale ground continued beside the water. The coast itself did not stop.

The jungle ended perhaps forty kilometers south, maybe less. Beyond it lay ground the color of the Glare. Dren watched that distant edge on every clear morning for two years. He learned which notch in the hull held the tube steady and where the green ought to appear when haze lifted. Only then did he trust his eyes.

He spent three more seasons on paper, doing arithmetic and guessing. People back home treated Ghor Vhuul and Mount Gor Luut as the western end of useful country. Beyond the mountain lay pale ground everyone called the Glare’s northern cousin: dry, empty, and without reason to cross. From Gor Luut’s shoulder it looked as if it continued forever.

No road crossed it on any map Dren had seen, and no traveler he knew had claimed the other side. There had been easier ways to die than walking west with your water on your back. Even Dren had accepted the emptiness without checking. He had recently learned the cost of deciding what a place held by looking from its edge.

He drew the jungle’s southern boundary east and west above Drenovar, using the needle to square it. Then he carried that line west: past Veyloth, past West Outpost, past Ghor Vhuul and the mountain, into the blank part of the page where his maps and memory both stopped. Down the left side he drew his own coast north to south, the sea on its western side.

The coast was west. Dren sat with the needle beside the paper and checked that conclusion until there was nothing left to check. If the sea had been north, it lay beyond the full depth of the jungle. If east, it lay past Trovgar and the farthest market anyone he knew used. West put it beyond Gor Luut, where his people had already decided there was nothing worth finding.

The river had carried him in darkness. He had awakened, walked downstream, and reached an ocean without knowing which side of home it lay on. Without the needle, the coast would still have been merely somewhere, and somewhere could not guide a second journey. Now it had a bearing. He drew pale ground beneath the jungle’s western end, running out from Gor Luut toward the coast.

The lines met on his paper. The shape closed. Dren followed it with one finger, slowly at first and then again.

South along the beach until the green ended and turned east. Then east beside the jungle’s southern boundary, with pale ground under his boots and the wall on his left shoulder. If the seam continued as he’d drawn it, it would lead behind Mount Gor Luut. He might have to go around the mountain, but Ghor Vhuul lay on the other side, joined by known roads to Veyloth.

His best reckoning gave him two hundred kilometers along the seam, perhaps three hundred or more. He paced known distances on the beach, compared them with days he remembered walking, and reached a different answer each time. Whatever the number, it meant weeks on a bad leg. The country looked dry and open, without a canopy or swamp underfoot—the sort of ground he understood, if appearances held.

He went outside and stood on the sand with the paper still open behind him. The jungle didn’t run forever; it only ran past the roads from its known southern face. He’d tried to force a way through it and nearly died. The drawing offered a simpler instruction: go around.

The possible route began behind Mount Gor Luut, exactly where the roads and useful maps stopped, then followed a boundary nobody from home had reason to seek. That explained why the way had remained absent from every account Dren knew. It also left one more fact for him to say aloud.

There was an ocean out here.

It held fish and bordered miles of white sand, fruit, timber, and a fresh river that ran all year. The ocean itself couldn’t ease anyone’s thirst; Dren had learned that with his first mouthful. The river at its edge could. Settlements were rationing wells and burying people for water a few hundred kilometers from that drinkable current, with Gor Luut and a reach of pale ground standing between.

Dren kept himself cautious. He’d been alone long enough to build a tidy answer from bad pieces and had nobody across the table to knock holes in it. The jungle might not end where he thought. The pale ground might run a thousand kilometers, turn north, or become something he hadn’t imagined. Mountains could block the seam. Water could fail for weeks. His route rested on a smudge through a brass tube, foreign charts, a needle, and memories drawn years after the walk.

It was a guess, and Dren knew how much guessing lay inside it. Still, it was worth scouting. If the seam held, if the ground stayed open, and if water crossed it often enough, families might be able to walk the route. Dren would have to travel it first, mark every water source, measure the distance, and come back before calling it safe for anyone else.

The needle made that attempt possible. The boundary would bend, break, and double back. He might have to leave it to cross bad ground or circle a ridge, with nothing in the open country to steer by. Each morning he could take a bearing. When the edge pushed him away, he could work back to the same line instead of choosing directions at random as he had in the jungle.

He sat with his back against the hull and the needle level beside him. As far as he knew, no one in the settlements of his language knew of this ocean or had drawn a possible route to it. The dead ship had come from somewhere, but its words and coastlines belonged to people beyond anything Dren knew.

Meanwhile he was getting older each wet season. The leg had settled into all the improvement it was likely to give him. A year would come when two or three hundred kilometers of pale ground was no longer a hard trip but an impossible one, and he had no way to know which year.

He looked down the beach, south, where the coast ran off into the haze.

Then he went to see how much dried fish he had.
