The Turquoise Coast

He tried to stand.

His good leg took it. The other one did not, and he went down into the sand on his hands.

So he crawled.

The dry sand was blistering. White and fine and soft as anything he’d ever touched, and hot enough that he could only keep his palms down for a few seconds at a time. He went in bursts. Hands down, drag, hands up. Elbows when the palms couldn’t take it.

It was a beautiful place. That was the thing he couldn’t get his head around. The beach went off in both directions further than he could see, white the whole way, and the trees came right down to the edge of it and stopped in a clean green line, and everything about it was soft and open and warm, and he was dying in the middle of it.

Twenty feet on, the sand went darker and firmer and cool, and after that it was easy.

The water came up and touched his hands.

He put his face in it and drank.

It came back up immediately. He gagged, spat, gagged again, and kept spitting long after there was nothing left to spit, his eyes streaming, his throat burning.

Salt.

He got up on his elbows and looked at it, all of it, out to the horizon, more water than existed in the entire world he came from, and not one mouthful of it any good to him.

He started laughing again. It didn’t last.

The river was behind him. That was the only reason he was still breathing, and he understood that clearly enough to turn around and crawl the whole way back up the beach to the mouth, where the current was still pushing out fresh, and he lay in the shallows there and drank until his stomach hurt.

He lay in the shallows a while longer.

That was when he noticed the thing missing.

Nothing was on his face. Nothing was in his ears. Nothing was landing on the back of his neck, nothing was in the corners of his eyes, nothing was crawling on him anywhere. The air was moving off the water, steady and cool and clean, and there was no weight in it. No haze of them. No hum.

He put his hands over his ears anyway, out of habit, and took them away, and there was still nothing.

The wind came off the water and dried the sweat on him and he had not been dry in a month.

And there was a sound. Big and slow, coming in and going out, over and over, never stopping and never quite the same twice. It filled up the whole place. He had never heard anything like it in his life and he didn’t have a word for it, so he just lay there and listened to it, and after everything, it was the sound that nearly finished him off.

Then the salt got into his leg.

It went up through him like something driven in with a hammer. His back arched off the sand and his ears rang, a high hard tone that blotted out the water and the wind and everything else, and he made a noise he didn’t recognize as his own.

It took a long time to come down.

When it did, he lay there panting, and he looked at the leg, and he thought about it.

Nothing had ever hurt like that. Not the thorns, not the hornets, not the bite itself. And in his experience, things that hurt like that were doing something.

He dragged it back into the water on purpose.

He screamed into the sand for as long as it took, and then he did it again, and by the third time he could hold it there without screaming, and the leg looked no worse for it.

That would have to do.

The shape was still out there.

The water had gone down while he lay in it. He didn’t know why. It was further out than it had been and there was more wet sand than there was before, and he had no explanation for that at all, so he stopped trying to have one and used it.

He got himself up onto the knee of his good leg and started shuffling.

Where he had to cross water it was shallow, but shallow for a man on his feet, not a man on one knee. He went in up to his chest and crawled the rest, dragging the bad leg, mouth up, taking it slow.

It got bigger the whole way.

By the time he was close it wasn’t a shape anymore, it was a thing standing over him. Leaning hard onto one side, the top of it higher than anything he’d ever stood next to, higher than the towers on the Veyloth wall. And it was made. That was what he couldn’t stop looking at. Thousands of pieces of wood, cut and shaped and fitted against each other with no gaps, banded with iron, ropes coming off it thicker than his arm, all of it black with tar and crusted over below the waterline with something hard and grey and living.

People made this. A lot of people. Over a long time.

He came around the leaning side and there was the hole.

It was big enough to walk into. The edges of it were splintered and the wood was pushed inward, folded in on itself, everything bent the same direction, into the ship.

Whatever had done it had come from out there, and it had come fast.

Dren didn’t have anything to make of that. He looked at it a moment longer and let it go.

Inside, in the dark, he could see barrels. Stacked and lashed and gone over on their sides. Crates behind them.

Barrels meant something was in them.

He put a hand on the wood and started pulling himself up.

The hole put him in the dark.

Light came in behind him and got about ten feet and quit. The floor wasn’t a floor, it was a slope, the whole ship lying over on its side, and everything that had been stacked in here had gone down to the low end and piled up against the wall.

The smell hit him before his eyes adjusted. Wet rot, tar, salt, something gone sour underneath all of it, and running through the middle of it something sweet and sharp that made his head go light.

The low side had water standing in it, black and still. Everything down there was swollen and split and furred over. Crates come apart. Sacks gone to slime.

The high side was dry.

The lean had lifted half the hold up out of the water, and whatever was lashed along that wall was still lashed there, sitting above the line, and it had been sitting there a long time.

Getting up to it was the problem. He couldn’t stand and he couldn’t crawl up a slope like that on wet boards. What he could do was grab. The whole wall was strung with rope, lashings run through iron rings to hold the cargo, and rope he understood. He went up hand over hand, dragging the bad leg behind him, resting where the netting gave him somewhere to hook an elbow.

He got to the first barrel and put his fingers under the rim and pulled.

It didn’t move. He braced his shoulder and pulled until something tore under his nails and it still didn’t move. The lid was set down in and the hoops held the whole thing shut like it was one piece of wood.

He sat back and looked at what he had. Everything up here was sealed. That was the reason any of it was worth anything.

Everything broken was at the bottom.

Going down was easy. Too easy. He let go of the rope and the slope took him and he went into the black water at the low end harder than he meant to, and it came up over his chest and stank.

He felt around in it. Staves floating loose, soft as bread. Sacking that came apart in his hands. A crate that folded in when he leaned on it.

Then something under the water with an edge.

He pulled it up. A barrel hoop. Flat iron, a long curve of it, come off a cask that had rotted out from inside it. There were more down there, a whole nest of them where the wood had gone and left the iron behind.

He took the straightest one and put it down the front of his shirt and started back up the rope.

That took him a while. Both hands, one good leg, resting every few feet with his forehead against the boards.

At the top he got the flat end of the hoop down between the lid and the rim and put his weight on the curve of it, and used his own body as the rest of the lever, and the wood cracked.

He worked it around the rim, cracking as he went, and the lid came off in pieces.

Inside it was full of small dark things.

He picked one out and looked at it. Wrinkled. Dry. Sticky. He had no idea what it was. He put it in his mouth anyway because he was past the point of caring what killed him.

It was sweet.

Not a little sweet. Sweet like nothing he had ever eaten in his life, sweet in a way that went straight up the back of his skull, and his whole face pulled tight and his eyes ran before he’d even swallowed it.

He put in a handful.

He was crying before the second handful and he didn’t stop and he didn’t stop eating either. He sat there on the slope of a dead ship with his ruined leg out in front of him, shoving them in with both hands, chewing and sobbing and swallowing and reaching in for more, and it went on like that for a while.

Then his stomach turned over.

It came up fast. He got a hand down and it all came back out onto the boards, and he stayed there over it, shaking, ribs cramping, gut twisting on itself so hard he couldn’t straighten out.

He’d eaten a fistful of nothing in twelve days and then put a month’s worth in at once. His body didn’t know what to do with it.

He lay on the slope with his cheek on the wood until the cramping let go of him.

Then he sat up and ate three of them. Slowly. Waited. Ate three more.

That worked.

He kept at it that way for a long time, three at a time, and he cried on and off through all of it, and eventually the crying stopped being about the food.

Later, when he could move again, he went along the wall with the hoop.

Another barrel, orange things, soft and leathery and almost as sweet. One packed with grain. One with something hard and dried and salty that he had to gnaw at. Crates of it. More barrels behind those, and behind those, more, going back into the dark past where the light reached.

And casks. A lot of casks, and one of them had split at the seam, and that was where the sharp smell was coming from. He knew that smell. He got a finger in it and tasted it and it burned all the way down, and it wasn’t whisky, but it was close enough to whisky that he knew exactly what he was holding. Sweeter. Rounder. Something else underneath it that he didn’t have a name for.

Whoever these people were, they made drink.

He sat with that for a moment.

Then he left it alone. On an empty stomach it would have put him flat.

He sat there in the middle of it, on the slope, in the dark, with his hands sticky and his stomach cramping and his leg going in the salt.

He was not going to die today.

That was the first time he’d been able to say that since he stepped between two trunks at the river.

He looked back into the dark end of the hold, where the light stopped and the barrels kept going.

He’d get to it. Not today.

The water came for him that night.

He heard it before he understood it. A change in the sound of the hold, a slow filling, and when he put a hand down where he’d been sitting his fingers went into it.

It was coming up.

He got to the rope and hauled, dragging the leg, and he was on the high side with his arm through the netting before he could think about what he was doing. Below him the black water climbed the slope, over the ruined crates, over the place where he’d found the hoops, up and up until it was most of the way to him.

The ship was sinking. That was the only answer he had. He’d crawled into it and it had waited for him and now it was going down with him inside it, and there was nothing he could do about it, so he hung in the netting in the dark and waited to drown.

It stopped.

It sat there a long while. Then it started going back down again, slow, the way it had come, and by the time the light came through the hole in the morning the low side was black mud and standing puddles and the ship hadn’t gone anywhere.

He didn’t understand any of it.

That afternoon it came again.

The third time it came he stopped being afraid of it and started watching it. It came in and it went out. It came in again. It did not care what he did. It was the first thing since he walked into the jungle that happened on a schedule, and he had no idea what the schedule was, but he could count it.

So he counted it. It was something to hold on to.

The next days were bad in a different way than the jungle had been bad.

The food was killing him and saving him at the same time. Everything he ate came out of him one end or the other, mostly the other, and he learned quickly to do it down on the low side where the water came in and took it away again. His gut cramped so hard he’d curl up on the boards and stay there. The fever was still in him. He’d sweat through a whole tide and then shake through the next one.

But he kept eating. Three at a time. Then five. Then a handful of grain soaked in fresh water he carried back from the river mouth in a small cask, corked, which took him most of a day to work out and was the proudest he’d been in a month.

And slowly, in pieces, it started staying in him.

He could feel it happening. That was the strange part. He could feel himself coming back, an hour at a time, like something being poured into a container that had been empty so long it had forgotten the shape.

The leg was the problem that would not go.

The salt was doing something but it was not doing enough. The red was still up past his knee and the smell was still there and he could not put weight on it.

He thought about the cask.

Men back home poured whisky into a cut. He’d seen it done, and he’d had it done to him once, on a hand, a long time ago, and he still remembered the man who held him down for it. He didn’t know if it actually worked. He knew that everybody did it.

The salt had helped and the salt had burned. This burned worse.

He filled his hands with it and poured it into the bite.

It was worse than the salt.

He went over sideways on the boards and lay there with his fists shut and his teeth locked, and it went on much longer than the sea had, and when he could hear again he was somewhere down on the slope with no memory of getting there.

He did it again the next day.

He went through the crates after that, properly, one at a time with the hoop. Folded canvas, heavy and stiff and clean where it had been packed tight. A chest of shirts, linen, better cloth than anything he’d ever owned, packed dry. Coils of light line. A pot of something black and tacky. Crocks sealed over with wax, and inside those, something thick and gold that was sweeter than the dark ones in the barrel and stuck to everything it touched.

He ate some of it. Then he put some on the leg, because it was sticky and it held the cloth down, and that was the only reason.

He washed it with the spirit, packed it with the gold stuff, and bound it in strips of clean linen with a wrap of canvas over the top. Then he did it again the next day, and the next, and every day after that.

The smell went first. Then the red came down off his thigh, then off his knee.

The hole stayed a hole. It closed slow and it closed ugly, and there was a dead patch in the middle of his calf where the muscle didn’t come back.

He found the stick without looking for it, up under the netting where it had wedged when the ship went over. A shaft of pale hard wood about as long as he was tall, with an iron head fitted onto the end of it, a hook on one side and a point on the other.

He had no idea what it was for.

He turned it over a few times and decided what it was for.

The first time he got up on it he made it four steps before he had to sit down. The next day he made it out through the hole and onto the sand.

He stood there in the open with the wind coming off the water, on two feet, leaning on the shaft, in a stranger’s clean shirt, with food in him.

He was going to keep the limp. He knew that already. Every step he took for the rest of his life was going to go through that leg and the leg was going to charge him for it.

He didn’t much care. He’d take it.

He told himself he was looking for a better stick.

That was the reason he gave himself for four days, while he ate and slept and changed the dressing and got a little further up the beach each morning. A better stick. Something straighter. That was all.

He’d been in the bottom of that ship for over a week and he had not once gone past where the light stopped.

He knew it. He just kept not doing it.

The problem he could admit to was light. Past ten feet in it was black, and he wasn’t going to crawl around in a wreck he couldn’t see. So he solved that instead, which was easier than solving the other thing.

The pot of black tacky stuff took a flame and held it. He’d known that the moment he smelled it. He cut a strip off the canvas, wound it around the end of a broken stave, worked the tar into it with his fingers, and then sat there with it in his lap because he had nothing to light it with.

He found what he needed the next morning in a small wooden box in a chest, wedged in a corner where the ship’s lean had thrown it.

Flint. A curl of steel. A pinch of char cloth gone to dust.

He turned it over in his hands for a while. Everything on this ship was strange. The wood was cut strange, the rope was laid strange, the cloth was better than anything he’d ever owned and nobody at home could have made it.

And they lit their fires exactly the way he lit his.

He struck it and it caught first time.

The dark end of the hold went back further than he’d guessed.

The torch only gave him a room’s worth at a time, so the ship came at him in pieces. Barrels standing in ranks where they’d been chocked, all still lashed. A wall of crates. Something that had been sacks of something and was now a black crust with things growing out of it.

The first one was down on the low side, half in the mud.

He saw the shape of it before he understood it and he stopped moving entirely. Held the torch out.

Bones. A long shape of them lying the way a person lies when nobody has moved them. Cloth still on it in patches, gone the color of the mud. One arm out. Boots. The boots had lasted better than the man.

Dren stood there for a long time.

He had been eating and sleeping and healing about thirty feet from this.

He said something to it. Not a prayer exactly, he didn’t have one that would fit. Just a few words, out loud, because it seemed wrong not to.

Then he went on, because the alternative was going back.

The end of the hold was a wall with a door in it, and the door was in the wrong place. The whole world in here was turned a quarter over. Floors were walls. Walls were floors. Doorways came at him sideways and he had to work out each one like a puzzle, hooking the shaft into whatever would hold and hauling himself through with the torch in his teeth.

Above the hold there was another deck, and this one was long and open and it ran most of the length of the ship, and it had things in it that stopped him dead.

Iron. Enormous tubes of it, each one longer than he was tall and thicker through than his own body, black, sitting in heavy wooden frames on small wheels. They were tied to the walls with rope as thick as his wrist, and there were a dozen of them, and one had torn loose when the ship went over and gone straight through the deck below.

He had never seen so much iron in one object. He had never seen so much iron. At home iron was a knife and a pot and a hinge. This was iron poured out like it cost nothing.

He put his hand on one. Cold. He looked in the open end of it, which was a hole big enough to put his head in, and there was nothing inside but soot.

Beside it, stacked in a wooden rack, were iron balls. Perfectly round, heavy as anything, dozens of them.

And square holes cut in the side of the ship, at even spacing, each one with a hinged lid. One tube for each hole.

He looked at the tube, and the hole, and the balls, and he could not put it together. He stood there a while with the torch up trying to make it be something, and the only thought he could get to was that it took a great many people to make this, and they’d wanted it very badly, and whatever it was for, it wasn’t for growing food or carrying water or keeping warm.

At the end of the deck there was a row of small casks lashed down, sealed and dry, and he stopped at those.

They were heavy. Heavier than they looked. He held the torch down close and turned one with his boot and there was no sound of liquid in it at all.

Whatever was in them, it wasn’t drink.

He’d come back for those in daylight.

There were more of them up here.

Two together against the wall. One on its own by the fourth tube, and that one had come apart, the bones scattered along the boards the way things slide when a ship goes over.

He crouched by that one with the torch and saw the iron in it. A short thick point with a spine down the middle, sitting in among the ribs where nothing like that had any business being.

He knew what that was. Different shape than any he’d seen, but he knew it.

He looked back down the deck at the tubes, and the square hatches, and the iron balls, and something moved a step closer to making sense and then stopped.

These people didn’t die of the sea.

The next door had been barred from the inside. He got through it and found a narrow room with racks along both walls, and the racks were full.

Swords. Rows of them, in a wooden frame with slots cut to hold each one. Axes with long hafts. Heads of maces. Bows built into wooden stocks with a crank on the side, a dozen of them hung in a line.

Some of it was rust. Most of it was not. Whatever the black stuff on the blades had been, it had done its job.

He took one down.

He knew swords. The last one he held had been put into his hands in his son’s front room by a man who was telling him not to go, and he had lost it in the river with everything else, and he had felt naked every hour since. He put his thumb along the flat and turned it in the torchlight and looked at the grain in the steel and the fit of the guard.

It was better than his had been. It was better than anything he’d held.

He stood in a dead man’s armory with a dead man’s sword and felt something come back into his chest that he’d thought was gone.

He took a belt off a hook and put the sword on it and did not take it off again.

On a shelf at the end, laid into a fitted wooden case, were three of something else.

He got one out. A tube of iron on a wooden stock, a bit longer than his forearm, shaped to be held against the shoulder the way you hold a bow-stock. A hole bored into the top of the tube near the closed end. A curved lever underneath. A little iron claw on a spring that snapped forward when he pulled the lever, and gripped in the claw, a stub of blackened cord.

He worked the lever a few times and watched the claw come down. Nothing happened.

He looked into the open end. Soot.

Then he looked at the tube in his hands, and back down the deck at the twelve enormous ones sitting in their frames, and then at his hands again.

Small ones and big ones. The same thing, made twice.

He still had no idea what the thing was.

He put it back in the case exactly the way he’d found it and closed the lid, and took the sword instead.

He kept going up.

At the high end of the ship, all the way back, there was a door that was better than the other doors. Carved. Fitted. He got it open and put the torch through and then he just stayed there in the doorway.

Light. Real light, coming in through a wall of small panes set in lead, most of them still whole, throwing squares of sun onto a room that had gone over on its side and stayed beautiful anyway.

The floor was polished. There was a table fixed down to it with brass at the corners. A chair. Shelves with rails to keep things in. And built into what was now the wall above him, a bed. A real bed, with a frame, and on it, held down by its own weight, bedding.

He got up there and put his hand on it.

He had never felt anything like it in his life. He didn’t have a word for the cloth. It was cool and it ran through his fingers like water and it made the linen shirts feel like sacking, and he sat on a wall in a wrecked ship holding a sheet and turning it over and over.

There was no one in the room. The bed was made.

The rest of it he went through slowly, with the sword on his hip.

Rolls of stiff paper in a rack. He got one open on the tilted table and held the torch over it.

It was a drawing. Shapes with edges, blue on one side and pale on the other, lines running out from them, little marks all over it in a hand that wasn’t any writing he knew.

Not a drawing. He could tell it wasn’t, and he couldn’t say how. The edges were too particular. Nobody invents a coastline that fussy.

It was a map.

He looked for the Glare, because the Glare was the biggest thing in the world. It wasn’t on it. He looked for the wall of green. Not on it. He put his finger down in the middle of the paper and moved it around looking for anything, any shape at all that he had ever heard described, and there was nothing.

He stood back from the table.

Either the whole thing was a lie somebody drew for fun, or everywhere he had ever been was somewhere off the edge of it.

There was a brass bowl hung in a ring so that it stayed level whichever way you tipped it, and in the bowl, under glass, a needle. He turned the bowl. The needle stayed where it was. He turned it again, faster, and the needle swung and settled and pointed at exactly the same nothing it had been pointing at before. He took his hands off it and stepped back from that, too.

A wooden frame with two glass bulbs and sand in the bottom one. That one he understood, and it was almost a relief.

A brass tube with glass at both ends. He looked through the wrong end first and the room went small and far away and he nearly dropped it. Turned it around. Held it up to the window.

The tree line, four hundred yards off, came and sat down in front of his face.

He put the tube down on the table very carefully, the way you put down something that might be listening.

A small locked chest under the table came open with the hoop. Inside it, coins. Hundreds of them, gold and silver, with faces stamped on them, faces of people who had presumably been someone. He ran his hand through it and let it fall.

It was the most money he had ever seen and it was worth exactly nothing.

He sat down on the tilted floor of the captain’s cabin, in the sun, on a polished deck, with silk in one hand.

Then he started laughing.

He’d been living in the bilge.

A week. More than a week. Sleeping on wet boards on a slope in the dark above a dead man, eating out of a barrel with his fingers, going down the low side to be sick where the tide could reach it, hauling himself around on a rope in the black.

And this had been up here the whole time. Windows. A bed. Sun.

“I’ve been living in the bilge,” he said out loud, to a room with no one in it, and it set him off again.

He brought the bedding down off the wall that night and made a place in the corner where the floor met the window frame, and he lay down on cloth that a dead lord had slept on, with the sea coming in and going out below him, and a sword within reach, and food in his stomach.

Best sleep of his life.

He rolled one of the heavy casks down out of the ship a few days later and opened it on the sand, in daylight, well away from anything.

Black grit. Fine and dry, a little shine to it, and it smelled foul in a way he didn’t have a word for. He rubbed it between his fingers. Put one grain on his tongue and spat it straight back out.

Not food. Not drink. Not medicine.

He put the lid back on, rolled it into the shade under the hull, and stopped thinking about it.

Then he just lived there.

That was the whole of it for a long time. He woke up in a bed. He ate. He went down to the water and stood in it and let the salt have the leg, which hurt less every week and then stopped hurting and just ached. He slept in the afternoon because there was nothing stopping him. He ate again.

He got the cabin the way he wanted it. Righted what would right, wedged what wouldn’t, cleared the glass, worked out where the sun came through and put the bedding where it landed in the morning. He hauled the good crates up out of the hold one at a time, over weeks, because there was no hurry and it gave him something to do.

He started cutting marks into the door frame. One a day. He’d gone a month in the jungle without knowing what a day was, then a week in the hold counting the water going in and out, and now he had a wall with a number on it, and he checked it the way a man checks a coin in his pocket.

The weight came back on him. Then it kept coming, past where he’d been when he left, because he was eating like a lord and doing nothing. His beard got long. His skin went from burned to brown. The dead patch on his calf stayed dead, and he stopped thinking about the limp because the limp was just how he walked now.

He was, by any measure he had ever been taught, a rich man.

He was also completely alone, and he talked out loud most of the day. To the room, to the leg, to the man in the mud down in the hold, who he’d started saying good morning to on the way past.

And somewhere in those months he noticed that the water was a color.

He hadn’t seen it before. The first time he looked at that bay he was dying on his face in the sand, and after that it was the thing that ate his leg every morning, and a man doesn’t admire what he’s screaming into. It took half a year of not being in trouble before he actually looked at it.

He was standing in it up to his knees one morning, doing nothing in particular, when the sun came up over the trees behind him and got onto the bay.

Close in, over the white sand, it went pale and bright and lit from underneath, blue and green at the same time and neither one of them, so clear he could count his own toes through it. Further out it deepened in bands, one after another, each one a shade further down, until it went dark and serious out past the point. It moved and the color moved with it. It came up the beach in a thin sheet, hissed, and slid back, and left the sand shining behind it.

He’d seen a stone that color exactly once. On a ring, on the hand of a man who came through Sotha Bal with money in a bad year. He remembered looking at it longer than was polite.

The whole bay was that stone. Miles of it.

He stood in it a while.

“Turquoise,” he said.

Then he laughed at himself, because he was doing it again. Standing in water at the one hour it went strange and putting a name on it out loud, like the name mattered, like there was another soul on this coast who needed telling.

He’d done it once before. Up to his shins in something rotten, in the dark under a hundred floors of green, mostly out of his mind.

He’d been right that time too.

He got in the habit of taking the glass up onto the high side of the hull in the evening.

He’d sit up there with his back against the rail and go along the sea first, left to right, slow, the whole horizon.

Nothing. Every night. Water, and then more water, and then the place where it stopped being water and started being sky, and nothing on it anywhere.

Then he’d turn around and go along the tree line, because he’d done exactly this on the rock, walking every angle of it looking for anything different, and finding nothing every time.

He never found anything there either. No smoke. No cloth on a branch. No one. In half a year of looking he saw nothing on that whole coast that a person had put there, except the ship he was sitting on.

But now he could actually see.

And what he saw, once he started really looking, was that the trees along the beach had things hanging in them.

The tall ones with the bare curved trunks and the burst of leaves on top had clusters of hairy brown balls up under the crown. Big ones. A dozen to a tree. He’d looked straight at those trees for half a year and never once looked up them.

There was a low broad-leafed thing back off the sand growing in stands, and hanging off it were bunches of yellow fingers, forty or fifty to a bunch.

Down at the edge of a clearing there were spiked knobs sitting low on the ground out of a nest of blades, gold at the bottom and green at the top.

There were red things. There were purple things. There was a tree further down the beach absolutely loaded with something orange, so heavy the branches were bowing.

He went along the whole tree line with the glass, and then he went along it again, and he sat there a long time with the tube in his lap.

He had no idea what any of it was.

But he knew what it looked like. It looked like an orchard nobody was minding.

He was careful about it, because he’d learned that lesson twice.

He watched first. Sat in the shade at the edge of the trees with the glass, mornings, for the better part of a week, and he watched what ate what. Birds went at the orange ones. Something with hands went up a tall tree and came down with one of the hairy balls and knew exactly what to do with it. Whole flocks came in for the yellow fingers.

That was good enough for him.

He brought one of the yellow ones back and turned it over. Skin, thick and soft. He tried a corner with his teeth and it was bitter and awful and he nearly threw it. Then it split where he’d bitten it and something else came out of the middle.

He sat down on the sand and ate it and he did not say anything for a while.

The hairy ball took him most of an afternoon and the sword. He got through the outside, then the shell under it, and it cracked and a whole cup of clear water came out over his hands and he got his mouth to it fast. Sweet. Cold, somehow, out of a thing that had been in the sun all day. And a white meat inside the shell that he scraped out with the point of the sword and ate until his jaw hurt.

The spiked knob looked like it would take his hand off and tasted like the best thing anyone had ever made.

He took it slow the way he’d learned in the hold. Small amounts, wait a day. Nothing came for him. Nothing made him sick. He kept going.

Within a month he had thirty of them. Fruit, tubers he dug out and found were better cooked, greens that were fine and greens that made his mouth go numb and taught him where the line was. He worked out which trees were near which, and where the good stands were, and he wore a track from the ship up into the trees, and then a second one, and then a third.

The place fed him. It did it without being asked and it did not run out.

The thing he could not get past was that it had been here the entire time.

He had lain down in a swamp thirty days from this beach with his gut folded in on itself, eating a fistful of dried strips and then nothing at all, going blind with hunger, and there had been food over his head the whole way.

He’d looked up at it. He remembered looking up at it. Plenty of fruit high up, he’d thought, and kept walking, because he was a plains man and he didn’t know one green thing from another and the not knowing had nearly killed him.

He wasn’t angry about it. It was too big to be angry about. He just sat with it now and then, out on the sand in the evening, the way you sit with something that ought to have gone differently.

And somewhere in there, without any particular day to it, he understood what he was looking at.

He had walked into that jungle to find something. Anything. Supplies, water, ground, whatever was in there that a man could carry back out. That was the whole of the errand. He had stood in a hall in Veyloth and said it out loud to a clerk in a purple coat and been laughed at, and then he had gone and done it himself, because there was nothing left of his own life to lose over it.

He had gone and found out.

He stood on the sand one evening and looked at it properly, the way a man looks at ground he’s thinking of buying.

Flat open shore, miles of it, firm behind the sand line. A river coming out of the trees at the end of it, running clean and hard all year, more fresh water in a day than Veyloth saw in a season. Fish in the shallows, jumping, and he knew fish. He’d caught them as a boy in the creek at Ral Koda, back when Ral Koda had a creek, and there were more in that one bay than in every water he had ever put a hand into. Timber standing in every direction. Ground that grew food without a plough or a prayer or anyone asking it to. Sun the year round. Rain that came and went and never once looked like drought.

No stones to clear. No one to fight for it.

You could put a city here. Not a village. A city, on the water, and it would feed itself the day it opened.

He’d spent his whole life around men who’d have wept for a tenth of it.

It was the answer. Nobody had sent him and nobody had believed a word of it, and he had gone and found it anyway, and then he had sat on it for months eating fruit and cutting marks into a door frame before he would let himself say it out loud.

“This is it,” he said, to nobody. “This is the place.”

Then it got quiet in the way it always got quiet, and he said, “Who am I telling?”

He started planning that night and he did not really stop for months.

Sheo first. Sheo, who had stood in a dead man’s front room telling him it was a suicide mission and then handed him a sword and a bottle and a pack of ribbons and everything else he had. He’d get a house here, up off the water, on the good ground where the soil went black. Sheo’s wife in it, Sheo’s kids in it, and not one of them hungry ever again for the rest of their lives.

The kids would have this place learned in a week. Kids learn that stuff in a week. They’d be up those trees inside a day and he would never get them down again.

He laid out the whole settlement that way, over months, house by house, and he never once put a house down for himself.

He noticed that eventually. He didn’t do anything about it.

Then it got bigger, the way it does when you’re on your own with a lot of time.

Not just them. West Outpost, the whole village of it, every last one, they were finished out there anyway and everybody knew it. Drenovar. Sotha Bal, and Sotha Bal deserved it more than anywhere. The seven at Dry Fork sitting on a checkpoint in the dust, guarding a road that went to nothing.

Bring them all. Bring the lot of them out here and give it to them.

He worked it in his head for weeks. Where the houses went. Where you’d put the wells and whether you’d even need wells. Which stands you’d leave alone and which you’d clear. He walked the beach counting paces and marking out ground that didn’t belong to him, for people who didn’t know it existed.

And every single time, the plan walked into the same wall.

Twenty-five kilometers from Drenovar to the trees. Then a month of the other thing. He’d done it once, in his prime, alone, carrying everything he owned, and it had taken his sword, his pack, his tent, his water, six weeks of his life and most of a leg, and the only reason he’d come out at all was that a river had picked him up and thrown him.

You don’t walk children through that. You don’t walk anyone’s mother through that. You’d bury them in the first week and the ones still standing would kill you for it.

He’d get that far and stop, every time.

And then, on the bad nights, he’d get to the part underneath it. The one he kept for last.

He couldn’t get back either.

He had no idea where he was. That was the plain truth of it and it did not improve with turning over. The storm had put him in the water in the dark and the river had carried him for hours, most of them with his arms around a log and some of them with nothing at all, and then he’d walked days downstream past a waterfall and a canyon and out here. He didn’t know how far. He didn’t know which way. He wasn’t even certain it was the same water he’d walked into, and if it wasn’t, then going up it would take him somewhere else entirely.

And to try it he’d go back in. On this leg. Into the thing that had eaten a fit man with a full pack and given him back in pieces.

He’d be dead in a week. Two, if he was lucky, and dead further in.

And say he wasn’t. Say the miracle came twice and he walked out of the trees onto the north road with his beard down his chest and stood in the square at Drenovar and told them. Paradise, he’d say. Thirty days that way, through the wall, past the graves, through the thing that has never given anybody back.

He knew exactly what he’d have been told, because he had already been told it, on a porch in Ral Koda with a bottle going back and forth between them.

There’s nothing out there, Dren.

He’d said you don’t know that, and Sheo had said I know what I’m looking at, and that had been the end of it.

He had been right. He’d been right the whole time and about the wrong thing, and it had taken nine years and a jungle and an ocean to find out how right, and there was still not one man alive who would take his word for it.

So that was the shape of it.

He had found the thing. He had actually found it. And he was the only person alive who knew, and there was no road to it, and no road out of it, and no way to say it that anyone would believe.

He sat with that for a long while.

Then he looked at the ship.

He went up on the hull most evenings after that, with the glass, and he’d put it on the sea and go along the horizon, and then swing south and follow the coast, that long pale line of sand running away and away until the haze took it.

Somebody had come here from somewhere. They hadn’t walked.

He didn’t know what he was looking for out there.

He kept looking anyway.

The years went by and they were good ones.

There was no winter here. He waited for one the first year, the way you wait for a thing you’ve known your whole life, and it never came. The sun went up and came down at more or less the same hour every day of the year and the air stayed warm at night. What it had instead was a wet half and a dry half, and after a while that was how he thought about time.

He gave up the door frame eventually. He’d cut marks every day for a year and a bit, and then he missed a few, and then he missed a week, and one morning he stood there with the knife in his hand and worked out that he did not care what day it was and had not cared for some time. So he stopped. After that he counted wet seasons, and he was not always sure about those either.

The storms came in the wet. Big ones, black and loud, the sea standing up and the rain going sideways, and the first one had him braced in the cabin with his arms around a beam waiting to be killed all over again.

The ship didn’t move. It had already been wrecked. There was nothing left for a storm to do to it. It had come to rest where it came to rest and it sat there through every blow that ever hit that coast, leaning, patient, and after the third or fourth one he stopped bracing and started sleeping through them.

He ate out of the jungle.

That was a rule he made early and kept. The barrels were for a bad year, an injury, a season that didn’t come. He’d nearly died of an empty stomach once and he was never going to be that man again if a decision of his could prevent it, so the stores stayed sealed and lashed and he went out and got his food like a person.

He allowed himself the little dark sweet ones now and then. Not often. A handful on a good evening, sitting up on the hull with the sun going down, eating them one at a time and taking his time about it.

He got good at the rest of it faster than he expected.

The crossbows he worked out in an afternoon, because it was a bow and he knew bows, and the crank was just a way of being stronger than you are. There were bolts by the crate. He hunted the tree line mornings and got better at it every month, and eventually he had a hide built up on the good ground where the animals came through, and it fed him.

Traps were slower to learn and he lost a lot of meals figuring it out. Then he stopped losing them.

There was fishing gear aboard, line and hooks and a net folded into a chest, and there was more fish in that bay than a man could eat. He’d take the net out at low water and come back with more than he wanted, and he learned to salt and dry the rest of it, because the ship had taught him that too, in a barrel, years back.

He built a fire pit up on the sand above the tide line and he cooked out there most nights, with the sea going in and out and the ship black against the sky behind him.

He buried them in the second year.

He’d been living with them a long time by then, saying good morning on the way past, and one wet season it started to sit wrong with him. So he brought them out. One at a time, over about a week, up out of the hold and along the deck and down through the hole, wrapped in canvas, carrying them the way you carry something that used to be somebody.

He put them above the tide line in the soft ground behind the sand, in a row, and he dug every one of those holes himself with a plank and his hands.

He stacked stones over them and set up boards.

He couldn’t write their names. He didn’t know their names and he couldn’t have read them if he’d had them. So the boards stayed blank, and he stood at the end of the row when he was done and said the only thing he had, which was that he was sorry, and that they’d been a long way from home too.

He thought about the north road out of Drenovar the whole time he was doing it. The little mounds. The stacked stones. The names carved into boards for people who’d gone in and never come back.

He’d walked past a hundred of those without stopping.

The grief came in its own time and it took as long as it took.

Some of it was clean. He mourned his wife the way you mourn a person, in pieces, at odd hours, over small things. A way she said something. The particular sound of her being annoyed with him. He’d be halfway through gutting a fish and have to stop for a minute. He’d had years with that one before he ever left. It had worn itself smooth.

James was the other thing.

He had never grieved his son. That was the plain fact of it, and it took him a long time out there to see it. He had sat in the boy’s shack for a week with his face in his hands, and then he had got up and walked into the jungle, and the jungle had not given him one hour to think about anything except the next step. Two weeks after a hole in the ground outside Drenovar with a few hundred people thrown into it like rubbish, he was standing in swamp water screaming at hornets.

It came for him out here. It took its time about it and it came anyway.

It would arrive in the middle of an ordinary afternoon and he would sit down wherever he happened to be standing and it would have him for an hour, sometimes two. The way the boy could wrap a woman’s vegetables by feel without once looking down at his hands. That he’d been proud of that stall. That Dren had walked up to the counter and said how are you doing, son, and then spent the whole visit talking about grain quotas and Veyloth and a man at Dry Fork, and had not said one thing worth keeping.

He was fifty-something years old, alone on a beach on the other side of the world, finally burying a boy he had already buried once and never sat down for.

And Sheo he mourned like a man mourns his own arm, and that one was the strangest of the three, because Sheo was not dead. Sheo was alive out there, going about it, and to Sheo he was a man who walked north and didn’t come back. By now he would have stopped waiting. Somebody would have put a board up.

He was grieving a man who was grieving him. There was no word for that. He looked for one for years and never found it.

He learned the ship the way you learn a house.

He went through every document in the cabin, and there were a lot of them, bound books and loose sheets and folded packets tied with ribbon. He couldn’t read one word of it. But he sat with it anyway, most wet seasons, when there was nothing else to do, and after enough years he could pick out shapes that came back.

There was one word that turned up everywhere. On the papers, on the packets, cut into the beam over the cabin door, and painted on the stern outside where he’d found it under the growth.

He decided that was the ship’s name. He worked out a way to say it that was probably wrong and he used it anyway, and after a while he used it the way you use the name of a thing you live in.

The charts he studied for years. He’d get them out on the table and hold the glass on them and follow the coastlines with his finger, and he learned every one of those shapes by heart without ever knowing what a single one of them was.

And every so often he’d take out one of the three iron tubes and sit with it on the step in the sun.

He never stopped being fascinated by them. He’d work the lever and watch the claw come down. Look into the open end. Turn it over and study the hole bored into the top, and the little pan under it, and try to think what a man was supposed to do with it. He’d hold it up to his shoulder because the shape of it wanted to go there, and sight down the length of it at the tree line, and feel like he was almost onto something, and then lose it again.

He never got it. Not in all those years. He’d sit with it for an hour and then put it back in the case exactly the way he’d found it, the way he had the first time, and go and do something he understood.

He got older out there.

He’d been forty-something on that porch in Ral Koda, squinting into the white, passing a bottle back and forth and arguing with Sheo about a demon. He was not that anymore. The beard came in grey at the chin first and then everywhere. His hands got harder and his back got worse.

The leg had come good, mostly. The bone had never broken. It was only ever the meat, and the meat had knitted itself back into something that worked, and he could walk on it all day if he wanted to. He just walked on it wrong. He kept the stick because he liked the stick, and in the wet season the whole leg went stiff and told him about it, and that was the price and it was a fair one.

He was, on the whole, happy. That was the strange part and he knew it was strange. He ate well, he slept well, he was warm, he was safe, he had work in the day and a fire at night and nothing on this earth to be afraid of.

He’d had worse years in a house with people in it.

But the thought never left. It sat at the back of everything, and it did not get quieter with time. It got louder. And it was always the same eight words.

I should go back and tell them.

He’d be salting fish and it would arrive. He’d be lying in the dark listening to the rain come down on a wrecked hull with a full stomach and it would arrive.

And in the end, after a very long time, he laid it out in front of himself properly, out on the sand, out loud, because there was nobody to do it with.

There were two ways this went.

One. He stayed. He lived out the rest of it here, in the best place he had ever seen or heard of, eating fruit and catching fish and getting old, and one day he’d not get up. He’d go into the soft ground behind the sand with the others, if there had been anybody left to put him there, and there wasn’t. Nobody would ever know he’d found it. The ship would sit there leaning until it fell in, and the fruit would keep dropping, and the whole thing would go back to being a place nobody had ever seen.

Two. He went back.

Back into it. On this leg, at this age, up a river he was not sure was the right river, through the thing that had taken everything off him once already and would be waiting. Almost certainly to die in there, somewhere in the green, another mound with no board on it. And on the sliver of a chance that he came out the other side, to stand in the square at Sotha Bal with grey in his beard and tell people there was a paradise out past the wall, and to be looked at exactly the way he’d have looked at any man who said it.

He sat there on the sand a long time with those two.

Then he got up and went to bed, and in the morning he went out and checked his traps, and he told himself he was staying.

He told himself that for a long while.

And in all that time he never once noticed that he’d started drying more fish than he could eat.

The needle was the thing on that ship he was afraid of longest.

He’d stepped back from it the first day and he kept stepping back from it for the better part of a year. It sat in its brass bowl on the table and he’d turn it as he went past, the way you press a bruise, and it would swing and settle and point at the same nothing it had been pointing at before he touched it.

Eventually he got tired of being afraid of a bowl.

He carried it out. Down the beach, up into the trees, out to the hide, back again, holding it flat in his palm and watching it the whole way. He took it out at night. He took it out in the rain. He set it on a stump and walked a circle around it and it did not care about him at all.

Then he checked it against the sun, because that was the only thing he had to check it against, and after a season of doing that most mornings he had it.

The needle pointed north. Every time, everywhere, in the dark, in a storm, under cloud, at the bottom of the hold where you couldn’t see anything at all.

He sat down with that when it landed.

He had spent a month in that jungle not knowing which way he was facing. That was the whole of what had happened to him in there. That was the ribbons, and the circling, and the standing on a rock walking every angle of it looking for a difference. He had run screaming through the green with no idea which way he had come from, and he had picked directions at random for weeks because it made no difference which one he picked.

With this in his hand he could have walked a straight line. Any line. Picked one and held it and come out the other side of that jungle in a week.

It was a very long time before he could look at the thing without that going through him.

After that he used it every day, the way you use a knife. He learned his own coast with it. The sea was west. The beach ran north and south, near enough. The tree line behind him ran east and west, near enough, and it did that for as far as he could walk in either direction.

The charts taught him the other half, and it wasn’t anything written on them.

He’d had them out on that table for years by then. Long wet afternoons with the rain going over and nothing else to do, holding the glass on some coastline he would never stand on, following it with a finger. He never learned a word of the writing. He learned the thing underneath the writing, which was how these people saw the world when they sat down to draw it.

They drew it from above. Like a bird.

That was not how anyone he had ever known thought about anywhere. At home you knew a place by how long it took to get there and what was on the way. Sotha Bal was two days and a dry stretch. Dry Fork was north and then more north. Nobody had ever drawn it. There was nothing to draw it on and no reason to.

But these people put water on one side and land on the other and ran a line between them, and the line went where it went whether anybody was walking on it or not.

He looked at that arrangement for years without once thinking about home.

Then one wet season, with nothing better to do, he turned a chart over and started drawing his own.

He had their ink and their pens and the backs of a hundred sheets nobody was ever going to read. He put Veyloth down first, because Veyloth was the middle of everything he knew. Then the road south to Sotha Bal. Then the Glare, which took up more of the sheet than he expected. Then north. Drenovar. Then the tree line above it.

He used the needle to lay it out square, and that was the first time in his life he had ever known for certain which way anything was.

Then he sat back and looked at what he’d done, and it was wrong, and he knew it was wrong, and he spent the rest of that season working out why.

It was the tree line.

He’d drawn it as a bar across the top of the page and run it off both edges, because that was how it had looked the day he stood at the bottom of it on the north road with his neck craned back. Forever left. Forever right. No breaks.

But you can’t draw forever. The paper ends.

And not one coastline on any of their charts went forever. Every one of them went along, and turned, and went somewhere else, and stopped being one thing and started being another. Those people would not have known how to draw a thing that had no end. They’d never have believed in it.

He put the pen down.

The wall had an end. It had to. It had one right here, because he was sitting on it. He had walked out of those trees onto a beach and the green had stopped, dead, in a line, and the water had carried on without it.

If it ended here, it ended over there too.

After that he could not stop looking south.

He’d been taking the glass along that coast every evening for years without knowing what he was looking for. Now he knew, and it made him useless for about a month.

Forty kilometers is a long way to see, even from up on a hull with good glass, and most days the haze took it long before that. But there were mornings, three or four in a year, when the air went hard and clear and everything a long way off came up sharp, and on those mornings he was up there before the sun with the tube braced on the rail, going down that coast as far as it would let him.

The green got lower.

Not all at once. It thinned. The hundred-floor stuff gave way to lower stuff, and the lower stuff went patchy and brown, and out at the very limit of what the glass would give him there was a place where the dark green simply stopped and the land went pale, and the pale went on, and the water went on beside it.

He watched that edge on every clear morning for two years before he would let himself believe it.

The jungle ended down there. Forty kilometers south of him. Maybe less. And past it the ground went the color of the Glare, and the coast kept running.

The rest of it he did on paper over about three more seasons, and most of it was arithmetic and guessing.

Everybody knew where the world stopped in the west. It stopped at Ghor Vhuul, and behind Ghor Vhuul was Mount Gor Luut, and past the mountain nobody went, because everybody knew what was past the mountain. More of the same. Dead pale ground running out to nothing, the Glare’s northern cousin, no water and no reason.

Nobody had ever gone and checked. Why would you. You could see it from the shoulder of the mountain and it looked exactly like what it was.

Dren sat with his pen a long time over that, because he had recently learned something about looking at a thing and knowing what it was.

He drew the tree line again, laid square off the needle, running east and west above Drenovar. He carried it west. Past Veyloth. Past West Outpost. Past Ghor Vhuul, past the mountain, out over the empty part of the page where nobody had ever been.

He drew his own coast down the left side, north to south, because now he knew that it was.

And that was the whole thing right there. That was the one fact the rest of it hung off.

West. Not north. Not east. West.

If that sea had been north of the tree line, it sat on the far side of the jungle and there was no road to it and there never would be. If it had been east, it was out past Dry Fork and beyond anything that mattered to anybody. It was worth something only because it was west, out past the last place a man went, in the one direction the whole world had already crossed off.

And he would never have known which. A river had taken him in the dark and he had woken up somewhere. Without the needle, the ocean was just somewhere, and somewhere is not a place you can walk to.

And he drew the pale ground under the jungle’s western end, running out from Gor Luut to meet it.

They met. On his paper they met, and the shape closed, and he sat there looking at a thing that had a way around it.

South down the beach until the green quit. Then east, with the dead ground under his boots and the jungle up on his left shoulder, walking the seam where the two of them ran along each other. Follow that seam far enough and it would put him out behind Mount Gor Luut, and Ghor Vhuul was on the other side of the mountain, and from Ghor Vhuul a man could walk to Veyloth with his eyes shut.

He worked the distance as best he could and got something near two hundred kilometers along the seam. Might be three hundred. Might be more. Weeks of it, in open dead country, on a bad leg.

Dry country. Open country. No canopy, no water underfoot, no clouds of them, nothing overhead you couldn’t see straight through.

The kind of country he was born in.

He got up and went out and stood on the sand for a while.

That was the whole of it. It was not the jungle. The jungle was a wall, and everybody he had ever known had spent their lives standing at the south face of it saying it went forever, and it did not go forever. It just went further than anybody was willing to walk.

You don’t go through it.

You go around it.

And the reason not one living soul had ever found that out was that the way around began behind Mount Gor Luut, at the exact spot where the known world quit, in the one direction everybody had already agreed was nothing.

Which meant something else, and that one took him a while to say out loud.

There was an ocean out here.

Miles of it. Fish in it. A whole coast of white sand and running fresh water and fruit falling off the trees, a few weeks’ walk west of Ghor Vhuul, and not one person between Veyloth and the Glare had the faintest idea it was there. They were dying of thirst a few hundred kilometers from more water than the world could hold, and the only thing standing between them and it was a mountain everybody used as a full stop.

He was careful with himself about it, because he had been alone a long time and he knew what a man alone can talk himself into.

He might be wrong. He was probably wrong somewhere. The green might not end where he thought it ended, and if it did, the pale ground behind it might run a thousand kilometers instead of two hundred, or turn north, or turn into something he had not thought of at all. He had built the entire thing out of a smudge at the far end of a brass tube, a stack of charts of a world that was not his, a needle in a bowl, and a drawing he had made himself out of things he half remembered.

It was a guess. He knew exactly how much of a guess it was.

But it was a road.

That was the part that finished him. For years the plan had died in the same place every time, on the same objection, which was that you cannot walk a village through that jungle and any man who tries it is a murderer. That objection was gone. If he was right, there was a way to bring people here that a child could survive. Dry ground, a long walk, and water at the end of it.

And he could walk it. That was the other half of what the needle had handed him. Two hundred kilometers of seam, or three, bending and gapping and doubling back the way an edge does, through open country with nothing on it to steer by, and he would be able to hold a line the whole way and know that he was holding it.

He had tried it the other way once. Picking a direction because one was as good as another. It had cost him a month, everything he owned, and most of a leg.

He sat down on the sand with his back against the hull.

He was the only person alive who knew there was an ocean. He was the only person alive who knew there was a way to it. And he was getting older every wet season, and the leg was not getting better, and there was going to come a year when he could not have done it even if he wanted to.

He looked down the beach, south, where the coast ran off into the haze.

Then he went and looked at how much dried fish he had.
