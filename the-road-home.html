The Road Home

He spent a season getting ready, and most of that was not packing.

He walked the whole business over and over. What he’d say. Who he’d say it to. What they’d ask him, and what he’d have in his hands when they asked.

Because he knew exactly what he was. A man who walked into the green nine years ago and came back out of the dead country in the west with grey in his beard, saying there’s a paradise past the wall, follow me. He’d have been run out of Sotha Bal inside the hour. He’d have run himself out.

So he packed the argument first.

The tube went in wrapped in canvas, padded on every side, and he checked it twice a day the whole way home. The needle in its bowl. One chart, the best of them, rolled tight into a leather case. A sheaf of the papers, the ones with the most writing on them, because writing was writing and nobody at home could make those marks. A length of the silk off the captain’s bed, folded small. Coins, two dozen of them, gold and silver, faces and letters stamped into both sides.

And food nobody had ever seen. Dried fruit out of the barrels, sealed in the wax the way he’d found it. He packed more of that than made any sense for eating.

Then the rest, which was easy, because he’d been living well for eight years and he had everything.

Ruck and a second bag. Dried fish and dried meat enough for six weeks, with room to add as he went. A skin and two corked casks for water. The crossbow, and bolts, and line and hooks and a folded net. Flint and steel. Canvas and cord for a tent that would go up in four minutes. Cord, needles, wax, salt, a pot. Two spare shirts and a good coat, because he could not remember what nights were like away from the sea. A small cask of the spirit, for the leg and for anything else.

The sword on his belt. The stick in his hand.

He left the three iron tubes where they were. He got one out on the last morning and sat with it on the step in the sun the way he had a hundred times, worked the lever, watched the claw come down, and he still did not have the faintest idea. Too heavy to carry a thing he couldn’t use.

He put it back in the case exactly the way he’d found it.

He went along the row of graves and stood at the end of it a while, the boards still blank, and told them he was sorry again, and that he’d been glad of the company, which was true and had been true for years.

Then he shut the cabin up properly. Bedding folded. Charts back in the rack. The chest closed. He wedged the door so the weather would stay out of it.

He did all of that carefully, because he was coming back. That was the entire point of leaving. He was going to walk out of here and come back up this beach with two thousand people behind him.

He stood on the sand and looked at the ship a minute. Then he turned south and started walking.

The walk was the easiest thing he had ever done in his life.

That was the part he wasn’t ready for. He kept waiting for it to turn on him and it never did.

He made about fifteen kilometers a day and could have made more. Firm wet sand at the tide line the whole way, flat, no thorns, no water to wade, nothing overhead. The sea on his right. Sun in the morning, shade in the trees when he wanted it, wind off the water at the hottest part of the day.

He fished in the evening and usually had something inside the hour. He set snares at the tree line at dusk and collected them at dawn and about half the time there was meat in them. He went up into the green when he saw a stand he recognized and came down with an armful. He knew thirty things by sight now and could call them at forty paces.

He camped on the beach. Tent up, fire going, fish on it, the water doing what it always did. He’d sit out with a cup of the spirit and watch the light go, then sleep like a dead man on soft sand under canvas.

The bugs left him alone out on the open beach with the wind moving.

It was, and he knew exactly how stupid it would sound if he ever said it to anybody, a lovely trip.

He caught himself enjoying it on about the third day and had to sit down.

The last time he’d travelled he had eaten a fistful of dried strips in twelve days and drunk out of a swamp and lost his mind in the dark and been chewed on while he was unconscious. Now he was strolling down a white beach with a full pack and a cup in his hand.

Same man. Same country. He just knew things now.

Three days south, he saw it.

He’d been checking with the glass every morning and every evening, and it had been getting realer since the first day, and on the third afternoon he did not need the glass at all.

The green ended.

Not thinned. Ended. The tall stuff came down in stages over the last few kilometers, hundred floors to fifty to twenty, then a band of low scrub, then a line, and past the line the ground went pale and open and dry and stayed that way as far as he could see.

And the coast kept running south without it.

He stood at the edge of it with his hand on his stick.

Then he sat down right there on the boundary and did not move for a long time. He put one hand back into the shade under the last tree and laid the other one flat on the hot pale dirt, and he sat like that, one in each, like a man checking that both of them were real.

Eight years. Three or four clear mornings a year, up on a hull with a brass tube, watching a smudge.

He had been right.

He said it out loud a few times, in a few different ways, and none of it was dignified, and there was nobody there to see it.

He camped at the seam that night with the jungle at his back and the dead ground in front of him, and he slept badly, the way you sleep when something good has happened.

In the morning he took out the needle, turned himself east, and started walking.

The seam ran the way he had drawn it.

Not neatly. Nothing out there was neat. The edge of the green bulged south where water came out of it and pulled back north where it didn’t, and there were days he walked a clean line with the wall on his left shoulder, and days he was ten kilometers out in the pale country going around a tongue of trees, and days where the boundary went vague and scrubby and he couldn’t have told you which country he was in.

That was what the needle was for. He took a bearing every morning and he held it, and when the edge pushed him off it he went around and came back on, and he never once had to guess.

He walked beside that jungle for weeks.

That was the thing that got him, more than the beach had. Off his left shoulder, a few hundred yards most days, was the same wall he had walked into nine years ago. The same hundred floors of green. The same wet black smell coming off it in the morning. He could hear it at night, the whole enormous machine of it, howling and clicking and screaming away in the dark, going about its business.

It could not touch him.

He was walking past it on dry ground with a pack on and boots that were staying dry, and there was not one single thing it could do about it.

He talked to it some evenings. Not kindly.

The country he was actually in was hard and open and dead and he got on with it fine. Scrub, rock, dust, hard light, cold at night, which he had forgotten about entirely and was glad of the coat. He rationed water properly and didn’t have to for long, because the jungle leaked. Every few days a stream ran out of the green into the sand and died twenty yards later, and he filled everything he had every time he crossed one, and never came close to trouble.

He hunted less out here and ate into the dried stores and didn’t mind.

Weeks of it. He stopped counting somewhere in the middle.

Then the ground started going up, and after a couple of days of that he was looking at mountains.

A range of them, jagged and grey and bare, running away north and south. And where they met the jungle it went strange, the green climbing up into the rock and the rock coming down through the green until you could not say where either of them stopped.

He stood and looked at that a good while, because he thought he knew what he was looking at.

There was no way through it that he could see. He went about five kilometers north along the base of it looking for one, then stopped and had a word with himself.

He had a month of food. He had water. He had legs, one and a half of them, and no reason on this earth to be in a hurry.

So he turned around and went south and walked around the whole thing.

It took him nine days and they were the easiest nine days of the trip. Flat pale ground the whole way, the range up on his left, and every night a clear cold sky with more stars in it than a man could use.

He came around the southern end of it and turned east, and after a while the ground started looking at him funny.

He knew this dirt.

He could not have told you how. Something in the color of it, and the way the scrub grew, and the particular shade of nothing on the horizon. He walked half a day arguing with himself about whether a man can recognize dust.

Then he came over a rise and saw the track.

A road. A real one. Wheel ruts and hoof marks in it, old and faint, running east and west.

He got down on one knee in it and put his hand in the ruts.

He was home. Not at home. In it. This was his country, this was the dust he was born on, and he had walked here from the other side of the world by going around the outside of the thing that everybody said had no outside.

A day and a half later he came up on Dry Fork.

He’d been thinking about it the whole way in. The officer and the six soldiers. Three tents. The hyena up on the spear that hadn’t been there the day before, that nobody had seen go up.

He came over the last rise ready to be stopped and questioned and looked at sideways. An old man with grey in his beard and a foreign sword on his hip.

There was nobody there.

The three tents were still standing. That was the first thing wrong with it. Bleached out to no color at all, sagging where the ropes had gone slack, one of them split down a seam and flapping. But standing. Right where they had always been.

No horses. No fire. No spear, and nothing on it.

He walked down into it slow, hand near the sword out of habit, and went through them one at a time.

Empty. All three. Somebody had taken everything worth carrying and left behind everything that wasn’t. No pots, no blankets, no lamps, no chests, no tack, no tools. What was left was a bootlace, a broken peg, the heel off a boot, and bones somebody had eaten off, old and clean and scattered around since by something.

Nobody had fought over this place. Nobody had burned it. Nobody had died in it. They had packed what was worth the weight and walked away and not bothered to strike the tents behind them.

The main tent was the one that got him. He had sat in this exact tent. Carpets and cushions cluttering the floor, a fire going in the middle of it, an iron pot of tea on the coals, the three of them lounging in the shade out of the heat while the officer went through the documents.

The carpets were gone. The cushions, the pot, the lot of it.

What was left was the ash ring in the middle of the dirt, gone to crust, and he stood over that and put every one of them back exactly where they had sat.

Now it was a dirt floor with garbage on it.

He stood in the middle of it and turned all the way around.

“Weird,” he said.

Nobody had been here in a long time. Not a season. Years, by the look of it. The desert had had a go at everything and mostly won.

He stood a minute longer trying to make it mean something ordinary. Moved the checkpoint. Closed the road. Ran out of money.

Then he shouldered the ruck, took a bearing he did not need, and started up the road to Sotha Bal.

Before he went he got the glass out and had a proper look around, because he could, and because it had never once occurred to him to point the thing at his own country.

Southwest, the Glare. Nine or ten kilometers off, and it came up in the tube white and shimmering and going absolutely nowhere, exactly the way it had his entire life. He held on it a while. He had been in there. Him and Sheo and the wagons and the dust and forty-odd days of it, and he had come out the far side and thought that was the hard part.

East, the road, running off between the same two low ridges it had always run between.

He couldn’t see Sotha Bal. Too far and too flat for that. But he could see the shape of the ground it sat in and he knew every line of it.

He used the glass most of the way in after that. No reason to. A hawk on a rock a kilometer out. A dust devil going along the flat. The far end of the road. Eight years and it was still the best thing he had ever owned.

He slept at the old campsite that night.

He hadn’t planned it. He was just walking, and the ground started looking familiar in the same way the dust had, and then he came around a low shoulder of rock and there it was.

The stones were still in a ring. Somebody had used it since, more than once, but the ring was the ring, and he had built it. Him and Sheo, one evening, nine years ago, walking home from Dry Fork with the annual report done and a dead hyena on a spear still sitting in both their heads, and no idea at all what was coming.

He put his fire in it and sat with his back against the same rock.

Sheo had talked most of that night. About his cousin’s boat scheme, about a girl in Drenovar, about whether the officer at Dry Fork had been lying to them. Dren remembered being tired and letting him go on. He could not now remember one single thing he himself had said, and he would have given a great deal to.

He sat there a long time with the fire going down.

He was a day from home. He had been rehearsing for a season and he ran it again from the top. The way he would say it, in what order, what he would take out of the bag and when.

He slept well. Last time for a while.

He glassed the town from the rise, out of habit, the way he had glassed the coast for eight years.

Then he took the tube down and rubbed his eye and put it back up.

There were towers.

Not stone. Scaffolding, four of them that he could see, lashed poles going up thirty feet with platforms on top and men standing on the platforms. And running between them, out across ground that had been open scrub the last time he stood here, a wall.

If you could call it that. Mud and brick and sand and sticks and whatever else had been to hand, thrown up in sections, some of it chest high and some of it twice a man, wandering along the approach in a line that had obviously been argued about.

Behind it the town had swollen. It had doubled. There was a whole second Sotha Bal out past the old edge of it, made of canvas and scrap and mud brick, packed in tight, smoke coming up off it in a hundred thin lines.

He stood on that rise a long while with the glass up.

Then he walked down.

Nobody stopped him.

That was the thing. He came in braced for a challenge, ready to be questioned by some boy with a spear, and he walked through a gap in that wall and past two men shoring up a section of it and neither of them looked at him twice.

He was an old man with a stick. Nobody has time for an old man with a stick.

The new part of town was worse than anything he had ever seen, and he had grown up poor in a bad place. Shelters built off each other three deep, no lanes between them, just gaps. Children everywhere, most of them not doing anything, sitting in the shade with the particular stillness of children who are not getting enough. Standing water where there should not be any and the smell that comes with it. Old women cooking on fires the size of a hand.

He heard the Ghor Vhuul accent before he had gone a hundred paces, and then he heard it everywhere, and he understood.

They never rebuilt it. They never even tried. What came out from under that mountain went to West Outpost, because West Outpost was the refugee dump and always had been, and West Outpost could not hold them and never could. So they had kept moving. And this is where they had fetched up, and this is where they stayed, and this is what they got.

And the town was working.

That was the part that took him longest to make sense of. Everyone was busy. Not the busy of a market. The other kind. Men splitting poles and burning the points hard. A line of women passing baskets of dirt hand to hand up onto the wall. Somebody in a yard hammering scrap into blade shapes, doing it fast and badly. A cart going through with bundled spears in it and two boys sitting up on top of them.

Sotha Bal was building weapons in the street.

He kept walking and looking for a face and not finding one. Then he did find them, three or four, people he had known, and he looked straight at them and they looked straight at him and there was nothing there at all. He was fifty-something with a white beard and foreign clothes and a limp. He had been dead for nine years.

He didn’t stop them. He wasn’t sure what he would have said.

He found Sheo in the open ground behind the wall, in the middle of about forty people, doing four things at once.

He had gone heavy through the chest and grey at the temples and he had a bad shave and a voice that had been shouting for days. He was pointing at a section of wall and arguing with a man half his age about whether it would hold, and he was doing it with the flat certainty of somebody who has been in charge of something too long.

Dren stopped about fifteen feet away.

He had nine years and a season to plan this and he did none of it. He just stood there and waited for a gap in the shouting.

The man Sheo was arguing with left. Sheo turned to find the next thing and his eyes went across Dren and past him, and then came back.

And stopped.

Everything went out of his face. All of it, all at once, the way a lamp goes out.

He didn’t say anything. His mouth moved a bit. He took one step and then stopped again, like the ground wasn’t safe, and he stood there looking, and Dren watched a man do arithmetic that would not come out.

“Sheo,” Dren said.

That did it. Sheo came across the dirt and grabbed him by both arms and held on and looked at his face from about six inches, hunting for something, and then he made a noise that was not a word and pulled him in.

He was shaking. Dren could feel the whole of him going.

“You’re dead,” Sheo said into his shoulder. “You’re dead. We put a board up. I put it up. I carved the bloody thing, Dren.”

“I know.”

“Where have you…” and he pushed him back out to arm’s length and looked at him again, the beard, the burnt hide of his face, the stick. “Where have you been?”

And there it was. The question. Nine years of it, a season of rehearsing, a bag on his back with a chart in it and a brass tube and two dozen gold coins with a dead king’s face stamped into them.

“There’s a sea,” Dren said.

Sheo blinked at him.

“West. Past Gor Luut, past all of it. There’s an ocean out there, Sheo, and a coast, and the ground grows food on its own. I’ve been living on it eight years. And there’s a way to get to it. A road. Dry ground the whole way, anybody could walk it, children could walk it. I’ve got it drawn.” He was already pulling at the strap, getting the bag around. “I’ve got it here. I can show you.”

Somebody shouted Sheo’s name from up on the wall.

Sheo’s head came around like it was on a string. He shouted something back that Dren didn’t catch. Then he turned again and his face had already changed. It was already half gone, back into whatever it had been in before.

“Dren.” He had a hand on his arm. He was gripping it. “Dren, I can’t. Not now. Not right now.”

“It’s twenty minutes.”

“There’s a column coming up from the north and it’ll be here inside two days.” He said it flatly, like weather. “Veyloth. Second time this year. Last time we lost the east side and forty people. I’ve got a wall that isn’t finished and men on it who have never held anything.”

“Sheo.”

“I know. I know.” He looked at him, and for about a second and a half he was actually there again, and he looked absolutely wretched. “You come back from the dead and I’ve got no time for you. I know what that is.”

Dren’s hand was still in the bag.

He didn’t take anything out.

The sword was on his hip. A garnet in the pommel and a guard that somebody had spent a month cutting, worth more than every blade in that town put together, and it had been six inches from Sheo’s hand the whole time, and Sheo had not seen it. His eyes had gone over it and kept going. He hadn’t seen the coat, or the boots, or the leather case, or the brass, or any of it.

There was nothing wrong with Sheo’s eyes. He just had a wall that wasn’t finished.

“Tonight,” Sheo said, moving already, walking backwards. “After dark. Find me.” Somebody shouted for him again and he half turned toward it. “Dren. I’m sorry. Tonight.”

“Sheo.” Dren got it out fast, before he went. “Your family. Are they all right?”

“Alive.” Sheo was already turning. “She’s at the house. Caius and Fimmion are at Trovgar.”

“Trovgar.”

“Veyloth put a camp up north of the market. Vey Trading Camp, they’re calling it, like it’s a place.” He said it the way a man says a thing he has said two hundred times and is sick of. “Sat it right on top of our stalls. We’ve been fighting over that market since spring. Both of them are down there now.”

And then he was gone across the yard.

Dren stood where he was.

The last time he had seen those two they had been small enough to be a nuisance. He had spent whole evenings on a beach on the other side of the world putting a house up for them on the good ground behind the sand, working out which room went where, deciding they would be up the tall trees inside a day and he would never get them down again.

They were grown. They were in a market ninety kilometers east with something sharp in their hands, over a stall.

He went up onto the wall.

Nobody stopped him. There were men up there passing baskets of dirt along a line and nobody had the attention left over for one more old man in the way.

He got the glass out and put it north.

Heat first, the whole flat swimming with it. He steadied his elbows on the top course and went along slowly, the way he had gone along a horizon every evening for eight years, and about where the road came down off the high ground he found the dust.

A long low smear of it, standing up off the track and drifting east on the wind. Not a wagon. Wagons make a thread. This was a wall of it, a kilometer of it, the kind of dust you only get when a great many feet come down in the same place one after another.

Under it, shapes.

He held on them until they came apart into pieces. Four abreast. Spear tips catching in a line, then losing it, then catching again as they came around the bend. Horses at the head of it. Two carts back in the tail.

He counted them in tens because that was how he had learned to count anything, and he lost the count twice, and both times he came out around the same number.

Three hundred. Near enough.

And they were close. He knew what far looked like now. He had spent years learning exactly what forty kilometers looked like through this tube and exactly what four hundred yards looked like, and this was neither. This was the ridge road, this side of the bend, ten kilometers out and walking.

Two days, Sheo had said.

That was not two days. That was before dark.

He took the glass down and looked at the yard below him, forty people running in every direction, and not one of them looking up at the man standing on their wall with a brass tube in his hand. He had carried it through the middle of them, and nobody had asked what it was.

He went back down.

“Sheo.”

“Dren, I told you.”

“They’re ten kilometers out.”

Sheo stopped.

“North road, this side of the bend. Three hundred of them, four abreast, horses at the front and two carts behind. They’ll be on this wall before dark.”

“Says who?”

“Says me. I watched them for ten minutes.”

“From where?”

“From your wall.”

Sheo looked at him a moment. “You can’t see the bend from my wall.”

“I can.”

And there it was, sitting in the dirt between them, and Sheo had nowhere to put it. A dead man walks in off the road in a foreign coat and tells him he can see ten kilometers. The runner had come in yesterday and the runner had said two days, and the runner was a boy Sheo had known since he was born.

“Then don’t stand here and wait for them,” Dren said, and he heard himself go too fast and could not stop it. “That’s what I’m telling you. You don’t have to hold this. There’s nothing here worth holding. West, Sheo. Past Gor Luut, around the green, dry ground the whole way and I have walked every step of it. Water that never quits. Food hanging off the trees. Empty. Nobody on it. You could put this whole town on that coast and there would still be room, and nobody would ever come up a road at you again. Pull them out. Tonight, before that column gets here. Just go.”

Sheo stared at him.

“Stop.”

“Sheo.”

“Stop.” He put a hand up. He was not shouting. That was the part Dren felt. “I am glad you’re alive. I want you to hear me say it, because I mean it, and because of what I’m about to say next.”

He waited until Dren shut his mouth.

“You’ve been gone nine years. You walk in here on the worst week of my life and you start telling me about a land over the hills where the food grows itself and nobody’s in it.” He let that sit. “Listen to it. Just listen to what it sounds like from where I’m standing.”

“It’s not like that.”

“I’ve got a wall that isn’t finished. I’ve got two children in a market ninety kilometers east that I can’t get word to. I’ve got old men out here who are going to be on that wall tonight because there isn’t anybody else.” His voice finally went. “And you want me to tell them to pack up and walk into the desert on your word. Nobody has time for this, Dren. I don’t have time for this.”

Somebody shouted his name from the gate.

“I know what you’ve been through,” Sheo said, quieter. “I don’t know what it did to you. But not today.”

And he went.

So Dren told somebody else.

He tried the woman filling jars at the trough first, because she was nearest. Soldiers on the north road, he said, a lot of them, and they are not two days out, and there is a place west with water in it, and there is a way to walk to it. She heard him out with her hands still in the water and told him he wanted to get out of the sun.

He tried two men lashing a cart across the mouth of a lane. One of them asked which way west, and Dren said past the mountains, past the green, and the man went back to the rope.

He tried a fire with four women around it and a boy stacking bundled reeds beside them. He got further with them than anyone, far enough to get the case off his back and the chart half unrolled, and one of them looked at it, actually looked, at the coastline and the marks in a hand nobody there could read, and asked what it was supposed to be.

“It’s a map,” he said.

“Of what?”

He tried a man carrying spear staves, and a man on a ladder, and an old woman in a doorway who was the only one who was unkind about it.

Not one of them argued with him. That was the thing. They did not tell him he was wrong, they did not ask him a single question, they did not want to see the tube or the coins or the sword. They looked at a fifty-something man with a white beard and a stick and a limp, come from nowhere, talking about an ocean, and they went back to what they were doing, because the thing in front of them was real and he was not.

He was not worth arguing with. Arguing costs you something.

He stopped in the middle of a street with the case under his arm and stood there while the town went around him, and he understood exactly where he was.

He had stood in a hall in Veyloth once and told a clerk in a purple coat that there was something out there worth going to look at, and the clerk had not argued either.

Nine years. A jungle. An ocean. Everything he had left in the river and everything the river had given back.

And here it was again, word for word, from people with dirt on their hands.

Dren stood in the middle of it with a bag on his back that nobody had asked to see.
