The Road Home

He spent a season getting ready. The packing came last.

He walked the whole business over and over: what he’d say, who he’d say it to, what they’d ask, and what he’d have in his hands when they asked.

He knew exactly what he was: a man who’d walked into the green nine years ago and come back out of the dead country in the west with gray in his beard, asking Sotha Bal to follow him to a paradise past the wall. They’d have run him out inside the hour. He’d have run himself out.

The proof went in first.

The brass tube went in wrapped in canvas and padded on every side, and he checked it twice a day the whole way home. He packed the needle in its bowl; his best chart, rolled tight in a leather case; a sheaf of papers covered in writing nobody at home could make; a length of silk from the captain’s bed; and the hourglass from the cabin shelf, wrapped and padded in cloth. He counted twenty-four gold coins into one pouch and set a separate small sack of silver beside it, all stamped with foreign faces and letters.

For proof of the food, he gathered and dried jungle fruit himself, wrapped it in clean ship’s cloth, and waxed the outer layer against damp. He packed more of it than he meant to eat.

Everything else had to fit in one ruck.

His food plan covered six weeks, but the ruck held only what the bare stretches required: dried fish, dried meat, and more of his fruit. Three waterskins, two rolled empty until he left the coast. The crossbow and bolts; hooks, line, and a folded net; flint, steel, needles, wax, salt, and a small pot. A square of canvas and cord for a low tent. One spare shirt and a good coat, because he couldn’t remember what nights were like away from the sea. A flask of the spirit for the leg and anything else. He meant to fish and gather along the beach, refill at the streams coming out of the jungle, and leave the dry stores sealed whenever the land fed him.

The sword on his belt. The stick in his hand.

He left the three iron tubes where they were. On the last morning he got one out and sat with it on the step in the sun the way he had a hundred times, worked the lever, watched the claw come down, and still didn’t have the faintest idea. It was too heavy to carry a thing he couldn’t use.

He put it back in the case exactly the way he’d found it.

He went along the row of graves and stood at the end of it a while, the boards still blank. He told them he was sorry again and that he’d been glad of the company, which had been true for years.

Then he shut the cabin up properly. Bedding folded. Charts back in the rack. The chest closed. He wedged the door so the weather would stay out of it.

He did all of it carefully because he was coming back. He meant to walk out of here and come back up this beach with two thousand people behind him.

He stood on the sand and looked at the ship a minute. Then he turned south and started walking.

The walk was the easiest he had ever made. He kept waiting for it to turn on him, and it never did.

The seam lay about forty kilometers south. He made fourteen kilometers a day and could have made more. Firm wet sand at the tide line the whole way, flat, with no thorns or water to wade. The sea stayed on his right. He had morning sun, shade at the tree line when he wanted it, and wind off the water in the hottest part of the day.

He fished in the evening and usually had something inside the hour. He set snares at the tree line at dusk and collected them at dawn, and about half the time there was meat in them. When he saw a stand he recognized, he went into the green and came down with an armful. What he didn’t eat, he smoked over the fire and wrapped for later. He knew thirty useful plants by sight now and could pick them out at forty paces.

He camped on the beach. Tent up, fire going, fish on it, the water doing what it always did. He’d sit out with a cup of the spirit and watch the light go, then sleep like a dead man on soft sand under canvas.

The bugs left him alone out on the open beach with the wind moving.

It was a lovely trip. He knew how stupid that would sound if he said it at home.

He caught himself enjoying it on about the third day and had to sit down.

On the last journey he’d eaten a fistful of dried strips in twelve days, drunk from a swamp, and lost his mind in the dark. He’d woken with an animal tearing at his leg. Now he was strolling down a white beach with a full pack and a cup in his hand.

The difference was that he knew where to camp and what to eat.

Late on the third day, he saw it.

He’d checked with the glass every morning and evening. The edge had sharpened each time, and now he didn’t need the glass at all.

The green ended.

Not thinned. Ended. The tall trees stepped down over the last few kilometers to low scrub, then stopped at a hard line. Past it the ground went pale and dry and stayed that way as far as he could see.

And the coast kept running south without it.

He stood at the edge with his hand on his stick, then sat on the boundary and didn’t move for a long time. He put one hand back into the shade under the last tree and laid the other flat on the hot pale dirt. He sat like that, one hand in each country, checking that both were real.

Eight years. Three or four clear mornings a year, up on a hull with a brass tube, watching a smudge.

He’d been right.

He said it out loud a few times, in a few different ways, and none of it was dignified, and there was nobody there to see it.

He camped at the seam that night with the jungle at his back and the dead ground in front of him. He slept badly, too pleased to settle.

In the morning he took out the needle, turned himself east, and started walking.

The seam ran the way he had drawn it.

Not neatly. The edge of the green bulged south where water came out and pulled north where it didn’t. Some days he walked a clean line with the wall on his left shoulder. On others, a tongue of trees drove him ten kilometers into the pale country before he could come back to his bearing. In places the boundary blurred into scrub and he couldn’t have said which country he was in.

He took a bearing every morning and held it. When the edge pushed him off, he went around and came back on without having to guess.

He followed the jungle east for thirteen days.

A few hundred yards off his left shoulder most days stood the same wall he’d walked into nine years ago. The same hundred floors of green. The same wet black smell coming off it in the morning. At night he heard the whole enormous body of it howling and clicking in the dark, going about its business.

It couldn’t reach him on the dry ground. He walked past with a pack on and his boots dry, close enough to smell the swamp inside but never putting a foot in it. Some evenings he talked to the jungle. Never kindly.

The open country was scrub and rock under hard light, cold enough at night that he was glad of the coat. He rationed water, though the jungle leaked often enough that he never came close to trouble. Every few days a stream ran out of the green and died twenty yards into the sand. He drank, filled all three skins, and looked for food along the wet bank. Where nothing useful grew, he ate into the dry stores.

On the thirteenth day the ground started going up. Two days later he was looking at mountains.

A jagged gray range ran away north and south. Where it met the jungle, the green climbed into the rock and the rock came down through the trees. From a distance he couldn’t say where either stopped, and he stood there a good while because he thought he knew what he was looking at.

There was no way through it that he could see. He went about five kilometers north along the base of it looking for one, then stopped and had a word with himself.

He had more than three weeks of food, full skins, and one and a half good legs. He had no reason to hurry.

So he turned around and went south and walked around the whole thing.

It took him nine days. The ground stayed flat and pale the whole way, with the range on his left. At night the sky was clear and cold, and he could check his east and west against the same stars he’d watched from the ship.

He came around the southern end of it and turned east, and after a while the ground started looking at him funny.

He knew this dirt.

He couldn’t have said how. Something in the color of the dirt and the way the scrub grew was familiar. He walked half a day arguing with himself about whether a man could recognize dust.

Then he came over a rise and saw the track.

A road. A real one, with old wheel ruts and hoof marks running east and west.

He got down on one knee in it and put his hand in the ruts.

He was home. Not at home. In it. This was his country, the dust he was born on, and he had walked here from the other side of the world by going around the jungle.

A day and a half later he came up on Dry Fork.

He’d been thinking about it the whole way in: the officer, six soldiers, three tents, and the hyena on a spear that hadn’t been there the day before.

He came over the last rise ready to be stopped and questioned. He was an old man with gray in his beard and a foreign sword on his hip.

There was nobody there.

The three tents still stood where they always had, bleached colorless and sagging on slack ropes. One had split down a seam and was flapping.

No horses or fire. No spear. No hyena.

He walked down slowly, hand near the sword out of habit, and went through the tents one at a time.

All three were empty. Somebody had taken everything worth carrying and left the rest. No pots, blankets, lamps, chests, tack, or tools. What remained was a bootlace, a broken peg, the heel of a boot, and old clean bones that animals had scattered.

He found no scorch marks, blood, or broken weapons. They had packed what was worth the weight, walked away, and left the tents standing.

The main tent stopped him. He had sat in this exact shade with Sheo and the officer, carpets and cushions cluttering the floor and an iron pot of tea on the coals. He could put the officer across from him with the documents spread over his knees and Sheo at Dren’s shoulder, still angry about the hyena.

The carpets were gone. So were the cushions, the pot, and the documents.

Only the ash ring remained, crusted over in the dirt. Dren stood above it and put every one of them back where they had sat, then looked at the spaces until they were only dirt again.

Now it was a dirt floor with garbage on it.

He stood in the middle of it and turned all the way around.

“Weird,” he said.

Nobody had been here in a long time. Not a season. Years, by the weathering on the cloth and ropes.

He stood a minute longer, trying ordinary reasons on it. Maybe they’d moved the checkpoint or closed the road. Maybe they’d run out of money and gone home.

He got the glass out before leaving and had a proper look around. It had never once occurred to him to point the thing at his own country.

Southwest, the Glare came up in the tube white and shimmering, nine or ten kilometers off, exactly as it had his whole life. He held on the old tavern as long as the heat allowed. He and Sheo had sat on its porch with the whiskey between them, then walked north to Dry Fork and carried their annual report back to Sotha Bal.

East, the road ran between the same two low ridges it always had.

He couldn’t see Sotha Bal. It was too far and the country too flat. He could see the shape of the ground it sat in, though, and knew every line of it.

Then he shouldered the ruck, took a bearing he didn’t need, and started up the road.

He used the glass most of the way in, though he didn’t need to. A hawk on a rock a kilometer out. A dust devil crossing the flat. The far end of the road. After eight years it was still the best thing he’d ever owned.

He slept at the old campsite that night.

He hadn’t planned to stop there. The ground had started looking familiar in the same way the dust had, and then he came around a low shoulder of rock and saw the old campsite.

The stones were still in a ring. Somebody had used it since, more than once, but the ring was theirs. He and Sheo had built it nine years ago, walking home from Dry Fork with the annual report done and the speared hyena still sitting in both their minds.

He put his fire in it and sat with his back against the same rock.

Sheo had talked most of that night, first about his cousin’s boat scheme and then about a girl in Drenovar. He kept circling back to whether the officer at Dry Fork had lied to them. Dren remembered being tired and letting him go on. Now he couldn’t remember one thing he himself had said, and he would have given a great deal to.

He sat there a long time with the fire going down.

He was a day from home. He’d been rehearsing for a season, and he ran through the speech again, including the order in which he’d open the bag.

He slept well.

A little over a month after leaving the ship, he glassed Sotha Bal from the rise. It was habit now, the same slow sweep he’d made over the coast every clear morning for eight years.

Then he took the tube down and rubbed his eye and put it back up.

There were towers. Not stone: four scaffolds that he could see, lashed poles rising thirty feet with platforms and men on top. A wall ran between them across ground that had been open scrub the last time he stood here.

If you could call it that. Mud and brick and sand and sticks and whatever else had been to hand, thrown up in sections, some of it chest high and some of it twice a man, wandering along the approach in a line that had obviously been argued about.

Behind the wall, the town had swollen. It had doubled. A whole second Sotha Bal covered the scrub beyond the old edge, built from canvas, scrap wood, and mud brick. The shelters were packed tight, and cooking smoke rose from them in a hundred thin lines.

He stood on that rise a long while with the glass up.

Then he walked down.

Nobody stopped him.

He came in braced to be challenged by some boy with a spear, ready to explain the sword and the foreign clothes. Instead he walked through a gap in the wall and past two men shoring up a weak section, and neither looked at him twice.

To them he was only an old man with a stick.

The new part of town was worse than anything he’d seen, and he’d grown up poor in a bad place. Shelters leaned off each other three deep, with gaps instead of lanes and roofs patched from whatever had been carried this far. Children sat still in the shade, thin arms across their knees, watching him without turning their heads. Water stood where no water should have stood and smelled of sewage. Old women cooked over fires the size of a hand.

He heard the Ghor Vhuul accent before he had gone a hundred paces, and then he heard it everywhere, and he understood.

Ghor Vhuul had never been rebuilt. Its survivors had gone first to West Outpost; when it couldn’t hold them all, families had kept moving. They’d fetched up in Sotha Bal with whatever they could carry, and now their accent filled these lanes.

The town was working. Men split poles and burned the points hard. Women passed baskets of dirt hand to hand up onto the wall. In a yard, somebody hammered scrap into rough blade shapes, fast and badly. A cart rolled through with bundled spears and two boys sitting on top to keep them from shifting.

Sotha Bal was building weapons in the street.

He kept walking and looking for a face without finding one. Then he saw three or four people he’d known. He looked straight at them and they looked straight back, but none showed a sign of knowing him. He was fifty-something now, with a white beard, foreign clothes, and a limp. To them he had been dead for nine years.

He didn’t stop them. He wasn’t sure what he would have said.

He found Sheo in the open ground behind the wall, in the middle of about forty people. Questions came at him from both sides while he pointed out work and sent people toward the gate.

He had gone heavy through the chest and gray at the temples. His shave was bad and his voice had been worn raw by shouting. He was pointing at a section of wall and arguing with a man half his age about whether it would hold, answering each objection before the man finished it. He looked like someone who’d been in charge too long and hadn’t slept enough.

Dren stopped about fifteen feet away.

He’d had nine years and a season to plan this, and he did none of it. He stood there and waited for a gap in the shouting.

The man Sheo was arguing with left. Sheo turned to find the next thing. His eyes passed over Dren, moved on, then came back and stopped.

His face went blank. His mouth opened, but nothing came out.

He took one step and stopped, as if he’d put his foot on bad ground. He looked Dren over again from the foreign boots to the white beard, then started once more and stopped again.

“Sheo,” Dren said.

At his name, Sheo crossed the dirt fast, grabbed him by both arms, and held on while he searched Dren’s face from six inches away. Then he made a noise that wasn’t a word and pulled him in.

Sheo was shaking. Dren could feel it through both their coats, the tremor running down Sheo’s arms and into his hands.

“You’re dead,” Sheo said into his shoulder. “You’re dead. We put a board up. I put it up. I carved the bloody thing, Dren.”

“I know.”

“Where have you…” He pushed Dren back to arm’s length and looked again at the beard, the burned hide of his face, the stick, as if one of them ought to answer for him. “Where have you been?”

It was the question he’d spent a season preparing for. The chart, brass tube, hourglass, and twenty-four gold coins were all in the bag on his back, arranged in the order he meant to bring them out.

“There’s a sea,” Dren said.

Sheo blinked at him.

“West. Past Gor Luut, past all of it. There’s an ocean out there, Sheo, and a coast where the ground grows food on its own. I’ve lived there eight years. There’s a way to it on dry ground. Anybody could walk it. Children could walk it. I’ve got the road drawn.” He was already pulling the bag around. “It’s here. I can show you.”

Somebody shouted Sheo’s name from up on the wall.

Sheo snapped toward the wall and shouted something Dren didn’t catch. When he turned back, his face had already changed. Half his attention had gone with the call, back to the wall and the people waiting on him.

Sheo gripped his arm. “Dren, I can’t. Not now. Not right now.”

“It’s twenty minutes.”

“There’s a column coming up from the north and it’ll be here inside two days.” He said it flatly, like weather. “Veyloth. Second time this year. Last time we lost the east side and forty people. I’ve got a wall that isn’t finished and men on it who have never held anything.”

“Sheo.”

“I know. I know.” He made himself look directly at Dren again, and for a moment the work left his face. He looked wretched. “You come back from the dead and I can’t give you twenty minutes. I’m sorry.”

Dren’s hand was still in the bag, but he didn’t take anything out.

The foreign sword had been six inches from Sheo’s hand the whole time. Its pommel held a garnet and its guard showed a month’s work, and it was worth more than every blade in town together. Sheo’s eyes had passed over it without stopping. He hadn’t noticed the coat, boots, leather case, or brass tube either.

Sheo was watching the unfinished wall, not Dren’s gear.

“Tonight,” Sheo said, moving already, walking backwards. “After dark. Find me.” Somebody shouted for him again and he half turned toward it. “Dren. I’m sorry. Tonight.”

“Sheo.” Dren got it out fast, before he went. “Your family. Are they all right?”

“Alive.” Sheo was already turning. “She’s at the house. Caius and Fimmion are at Trovgar.”

“Trovgar.”

“Veyloth put a camp an hour north of Trovgar. Vey Trading Camp, they’re calling it, like it’s a place.” He sounded sick of the name. “They control the market from there. Commandeered our stalls and put their own men in them. We’ve been fighting over it since spring. Both of them are down at Trovgar now.”

And then he was gone across the yard.

Dren stood where he was.

The last time he’d seen those two, they’d been small enough to be a nuisance. He had spent whole evenings on a beach on the other side of the world placing a house for them on the good ground behind the sand, working out which room went where. He’d decided they would be up the tall trees inside a day and he’d never get them down again.

They were grown and, by Sheo’s report, ninety kilometers east defending a market stall.

He went up onto the wall.

Nobody stopped him. Men were passing baskets of dirt along the wall and had no attention left for one more old man in the way.

He got the glass out and put it north.

Heat first, the whole flat swimming with it. He steadied his elbows on the top course and moved the glass slowly along the horizon, the way he’d searched the sea every evening for eight years. He checked the road, lost it in the folds of the ground, found it again, and followed it to where it came down from the ridge. There he found dust.

A long low smear stood above the track and drifted east on the wind. Not a wagon; wagons made a thin thread that broke apart behind them. This was a wall of dust, a kilometer long, the kind raised when a great many feet came down in the same place one after another.

Under it, shapes.

He held on them until they came apart into pieces. Four abreast. Spear tips catching in a line, then losing it, then catching again as they came around the bend. Horses at the head of it. Two carts back in the tail.

He counted them in tens because that was how he’d learned to count anything at a distance. He lost the count twice when the road dipped. Both times he started over and came out near the same number.

Three hundred. Near enough.

They were close. He had spent years learning what forty kilometers looked like through this tube and what four hundred yards looked like, watching the same pieces of coast until distance became practical. These men were on the ridge road, this side of the bend. Ten kilometers out and walking.

Two days, Sheo had said.

They would be at the wall before dark.

He took the glass down and looked at the yard below him. Forty people were running in every direction, and not one looked up at the man standing on their wall with a brass tube in his hand. He had carried it through the middle of town, climbed past the dirt crews, and watched an army for ten minutes. Nobody had asked what the tube was.

He went back down.

“Sheo.”

“Dren, I told you.”

“They’re ten kilometers out.”

Sheo stopped.

“North road, this side of the bend. Three hundred of them, four abreast, horses at the front and two carts behind. They’ll be on this wall before dark.”

“Says who?”

“Says me. I watched them for ten minutes.”

“From where?”

“From your wall.”

Sheo looked at him a moment. “You can’t see the bend from my wall.”

“I can.”

Sheo glanced toward the wall, then back at Dren. The runner who’d come in yesterday had said two days. Sheo had known that boy since birth and knew how fast he could cover the north road. Dren had walked in from nowhere that morning, dead for nine years and dressed like a foreigner, and now claimed he could see ten kilometers through a brass tube. Sheo looked at the glass in Dren’s hand before he answered.

“Then don’t stand here and wait for them,” Dren said. The words came too fast. “You don’t have to hold this. There’s nothing here worth holding. West, Sheo. Past Gor Luut, around the green. I walked every step on dry ground, and I marked the water. There are streams all along the trees, then a river that never quits and food hanging everywhere. The coast is empty. You could put this whole town there and still have room. I never saw another person there in eight years. Pull them out. Tonight, before that column gets here. Just go.”

He heard it once the words were out: tonight, before dark. There weren’t enough hours.

Sheo stared at him.

“Stop.”

“Sheo.”

“Stop.” He put a hand up and kept his voice low. “I’m glad you’re alive. I want you to hear me say it, because I mean it, and because of what I’m about to say next.”

He waited until Dren shut his mouth.

“You’ve been gone nine years. You walk in here on the worst week of my life and you start telling me about a land over the hills where the food grows itself and nobody’s in it.” He let that sit. “Listen to it. Just listen to what it sounds like from where I’m standing.”

“It’s not like that.”

“I’ve got a wall that isn’t finished. I’ve got two children in a market ninety kilometers east that I can’t get word to. I’ve got old men going onto that wall tonight because there isn’t anybody else.” His voice cracked. “And you want me to tell them to pack up and walk into the desert on your word. Nobody has time for this, Dren. I don’t have time for this.”

Somebody shouted his name from the gate.

“I know you’ve been gone a long time,” Sheo said, quieter. “I don’t know what happened to you. But not today.”

And he went.

Dren turned to the people in the street.

He tried the woman filling jars at the trough first because she was nearest. Soldiers on the north road, he said, a lot of them, and they weren’t two days out. There was a place west with water and a road to reach it. She heard him out with her hands still in the trough and told him to get out of the sun.

He tried two men lashing a cart across the mouth of a lane. One of them asked which way west, and Dren said past the mountains, past the green, and the man went back to the rope.

He tried four women by a fire and a boy stacking bundled reeds. He got further with them than anyone, far enough to take the case off his back and unroll half the chart across his arms. One woman actually looked at it. She followed the coastline with a wet finger, stopped at the marks in a hand nobody there could read, and asked what it was supposed to be.

“It’s a map,” he said.

“Of what?”

He tried a man carrying spear staves. The man shifted the bundle to his other shoulder and kept walking while Dren talked. He tried another man on a ladder and an old woman in a doorway, who was the only one unkind about it.

Nobody stayed with him long enough to argue. A few asked a question, then went back to the rope, the fire, or the wall before he finished answering. Most didn’t look at the chart. They saw a white-bearded man with a stick and a limp who had come from nowhere and was talking about an ocean while soldiers marched down the north road. The wall was in front of them. Dren’s coast was lines on foreign paper under his arm.

He stopped in the middle of the street with the case under his arm while the town moved around him.

Nine years ago, he’d leaned on a sticky counter in Veyloth and told a clerk in a purple coat that the jungle might hold supplies. The clerk had argued that people had tried and never come back, that an expedition would cost more lives than it saved. Then he’d called for the next person in line.

Here nobody even had time to send him away. Their hands went back to jars and ropes and bundled reeds.

Dren stood in the middle of it with a bag on his back that nobody had asked to see.
